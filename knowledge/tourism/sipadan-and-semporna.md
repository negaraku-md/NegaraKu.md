A single coral-covered pinnacle rising six hundred metres straight off the floor of the Celebes Sea, swirling with turtles and tornadoes of barracuda — Sipadan is the reason serious divers put eastern Sabah on their list, and the reason an entire travel economy has grown up around the little town of Semporna. But it is not a place you can simply turn up and dive. Sipadan is a strictly protected marine park with a capped daily permit, no accommodation on the island itself, and a security-zone context on the surrounding coast that first-timers need to understand calmly and factually. This guide covers what makes the diving world-class, how the permit and the daily cap actually work, where you stay, how Sipadan differs from the nearby Tun Sakaran Marine Park, who the region suits, and when to go.

## Where is Sipadan, and what makes it special?

Sipadan sits in the **Celebes Sea off the east coast of Sabah**, reached from the mainland town of Semporna. It is **Malaysia's only oceanic island** — rather than sitting on the continental shelf like most of the country's islands, it rises roughly **600 metres (about 2,000 feet) straight from the seabed**, built on an extinct volcanic cone that living corals have grown over for thousands of years (Wikipedia). That geology is the whole story underwater: just metres from the beach, the reef ends and a sheer wall drops into the blue, which is what concentrates the marine life and gives Sipadan its reputation.

The island is tiny and uninhabited, and it is **fully protected**. Sabah Parks declared it a marine park in **2004**, and the hotel and resort facilities that once stood on the island were closed because of their impact on the ecosystem (Wikipedia). In **2015** the protected area was expanded, bringing the **Sipadan Island Park** to a total of **16,860 hectares** of marine conservation zone managed by Sabah Parks (Wikipedia). The practical consequence for a visitor is simple but important: **you cannot stay on Sipadan**. Everyone who dives it comes over for the day from somewhere else and goes back by boat.

Sipadan's fame is long-standing. The ocean explorer Jacques Cousteau filmed here and famously called it "an untouched piece of art" in his 1989 documentary (Wikipedia) — a line the dive industry has quoted ever since, and one worth keeping in perspective now that access is deliberately rationed to protect what he saw.

## What will you see underwater?

This is where Sipadan earns the superlatives. The wall and the shallows together hold an unusual density of large animals (Wikipedia):

- **Turtles everywhere.** Green and hawksbill turtles are seen on almost every dive; they mate and nest on the island, so encounters are close and frequent (Wikipedia).
- **The barracuda tornado.** Enormous schools of barracuda gather in slow, spiralling, tornado-like formations — the signature Sipadan image — alongside dense schools of bigeye trevally (Wikipedia).
- **Sharks and pelagics.** Reef sharks patrol the walls, and the site also draws **scalloped hammerheads and whale sharks** seasonally, plus manta and eagle rays passing in the current (Wikipedia).
- **Reef life at scale.** The surrounding ecosystem is credited with more than **400 fish species** and hundreds of coral species, including schooling bumphead parrotfish (Wikipedia).

The flip side of all that pelagic action is that Sipadan is an **advanced environment**: sheer drop-offs, open blue water and current mean it rewards experience and demands respect, not a casual first dip.

## How do the permits and the daily cap work?

Because Sipadan is a protected park, **entry is capped and controlled by Sabah Parks through a daily permit** (Wikipedia). You do not buy a Sipadan permit directly as a walk-in; permits are allocated to **licensed dive operators**, who apply on their guests' behalf, which is the single biggest reason you must book your trip well in advance.

The number of permits issued each day is deliberately limited — and it has **changed over time**, which is exactly the kind of figure not to trust from an old blog post. Sabah Parks raised the daily allocation to **176 permits a day from 2019** according to Wikipedia, up from a lower earlier limit, and dive-industry reporting through 2025–2026 has cited both proposed increases and higher operating figures (Wikipedia; Divernet). The number of **dives allowed per permit** has also been adjusted in recent years. Treat any single number you read — including the ones here — as provisional, and **confirm the current daily cap, the dive limit per permit, and all fees directly with Sabah Parks or your licensed operator** before you commit (Sabah Parks). The same applies to the park's **annual conservation closure**, commonly cited around November, which varies by year and should be checked against the official notice.

The planning takeaway is durable even if the numbers aren't: **places are scarce and spoken for months ahead**. Popular resorts ration their permits across guests and stay lengths, so a longer stay generally improves your odds of a Sipadan day, and peak season makes permits harder to secure.

## Where do you actually stay — Mabul, Kapalai, Mataking and Semporna?

Since no one stays on Sipadan, the region's accommodation is spread across the **gateway town of Semporna** and a handful of nearby islands, each with a slightly different character (Malaysia Airlines):

- **Mabul.** The best-known base, a short boat ride from Sipadan, famous in its own right for **muck diving** — the critter-hunting that turns up frogfish, blue-ringed octopus, mandarinfish and other small, strange creatures on its house reefs. Mabul has everything from backpacker dives to water-bungalow resorts.
- **Kapalai.** Not really an island but a stilted resort built over a sandbar between Sipadan and Mabul, known for shallow coral gardens with pygmy seahorses, lionfish and macro life — a quieter, over-water base.
- **Mataking.** Further out, a single-resort island ringed by house reefs that make for easy snorkelling and diving straight off the beach — a good choice if you want calm and seclusion.
- **Semporna town.** The mainland hub where boats depart. Many budget trips are based here, running out to the dive sites each morning; it is also the practical arrival point and the place to stock up.

You will also see **Pom Pom, Mantabuan, Sibuan and Singamata** offered as bases or day-trip stops. Which one suits you is largely about trade-offs between price, seclusion, boat-ride time to Sipadan, and whether you want macro muck diving (Mabul), over-water comfort (Kapalai), or beach-and-reef seclusion (Mataking).

## What is the Tun Sakaran Marine Park?

Not every reef in the region is Sipadan, and that is good news for snorkellers and families. The **Tun Sakaran Marine Park** — also called **Semporna Islands Park** — is a separate protected area **gazetted in 2004** as the seventh park under Sabah Parks, sitting closer to Semporna town than Sipadan does (Wikipedia). It is a cluster of islands and reefs: **Bodgaya, Boheydulang, Sebangkat and Selakan**, the sand cays of **Maiga, Sibuan and Mantabuan**, and patch reefs, with reported areas differing between sources (around 350 km² versus a smaller figure for the core zone) (Wikipedia).

The park is scenic and shallow — think turquoise sand cays, coral gardens and the dramatic crater rim of **Boheydulang** for hikers — which makes it the region's **snorkelling and island-day-trip** counterpart to Sipadan's deep-wall diving (Wikipedia). It is also home to a substantial community of **Bajau Laut** ("sea nomads"), who live in stilt houses and boats within the park (Wikipedia). Common day trips split the islands into routes such as Sibuan–Mantabuan–Maiga or the Bohey Dulang circuit.

## Is it for you — diver, snorkeller, or neither?

The honest answer depends on what you can do in the water:

- **Certified scuba divers** are the core audience. Sipadan's walls are the draw, and they need an open-water certification and ideally some logged dives, because the environment is deep and current-prone (Sabah Parks).
- **Snorkellers and families** should not plan a trip around diving Sipadan itself, but they are well served by the shallow house reefs of **Mabul and Kapalai** and the sand-cay reefs of the **Tun Sakaran Marine Park**, where the water is calm and the coral close to the surface (Malaysia Airlines).
- **Non-swimmers** will find the region less rewarding — this is fundamentally a marine destination.

If your group mixes certified divers with snorkellers, the usual solution is to base on Mabul or at a resort that runs both Sipadan dive boats and Semporna-islands snorkelling trips, so everyone has a full day.

## Is eastern Sabah safe? Handling the advisory context

This deserves a calm, factual treatment because it genuinely shapes trip planning. Eastern Sabah's coast and islands fall within a **designated security zone (ESSZONE)**, established and patrolled after past cross-border kidnapping incidents linked to armed groups from the southern Philippines; the last reported kidnap-for-ransom incident in these waters was in January 2020 (US Department of State; news reports). For years, foreign governments issued cautious advisories for the islands and surrounding waters.

The picture has been **improving and advisories have been easing**. Through 2025 and 2026, several governments downgraded their warnings as security conditions improved — US reporting in early 2026 described mainland eastern Sabah as lowered to its lowest advisory level while keeping a heightened level for the **islands and maritime areas**, and other governments relaxed island restrictions over the same period (US Department of State; news reports).

What this means in practice:

- **Advice differs between the mainland and the islands**, and it changes, so do not rely on an old number or a tour operator's reassurance alone.
- **Check your own government's current official advisory** — for example the US Department of State, the UK FCDO or Australia's Smartraveller — close to your travel date (US Department of State; UK FCDO; Smartraveller).
- **Dive operators coordinate with the security forces**; follow their guidance, travel with a licensed operator, and keep to the established dive areas.

The region remains one of the world's premier dive destinations with a thriving, well-run tourism sector — the point is to travel informed, not anxious.

## When should you go?

Diving is possible **year-round**, but the calmest seas and clearest water generally fall in the **drier stretch from around April through roughly October to December**, with the sharpest visibility often earlier in that window (dive-the-world; PADI). Different guides draw the boundaries slightly differently — some say March to October, others April to December — so treat the exact months as a guide rather than a rule, and expect the odd choppy or rainy day in any season.

Two planning points follow from the season. First, **permits are hardest to secure in the busiest months**, so if you want a peak-season slot, book even further ahead (Malaysia Travel). Second, factor in the park's **annual conservation closure** (commonly around November, but confirm the year's dates), during which Sipadan itself is off-limits while the Semporna-islands diving continues.

## How do you get there?

The standard route is to **fly to Tawau Airport (TWU)** in southeastern Sabah — reachable on domestic flights from Kota Kinabalu and Kuala Lumpur — then transfer by road to **Semporna** (roughly an hour and a half), where boats depart to the islands and dive sites. Check current flight routes and schedules with the airlines before booking, as these change. From Semporna, your resort or operator handles the **boat transfer** to Mabul, Kapalai, Mataking or your chosen base, and the daily dive boats out to Sipadan.

Because the whole trip runs through licensed operators and capped permits, the sensible pattern is to **book the package first** — flights, transfer, accommodation and dive days as one plan — rather than piecing it together on arrival.

## What do first-time visitors get wrong?

A few recurring mistakes are easy to avoid:

- **Expecting to stay on Sipadan.** You can't — no accommodation remains on the island, and you day-trip in with a permit (Wikipedia).
- **Booking too late for a Sipadan permit.** The daily cap is strict and permits go through operators months ahead; a last-minute plan often means no Sipadan day at all (Sabah Parks).
- **Trusting old permit and fee numbers.** The cap, the dives-per-permit and the fees have all changed — verify the current figures with Sabah Parks or your operator (Wikipedia; Sabah Parks).
- **Underestimating the certification requirement.** Sipadan's walls are for certified, reasonably experienced divers; snorkellers should plan around the shallow Semporna reefs instead (Sabah Parks).
- **Ignoring the advisory homework.** Read your own government's current advice for the eastern Sabah islands before you go, rather than assuming either the best or the worst (US Department of State).
- **Confusing Sipadan with Tun Sakaran.** They are different parks — Sipadan for deep-wall diving, the Tun Sakaran Marine Park for shallow snorkelling and island day trips (Wikipedia).
- **Visiting during the conservation closure.** Check whether Sipadan's annual closure overlaps your dates before you lock them in (Sabah Parks).

## What's next

If Sipadan is the goal, the order of operations is unusual: **secure the trip before you finalise the dates**, because the permit — not the flight or the hotel — is the scarce resource. Pick a base that matches your group (Mabul for muck diving and variety, Kapalai for over-water calm, Mataking for seclusion, Semporna town for budget), confirm the **current permit cap, fees and any closure** with Sabah Parks or your licensed operator, and read your own government's **current advisory** for the eastern Sabah islands close to departure.
