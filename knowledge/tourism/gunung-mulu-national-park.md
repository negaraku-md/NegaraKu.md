Deep inside Sarawak's rainforest, where limestone mountains rise straight out of the jungle and no road reaches, sits one of the most extraordinary landscapes in Malaysia — and one of the most studied cave regions on the planet. Gunung Mulu National Park is where you find the world's largest cave chamber, a cave passage big enough to fly a jumbo jet through, a nightly river of millions of bats, and a hilltop forest of limestone blades sharp enough to shred a boot. It is also genuinely remote: you arrive on a small plane, you go almost everywhere with a guide, and you book months ahead. This guide covers what Mulu is, what there is to see underground and above it, the two big treks, how access works, and the mistakes first-timers make.

## Where is Gunung Mulu National Park, and why is it a UNESCO World Heritage Site?

Gunung Mulu National Park lies in the Miri Division of Sarawak, in Malaysian Borneo, close to the border with Brunei and Sabah. It covers **528.64 sq km** (52,864 hectares) and rises from lowland rainforest at around 50 m near park headquarters to the **2,376 m** sandstone summit of **Gunung Mulu**, the mountain that gives the park its name (mulupark.com; Wikipedia). Two great limestone massifs, **Gunung Api** (1,750 m) and **Gunung Benarat** (1,858 m), hold the cave systems and the Pinnacles.

The park was inscribed as a **UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2000**, at the 24th session of the World Heritage Committee, under reference 1013 (UNESCO). What makes it rare is that it qualified under **all four of UNESCO's natural criteria** — outstanding natural beauty, an outstanding record of Earth's history, ongoing ecological and biological processes, and significant habitats for biodiversity (UNESCO). Few sites anywhere meet all four. Mulu does because it packs several whole landscapes into one protected area: a vast, superbly preserved limestone karst honeycombed with caves; mountains and gorges; and **seventeen vegetation zones** stacked from steamy lowland forest up to cool, mossy montane forest (Wikipedia). It is frequently described as the most studied tropical karst area in the world (UNESCO).

That biodiversity is staggering on paper. The park records roughly **3,500 vascular plant species**, including some **109 species of palm** — one of the richest palm floras anywhere — alongside around 20,000 invertebrate species and dozens of mammals, birds, reptiles, amphibians and fish (Wikipedia). But for most visitors the headline act is underground.

## What are the caves — and what makes them record-breaking?

Mulu's caves are the reason the world knows the park. Decades of expeditions have surveyed hundreds of kilometres of passages, and the superlatives are real:

- **Deer Cave** is long described as the **world's largest cave passage**, a cathedral-scale tunnel more than a hundred metres across, draped in forest at its entrances and home to millions of bats (Wikipedia). It is one of the two main show caves and needs no caving experience to visit.
- **Lang Cave**, a short walk from Deer Cave, is small by comparison but famous for delicate stalactites, stalagmites and flowstone — the pretty cave to Deer Cave's giant.
- The **Clearwater Cave system** is the **longest cave in Southeast Asia**, with well over 200 km of surveyed passages and still growing with each expedition (Wikipedia). Visitors reach it by longboat up the Melinau river and can swim in the cold, clear pool at its mouth.
- **Wind Cave**, near Clearwater, is named for the breeze that funnels through it and is known for its Garden of Eden-like chamber of formations.
- The **Sarawak Chamber**, hidden in the Gunung Api limestone, is **among the largest known cave chambers on Earth** — on the order of **600 m long, 415 m wide and at least 80 m high** (Wikipedia). It is not a casual visit: reaching it is a demanding guided adventure-caving trip.

The four **show caves** — Deer and Lang as one pairing, Clearwater and Wind as the other — are the accessible core, visited on guided walks and boat trips from park headquarters. Beyond them, Mulu offers a graded menu of **adventure caving** for fitter, more experienced visitors, from half-day scrambles to the overnight expedition into the Sarawak Chamber; these require booking, briefing and licensed guides (mulupark.com). A note on the numbers: cave lengths and chamber records at Mulu are revised as exploration continues, and some long-held superlatives have been challenged by discoveries elsewhere, so treat the records as leading estimates rather than fixed facts.

## What is the bat exodus at Deer Cave?

The single most-watched event in the park happens at dusk. **Deer Cave shelters millions of wrinkle-lipped free-tailed bats**, and on most dry evenings they stream out of the cave mouth to hunt insects over the forest — swirling into long, pulsing ribbons that twist across the sky to confuse predatory hawks and bat-eating raptors (Wikipedia). This is the **bat exodus**, and it is free to watch from the open-air **bat observatory** near the cave entrance in the late afternoon (mulupark.com).

Two honest caveats. First, it is weather-dependent — in heavy rain the bats often stay put, so there is no guarantee on any given evening. Second, the population figures you will see quoted vary a great deal between sources, from a couple of million upward; take any single number as an estimate rather than a precise count. Either way, when the exodus runs, it is one of the great wildlife shows in Asia.

## What are the Pinnacles, and what does the trek involve?

High on the flank of **Gunung Api** stands a forest of grey limestone blades — the **Pinnacles** — razor-sharp spires up to around 45 m tall, carved out of the mountain by millions of years of tropical rain dissolving the rock along its fractures (Wikipedia). Seeing them up close is one of the hardest day-walks in Malaysian tourism, and it is firmly a guided, pre-booked undertaking.

The shape of the trip is this: from park headquarters you travel by longboat up the Melinau river and then walk to **Camp 5**, a riverside base beneath the Melinau Gorge; the Pinnacles climb itself is a separate, very steep ascent the next day, hauling up fixed ropes and ladders against strict **cut-off times** — miss a checkpoint and guides will turn you back for safety (mulupark.com; Sarawak Tourism Board). It is typically run over **three days and two nights**, demands real fitness, is **not suitable for young children**, and is **cancelled in wet conditions** when the wet rock becomes too dangerous to climb. Places, guides and camp space are limited, so this is one to **book well ahead** and to confirm current fitness rules, group minimums and fees directly with the park, since those details change.

## What is the Headhunters' Trail?

The **Headhunters' Trail** is Mulu's classic point-to-point jungle journey, a route that links the park with the town of **Limbang** to the north. It takes its name from the historical overland path that raiding war parties are said to have used, dragging their longboats across the watershed to strike at communities downriver (Sarawak Tourism Board).

As a modern trek it is usually walked one way: from **Camp 5** you hike through the forest to **Kuala Terikan**, then continue by **longboat** down the river, commonly breaking the trip with an **overnight stay at an Iban longhouse**, before reaching a road that leads out to Limbang (Sarawak Tourism Board). It is normally arranged as a guided multi-day package combining foot and boat travel, and because it ends in a different place from where it starts, it pairs naturally with a Pinnacles climb or a show-caves stay rather than being a simple there-and-back. It can also be walked in reverse, starting near Limbang and finishing at the park. As with everything at Mulu, logistics (guides, boats, longhouse nights, permits) are booked in advance.

## Who are the Penan and Berawan people here?

Mulu is not empty wilderness. The park and the rivers around it are the homeland of several Indigenous communities, among them the **Penan** — historically nomadic hunter-gatherers of the Borneo interior — and the **Berawan**, alongside Kayan, Kenyah and other Orang Ulu groups (Wikipedia). People have used these caves for a very long time: human remains and artefacts recovered from the Cave of the Winds area have been dated across a range of roughly 500 to 3,000 years old (Wikipedia).

For visitors, the respectful posture is simple. The guides, boat operators and longhouse hosts you meet are part of living communities, not a backdrop; buying local crafts, hiring local guides and following your hosts' lead at a longhouse keeps tourism income in the area and treats the culture as present-tense rather than a museum piece. If you photograph people, ask first.

## When should you go?

Mulu sits in equatorial rainforest, so it is warm, humid and can rain in any month — this is one of the wetter corners of Malaysia, and you should expect downpours whatever the season. There is no neat dry season to aim for the way there is on the beaches. The sensible planning logic is therefore about **activity risk, not guaranteed sunshine**: the Pinnacles climb and some caving are called off when heavy rain makes the rock or rivers unsafe, and the bat exodus fizzles on wet evenings, so a run of drier weather improves your odds on the weather-sensitive highlights.

In practice that means building in **buffer days**. If the Pinnacles or a particular adventure cave is the centre of your trip, give yourself more than one shot at it rather than a single tight window, and keep the show caves and gentler walks — which run rain or shine — as reliable fallbacks. Avoid timing a visit to the very wettest local stretch if you can, and confirm current conditions with the park when you book.

## How do you get there and around?

**By air — and essentially only by air.** There is **no road into Gunung Mulu National Park**. Visitors fly in on a small aircraft to **Mulu Airport**, most commonly on the short hop from **Miri** in Sarawak, with the airport also connecting to other points in the region (Wikipedia; mulupark.com). The flights are on small planes with tight luggage limits, so pack light and check the current baggage allowance. River travel from Marudi is possible but slow and rarely used by visitors.

**On arrival**, you register at **park headquarters**, which sits close to the airport and the main accommodation. From there, nearly everything runs on **guides and boats**: the Deer and Lang caves are reached on a plank walkway through the forest; Clearwater and Wind caves are a longboat trip up the river; Camp 5, the Pinnacles and the Headhunters' Trail all start with a boat ride. Because the park is compact around headquarters but the attractions are spread along trails and rivers, you move at the pace of the booked activity rather than wandering freely.

**Booking** is the thing to get right. Seats on the planes, rooms in and around the park, and places on guided caves and treks are all **limited and fill up**, especially in peak periods. Reserve flights, accommodation and your guided activities **together and well in advance**, and buy the kind of flexible travel insurance that copes with weather delays — small-plane schedules and jungle treks both bend to the weather. ## What do first-time visitors get wrong?

A handful of mistakes come up again and again:

- **Treating Mulu like a drop-in day trip.** It is a fly-in, book-ahead destination where the best experiences need guides and permits. Turning up hoping to climb the Pinnacles tomorrow does not work — reserve weeks or months out.
- **Underestimating the Pinnacles.** It looks like a day-walk on a map; it is a brutally steep, cut-off-timed climb that is cancelled in rain and unsuitable for young children. Train for it, and have a backup plan.
- **Expecting a dry season.** Mulu is one of Malaysia's rainier spots with no reliable dry window. Build in buffer days for weather-sensitive activities rather than betting everything on one afternoon.
- **Over-packing for the small plane.** Flights to Mulu Airport have strict luggage limits; bring soft bags and leave the heavy case in Miri.
- **Assuming the bat exodus is guaranteed.** It depends on the weather and the bats, not the schedule. Watch on more than one evening if you can.
- **Confusing the show caves with adventure caving.** Deer, Lang, Clearwater and Wind are accessible guided walks; the Sarawak Chamber and similar trips are serious, fitness-gated caving. Pick the right tier for your experience.
- **Treating local communities as scenery.** The Penan, Berawan and other Orang Ulu people live here. Hire local, buy local, and ask before photographing.

## What's next

Mulu rewards planning more than almost any destination in Malaysia. Decide first which big-ticket experience anchors your trip — the Pinnacles, the Headhunters' Trail, an adventure cave, or simply the show caves and the bat exodus — because that choice drives how many days you need and how hard you have to train. Then lock the chain of bookings together: the flight to Mulu Airport, the rooms, and the guided activities, all well ahead, with buffer days for weather.

Because fees, flight schedules, trek rules and cave access change, verify the current specifics close to your travel date with the **official park website (mulupark.com)** and the **Sarawak Tourism Board**, and read the park's safety and fitness requirements before you commit to the Pinnacles or adventure caving.
