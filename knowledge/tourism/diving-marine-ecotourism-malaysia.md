The clearest water in Malaysia is also the hardest to reach: to drop into Sipadan's wall you need a government permit that no amount of money will let you buy directly. That single fact captures how the country runs its dive tourism — access is rationed, priced, and split between two very different authorities depending on which sea you want to descend into.

## Who decides where you can dive in Malaysia?

There is no single "national dive authority." Jurisdiction is divided by geography.

Marine parks in **Peninsular Malaysia and Labuan** are federal, managed by the **Department of Fisheries Malaysia (DOF)** under the **Fisheries Act 1985**. DOF gazettes the waters around 49 islands as marine parks, spread across five states and the federal territory of Labuan, with a protected zone running two nautical miles seaward from the lowest sea level (one nautical mile for some islands in Terengganu and Labuan).

**Sabah** is different. Its islands — including Sipadan and the reefs around Semporna — fall under the state agency **Sabah Parks**, which sets its own permits and rules. For a diver this distinction is not academic: the fee you pay, the permit you need, and who you must book through all depend on which side of the map you are on.

| Where you dive | Governing authority | Legal basis |
|---|---|---|
| Tioman, Redang, Perhentian, Payar, Tenggol | Department of Fisheries Malaysia | Fisheries Act 1985 |
| Labuan marine park | Department of Fisheries Malaysia | Fisheries Act 1985 |
| Sipadan, Semporna-area islands | Sabah Parks (state) | State parks law |

The DOF marine parks are concentrated on the peninsula's east and north: Johor holds 20 islands, Pahang 9 (the Tioman group), Kedah 4 (including Payar), Melaka 3, and Labuan 3, with Terengganu's Redang and Perhentian archipelagos making up the balance of the 49.

## What does it cost to get in the water?

Entering a DOF marine park is not free. Visitors pay a **conservation charge** set under the **Fees Act 1951** and the **Fees (Marine Parks Malaysia) Order 2017**. The money goes into the Marine Parks and Marine Reserves Trust Fund, earmarked for managing and maintaining the parks.

| Category | Malaysian (MyKad) | Foreigner |
|---|---|---|
| Adult | RM 5.00 | RM 30.00 |
| Child (6–12) & senior (60+) | RM 2.00 | RM 15.00 |
| Disabled person | Free | Free |

Operators and specialist visitors pay separate activity fees. Under the same order, a dive operator pays **RM 150 per month** to run scuba activities in a marine park. Camping costs RM 10 a night (up to four people), commercial filming RM 100 a day, a cruise entry RM 100, and a research permit RM 200 plus a RM 500 deposit. Island residents and licensed dive-shop staff can be exempted from the visitor charge.

Note that these are DOF figures for the peninsula and Labuan. Sabah sets its own park fees separately, on top of which Sipadan carries its permit system.

## Why can't you just book Sipadan and go?

Because Sabah Parks deliberately rations it. Sipadan sits inside a conservation zone with a **fixed daily permit quota** — reported at **178 divers per day** — and those permits are handed only to licensed operators in advance, never sold to individuals. The practical route is to book a package with a resort or dive operator that holds an allocation; the permit comes with the booking.

The controls tightened sharply after commercialisation strained the reef. As Sabah Parks director Maklarin Lakim explained when the rules were revised, the number of permits issued to tour operators had climbed from 12 to 24 within two years. The restrictions now in force include:

- **Advanced Open Water** certification as the minimum to dive Sipadan (introduced in 2022).
- **Two dives per day** per permit (reduced from three).
- A maximum of **50 divers per dive site** at any one time.
- **No repeat visits** to the same dive site on the same day.
- Diving windows limited to roughly **7:00am–4:30pm**.
- Each dive master accompanying up to **four divers**.

The exact quota figure has been revised over the years as Sabah Parks balances demand against reef health, so treat the day-count as a moving target and confirm with your operator when booking.

## What are the rules once you descend?

Whether you are on a federal or a state reef, marine park rules exist to keep the ecosystem intact. The standard visitor rules the Department of Fisheries applies inside its marine parks include:

- **Do not touch coral** or step or stand on it.
- **Do not collect** any part of a marine organism, alive or dead — that covers coral pieces and shells.
- **Do not interfere with, chase, harass, or capture** marine life.
- **Boats may not anchor on reefs** — only mooring buoys or designated anchoring areas may be used.
- Scuba diving and snorkelling are **prohibited in boat-passage lanes**.

The Department of Fisheries administers these parks under the Fisheries Act 1985 — the same framework under which it gazettes the waters and collects the conservation charge. Divers should always confirm the current site rules with the managing authority or their operator before entering the water.

## Where does Malaysia actually dive?

Malaysia's reputation rests on a handful of very different sites. **Sipadan** is the headline — an oceanic pinnacle rising from deep water, famous for turtles, barracuda tornadoes and reef sharks, and gated by the permit system above. Divers usually stay on neighbouring **Mabul** and **Kapalai**, muck and macro sites in their own right, and day-trip across on their permit days.

On the peninsula, **Tioman** (Pahang) and **Redang**, **Perhentian** and **Tenggol** (Terengganu) are the accessible face of Malaysian diving — DOF marine parks with fringing reefs, easy shore and boat dives, and the conservation charge applied at the mainland jetty. Further out, **Layang-Layang**, a remote atoll in the South China Sea, is the advanced-diver frontier known for schooling pelagics; access there is seasonal and dependent on the resort operating in a given year, so it is worth verifying current status before planning a trip.

## What's next

If Sipadan is your goal, start with the operator, not the permit — pick a licensed resort with a permit allocation and book months ahead, and make sure your certification is at least Advanced Open Water. For peninsula diving, budget the marine park conservation charge into your trip (RM30 per foreign adult, RM5 per Malaysian adult) and expect to pay it at the jetty counter. Before any trip, reconfirm current fees, quotas and site rules with the Department of Fisheries or Sabah Parks and your operator, since Malaysia periodically revises both the numbers and the boundaries as it manages reef health.
