Most visitors meet Johor at a border queue — in a car on the Causeway, on a bus at the Second Link, or on the short shuttle train into JB Sentral — and treat the city as a shopping-and-food detour from Singapore. That sells it short. Johor Bahru is the capital of Malaysia's southernmost state and the gateway where the peninsula meets the island republic across the water, with a walkable heritage core, one of Asia's biggest family theme-park clusters on its doorstep, and a whole state behind it: beaches on the east, mangroves at the southern tip, and mountains and rainforest inland. This guide covers how the crossings work, what to do in the city, the theme parks, the coast and the interior, who Johor suits, and the one mistake that catches almost everyone — getting the crossing timing wrong.

## Where is Johor Bahru, and what makes it the gateway?

Johor Bahru — JB for short — sits at the very southern tip of Peninsular Malaysia, directly across the Straits of Johor from Singapore. It is the capital of the state of Johor, and the 2020 census recorded roughly **858,000 residents** in the city proper, making it Malaysia's most populous state capital, with a far larger metropolitan population around it (Wikipedia, citing the Department of Statistics Malaysia). The city grew out of a Malay settlement called **Tanjung Puteri**; an 1855 treaty placed the territory under the Temenggong Daeng Ibrahim, and it was later developed and renamed Johor Bahru under Sultan Abu Bakar, often called the father of modern Johor (Wikipedia). It was granted city status on 1 January 1994 (Wikipedia).

What makes JB a gateway rather than just a provincial capital is its position. It is one half of a cross-border conurbation with Singapore — tens of thousands commute across the strait each day for work — and that gives the city an unusual energy: malls, clinics, salons and restaurants pitched partly at Singaporean visitors, alongside an ordinary state capital going about its business. For a first-time visitor the practical point is simple: you are almost always arriving from, or heading to, Singapore, so understanding the crossings matters more here than anywhere else in Malaysia.

## How do the Causeway and Second Link work?

Two road links join Johor to Singapore, and they behave very differently.

The **Johor–Singapore Causeway** is the original and the famous one: a combined road-and-rail embankment across the narrow strait, completed in **1923**, that lands almost in the centre of JB at the JB Sentral and City Square complex (Wikipedia). It was dramatically breached by retreating Allied forces in early 1942 and later repaired (Wikipedia). Today it is the shorter, more central and far busier of the two crossings, and the one that generates the queue stories.

The **Malaysia–Singapore Second Link** is a bridge further west, opened in **1998**, running between Tanjung Kupang on the Johor side and Tuas in Singapore (Wikipedia). It is a longer drive but usually flows more smoothly, which makes it the pressure-release valve when the Causeway is jammed, especially for cars.

Taken together, these are routinely described as **among the busiest land crossings in the world**. Singapore's Immigration and Checkpoints Authority has put combined daily traffic through its Woodlands and Tuas checkpoints on the order of **400,000 travellers, rising higher on school and public holidays** (ICA), and Malaysian authorities have described the pair as the world's busiest land border checkpoints (The Star). There is also a short **KTM Shuttle Tebrau** train between JB Sentral and Woodlands for foot passengers. Whichever way you cross, you clear immigration on both sides, so a valid passport is essential — see /tourism/malaysia-entry-visa-requirements for who needs a visa and what the entry rules are.

## What about the RTS Link rail line?

The crossing that everyone is waiting for is the **Johor Bahru–Singapore Rapid Transit System (RTS) Link** — a short cross-border metro line designed to take a large share of commuter traffic off the Causeway. It is a roughly **4 km line between Bukit Chagar in JB and Woodlands North in Singapore**, with co-located immigration at each end and no intermediate stops, so passengers clear both countries' checks at the terminus rather than in a vehicle queue (LandTransportGuru; Wikipedia).

As of this writing the line is **under construction and not yet open**. Civil works have been reported as largely complete, with the project's **completion targeted for late 2026 and passenger service planned for early 2027**; a February 2027 start was reported in late 2026 after the two governments agreed a buffer for testing and approvals (Bernama; The Vibes). Treat all of that as planned rather than settled — the opening date has moved more than once over the project's life, so confirm the current status against official Malaysian and Singaporean transport announcements before building a trip around it. When it does open, the existing KTM Shuttle Tebrau service is expected to be wound down within months (LandTransportGuru). The durable fact is that a dedicated cross-border rail link is coming; the exact date is not something to bank on.

## What is there to see in Johor Bahru itself?

JB's sights cluster in a compact, walkable heritage core near the waterfront, and you can see the headline ones in a half-day.

The **Johor Bahru Old Chinese Temple** on Jalan Trus is the city's spiritual anchor for its Chinese community. It dates to the **1870s–1880s**, built in an era when Sultan Abu Bakar encouraged the local Chinese to establish their own places of worship, and it is associated with the Ngee Heng community leader Tan Hiok Nee (Tourism Johor). Unusually, the temple is shared by **five Chinese dialect groups** — Teochew, Hokkien, Cantonese, Hakka and Hainanese — each with its own principal deity under one roof, a symbol of unity across the community (Wikipedia). Every Chinese New Year it stages the **Chingay**, a night-time Parade of Deities that is over 150 years old and draws large crowds through the streets of the old town (Wikipedia; The Star).

Up on the hill overlooking the strait is the **Sultan Abu Bakar State Mosque**, Johor's grand state mosque, built during Sultan Abu Bakar's reign in a distinctive blend of Victorian and Malay architecture — one of the most elegant colonial-era mosques in the country (Wikipedia). Nearby stands the **Istana Besar**, the former main royal palace completed in 1866, set in landscaped grounds; part of it has served as the Royal Abu Bakar Museum, though you should check current public-access arrangements before visiting, as sources differ on whether the museum is open (Wikipedia). Other civic landmarks — notably **Bangunan Sultan Ibrahim** atop Bukit Timbalan — give the skyline its historic silhouette.

Beyond the monuments, JB is a food-and-shopping city. It has a strong street-food and kopitiam culture, night markets, and large modern malls, and much of its everyday draw for visitors is simply eating well and shopping for value. It makes an easy one-to-two-day base, and it pairs naturally with Melaka further up the coast — see /tourism/melaka-heritage-visitor-guide — or with the capital, Kuala Lumpur, a few hours north via /tourism/kuala-lumpur-visitor-guide.

## Where are the theme parks?

Southern Johor has grown into one of Southeast Asia's densest clusters of family attractions, and the anchor is the **Legoland Malaysia Resort** in Iskandar Puteri, west of central JB. It opened in **2012 as the first Legoland in Asia** (Tourism Malaysia; Wikipedia). The resort combines a **theme park** — Lego-brick-themed rides, shows and a scale-model MINILAND of Asian landmarks — with a separate **water park**, a **SEA LIFE aquarium** and themed hotels, enough to fill a full day or an overnight stay with younger children (Wikipedia).

It is not the only park. The broader Iskandar area and the coast hold several other family draws, and the **Desaru Coast** to the east has its own large water park within a resort complex (described below). Because these attractions are spread across the Iskandar zone and out along the eastern coast, a **car or a booked transfer** makes visiting them far more practical than public transport. For first-time family visitors from Singapore, a common pattern is a day at Legoland plus a night in JB, then either home or onward to the beach.

## What does the Johor coast and countryside offer?

Step outside the JB–Iskandar zone and Johor opens into a varied state well worth a few extra days.

**Desaru Coast** is the headline beach destination: a stretch of the South China Sea coast about **90 minutes east of JB** that has been developed into an integrated resort precinct with long sandy beaches, international hotels, golf courses and a large adventure water park. It is the state's main sun-and-sand getaway and a popular weekend escape for Singaporean families.

On the opposite, southwestern side of the state lies a quieter, watery landscape. **Kukup** is a Chinese fishing village largely built on stilts over the water, famous for seafood and for boat trips out to **Pulau Kukup National Park**, a mangrove island of roughly 15 km² (Wikipedia). A little to the southeast, **Tanjung Piai National Park** protects mangroves and mudflats near the southern tip of the peninsula, with boardwalks out into the forest — the area is often cited as the southernmost point of continental mainland Asia, a claim worth noting rather than leaning on. Together Kukup and Tanjung Piai offer a very different Johor from the theme parks: slow, coastal and ecological.

Inland, the state rises into hills and rainforest. **Gunung Ledang**, also known as **Mount Ophir**, is Johor's highest peak at **1,276 m** and the centrepiece of Gunung Ledang National Park, established in 2005 (Wikipedia; Tourism Johor). It is a steep climb wrapped in Malay legend — the mountain of the mythical Puteri Gunung Ledang — and access is controlled: a **licensed guide is required and daily climber numbers are capped**, so book ahead and confirm current permit arrangements with the park authority rather than turning up (Tourism Johor). Further north, straddling the Johor–Pahang border, **Endau-Rompin** is one of Peninsular Malaysia's great lowland rainforests and its **second-largest national park after Taman Negara**, with ancient forest, waterfalls and rivers reached via permit-and-guide trips from gateway stations (Wikipedia). It is a genuine wilderness experience and is covered more fully for the peninsula as a whole in /tourism/taman-negara-ecotourism-guide.

## Who does Johor suit?

Johor is a broad-church destination, but a few profiles fit it especially well:

- **Day-trippers and weekenders from Singapore.** This is the core audience — people crossing for food, shopping, services and short breaks. JB rewards a planned day and punishes a careless one, mostly through crossing timing.
- **Families with children.** The Legoland resort, the Desaru water park and the general ease of the Iskandar zone make southern Johor one of the most family-friendly corners of Malaysia.
- **Road-trippers.** Johor is the start or end of the classic west-coast peninsular drive linking JB, Melaka and Kuala Lumpur; a hire car unlocks the beaches, the mangrove coast and the parks that public transport reaches poorly. See /tourism/getting-around-malaysia-for-tourists for how driving and intercity transport work.
- **Hikers and nature travellers.** Gunung Ledang and Endau-Rompin are serious outdoor objectives, and the Kukup–Tanjung Piai mangroves add a gentler ecological day.

It is less of a fit for travellers chasing postcard tropical-island snorkelling — for that, Malaysia's offshore islands elsewhere are the draw — though Desaru covers the mainstream beach-holiday brief well.

## How do you get there and around?

**From Singapore.** The Causeway (to central JB) and the Second Link (further west) are the two road options, served by buses, taxis and private cars; the KTM Shuttle Tebrau train links Woodlands to JB Sentral; and the RTS Link rail is planned for the late-2026 to early-2027 window once it opens. Whichever you choose, allow generously for immigration — queues can run long at peak times.

**From the rest of Malaysia.** JB is the southern terminus of the peninsula's road and rail spine. Long-distance buses and the KTM intercity/ETS rail network connect it to Melaka, Kuala Lumpur and beyond, and **Senai International Airport**, just north of the city, handles domestic and regional flights — a useful alternative to flying into Singapore. Confirm current routes and schedules with the carriers rather than relying on fixed timetables.

**Getting around the state.** Within JB, the heritage core is walkable and ride-hailing is widely available, but the theme parks, Desaru, Kukup and the parks are spread out and thinly served by public transport. A **rental car** is the single biggest convenience for anyone exploring beyond the city centre, and it is essential for the mountains and the mangrove coast.

## What do first-time visitors get wrong?

A handful of avoidable mistakes account for most ruined Johor days:

- **Mistiming the crossing.** This is the big one. The Causeway clogs on Friday evenings, weekends, Singapore school holidays and the eves of public holidays — queues of a couple of hours are not unusual. Cross early in the day, consider the Second Link when the Causeway is jammed, and never schedule a tight onward connection straight after a return crossing.
- **Underestimating how spread out Johor is.** JB, Legoland in Iskandar, Desaru on the east coast and Kukup in the southwest are in genuinely different parts of the state. Do not try to string them in a single day without a car and a realistic plan.
- **Assuming the RTS Link is already running.** It is still under construction; its opening date has moved before. Plan your crossing around the Causeway, the Second Link and the shuttle train, and treat the rail link as a bonus for a future trip.
- **Forgetting the passport basics.** Every crossing is an international border. Carry a valid passport, mind passport-validity rules, and check entry requirements in advance via /tourism/malaysia-entry-visa-requirements.
- **Showing up at capped or permitted sites unprepared.** Gunung Ledang requires a guide and limits daily numbers, and Endau-Rompin needs permits and guides — these are not walk-up hikes. Arrange them ahead.
- **Visiting the east coast in the wrong season.** The Desaru side is exposed to the northeast monsoon roughly November to March, when seas are rougher; the city and theme parks are unaffected, but time a beach trip accordingly.

## What's next

Start by deciding what kind of Johor trip you want — a value day from Singapore, a family theme-park weekend, a beach break at Desaru, or a nature run to Gunung Ledang and the mangrove coast — because each implies a different base and a different crossing strategy. Whatever you choose, plan the border timing first: it shapes everything. Then confirm the details that move — current RTS Link status, Senai and airline routes, park permits for Gunung Ledang and Endau-Rompin, and the opening arrangements at the Istana Besar museum — with the official sources close to travel. From JB it is an easy onward run up the west coast to /tourism/melaka-heritage-visitor-guide and /tourism/kuala-lumpur-visitor-guide, making Johor a natural start or finish to a peninsular journey rather than just a border town you pass through.
