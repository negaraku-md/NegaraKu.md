You can fly from Kuala Lumpur to Kuching in under two hours, never leave your own country, and still get stamped through immigration on the other end. That surprise at the arrivals hall is the single most important thing to understand about Sarawak: it is a Malaysian state that runs its own border.

Sarawak is the largest of Malaysia's states by area, occupying the northwest of Borneo. It is a place of ancient rainforest, the biggest cave chambers on Earth, and communities who still live communally in longhouses. This guide covers what you need to enter, how to base yourself in Kuching, how to reach the World Heritage caves of Gunung Mulu, and how to visit a longhouse without turning it into a spectacle.

## Why does Sarawak have its own immigration?

When the Federation of Malaysia was formed in 1963, Sarawak joined under terms that included retaining control over immigration into its own territory. That power remains in place today, which is why, more than sixty years later, Malaysia operates immigration checks between the Peninsula and Sarawak.

The mechanism is federal law. Section 66 of the Immigration Act 1959/63 is, in the Immigration Department's own words, "a restriction on the right of entry of citizens into the State of East Malaysia" — that is, into Sabah and Sarawak. In practice it means Sarawak decides who may enter and on what terms, and it enforces that at Kuching International Airport, Miri Airport, and the land crossings with Brunei and Indonesian Kalimantan.

Under these powers, Sarawak's immigration authorities can decline entry to individual travellers, separately from federal admission. For a visitor, the takeaway is simple: clearing immigration into Malaysia does not automatically clear you into Sarawak.

## Who needs what to enter Sarawak?

The rules split three ways — Sarawakians, other Malaysians, and foreigners — and each is treated differently at the door.

| Traveller | Document to carry | What happens on arrival |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sarawakian | MyKad | Cleared as belonging to the state |
| West Malaysian or Sabahan | MyKad (passport not required for a domestic flight) | Cleared through Sarawak immigration and issued a social visit pass |
| Foreign visitor | Passport (+ any Malaysian visa; MDAC submitted online) | Separate Sarawak entry stamp added on top of Malaysian entry |

A few points are worth spelling out.

- **Malaysians from other states are visitors here.** A West Malaysian or Sabahan is cleared through Sarawak immigration and granted a social visit pass on presenting a MyKad. It is quick and free, but it is a genuine immigration step, not a domestic-flight formality.
- **Visiting is not the same as working.** Entering Sarawak as a tourist is easy for any Malaysian. Taking up employment or study is not — that requires the appropriate Sarawak work or student pass, because the state controls immigration for those purposes too. Do not assume a Peninsular job or university place carries over automatically.
- **Foreigners get stamped twice.** Your Malaysian entry (at, say, KLIA) gets you into the country; the Sarawak stamp gets you into the state. Crucially, entering Sarawak from elsewhere in Malaysia does not usually hand you a fresh allowance — your permitted stay still counts from your first arrival into Malaysia. If Sarawak is your very first port of entry into Malaysia, you receive the full stay for your nationality.

## What do foreign visitors need before flying?

Most foreign nationals can enter Malaysia (and Sarawak) visa-free for tourism, with the length of stay depending on nationality. On top of any visa rule, most foreign arrivals must complete the **Malaysia Digital Arrival Card (MDAC)** — a free online pre-arrival declaration run by the Immigration Department of Malaysia. It is not a visa; it is a form you submit shortly before you travel, and certain groups are exempt (the current exemption list is published by the Immigration Department — confirm your category before you travel).

Two habits will save you grief:

1. **Keep your passport handy for the Sarawak leg**, even on a domestic-looking flight from the Peninsula — you will be stamped in again.
2. **Do not overstay by miscounting.** Because the Sarawak stamp does not reset your clock when you arrive from Peninsular Malaysia, plan your exit around your original Malaysian entry date.

If in doubt about your specific nationality, confirm current rules with the Immigration Department of Malaysia before booking.

## Where should you base yourself in Kuching?

Kuching, the state capital, is the natural hub for a first Sarawak trip. It is a river city, walkable and relaxed, with a restored waterfront, colonial-era shophouses, and an outsized reputation for food (Sarawak laksa and kolo mee are near-obligatory). Its nickname — the City of Cats — is taken literally enough that there is a museum devoted to them.

Kuching's real advantage is that world-class nature and culture sit within a day trip:

- **Semenggoh Wildlife Centre**, roughly 24 km south of the city, is an orangutan rehabilitation centre where semi-wild apes come to feeding platforms. Feedings run in the morning (around 8:00–10:00) and afternoon (around 14:00–16:00); sightings are never guaranteed because the animals are free to forage in the surrounding forest.
- **Sarawak Cultural Village**, near Santubong on the coast north of the city, is an award-winning "living museum" where the state's major communities each maintain a traditional dwelling — including an Iban longhouse — with craft demonstrations and dance.
- **Bako National Park**, reached by boat, offers proboscis monkeys and coastal rainforest for those with an extra day.

Use Kuching for three or four nights, then decide whether to add Mulu.

## What makes Gunung Mulu worth the flight?

Gunung Mulu National Park is Sarawak's headline wilderness and one of the reasons Borneo appears on serious travellers' lists. It was inscribed as a **UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2000** and, at around **55,000 hectares**, is the largest national park in Sarawak. Its terrain climbs from near sea level at park headquarters to the roughly **2,376-metre** summit of Gunung Mulu itself, over rainforest that took some 60 million years to form.

The superlatives are mostly underground. Mulu holds the world's largest cave passage (Deer Cave), the largest naturally enclosed space on the planet (the Sarawak Chamber), and one of the ten most voluminous cave systems in the world (Clearwater Cave). Above ground, the razor-sharp limestone Pinnacles are the park's signature landmark, reached by a demanding climb. And most evenings, weather permitting, some three million bats spiral out of Deer Cave in a ribbon that draws a crowd to the observation area — a free, world-class spectacle open to anyone in the park.

### How do you actually get to Mulu?

There is no realistic road. Access is by air, on MASwings (a Malaysia Airlines subsidiary):

| From | Approximate flight time |
| --- | --- |
| Miri | ~30 minutes |
| Kuching | ~1 hour 20 minutes |
| Kota Kinabalu | ~55 minutes |

From the airstrip, park headquarters is a short transfer away. Because the small aircraft depend on visibility, **flights are regularly delayed or cancelled by weather** — never schedule an onward international connection for the same day you fly out of Mulu.

### What does Mulu cost?

Park entry is a **five-day pass** giving unlimited entry over the period. The fees cover entry only; guided cave tours and adventure activities (including the Pinnacles climb and the show caves) are booked and paid separately.

| Visitor | Adult | Child (6–18) | Senior (60+) | Under 5 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Foreign | RM30 | RM10 | — | Free |
| Malaysian | RM15 | RM5 | RM7 | Free |

Passes are non-transferable. Most cave visits are done with the park's licensed guides, so booking activities in advance — especially in peak season — is wise.

## How do you visit a longhouse respectfully?

The longhouse is the enduring image of Dayak Sarawak: a single elevated structure, sometimes hundreds of metres long, where many families live in private rooms opening onto a shared communal gallery. Two very different ways to experience one are available, and it helps to know which you are choosing.

- **A living community**, such as the Bidayuh longhouse at **Annah Rais** in the Padawan highlands — roughly 60 km from Kuching city (about 65 km from the airport). Families still live here; visits are open daily but are best arranged in advance, ideally through a guide who can interpret customs and introduce you properly.
- **A living museum**, such as the **Sarawak Cultural Village**, where traditional houses of the Iban, Bidayuh, Orang Ulu, Melanau, Malay and Chinese communities are maintained for interpretation under one site.

Both are legitimate. A working longhouse offers authenticity but asks more of you as a guest; a cultural village offers breadth and convenience in a couple of hours.

A short etiquette checklist for a living longhouse:

1. **Arrange the visit ahead of time** — do not simply turn up and wander in; the gallery is public, but the rooms are people's homes.
2. **Ask before photographing** people, and especially before photographing anything ceremonial or ancestral.
3. **Accept hospitality graciously.** Food or a drink may be offered; declining outright can cause offence, so at least accept the gesture.
4. **Bring a modest contribution or buy local crafts** rather than treating the visit as free entertainment.
5. **Follow your host's lead** on where you may go and what you may touch.

## Which itinerary fits your time?

Use this as a decision framework rather than a fixed plan.

| You have | Sensible shape |
| --- | --- |
| 3 days | Kuching only: waterfront and food, Semenggoh orangutans, one longhouse or the Cultural Village |
| 5–6 days | Kuching (3 nights) + Mulu (2–3 nights) by air |
| 7+ days | Add Bako National Park, or extend Mulu for the Pinnacles / Headhunters Trail |

If you can only pick one add-on beyond Kuching and you are fit and flexible with dates, Mulu rewards the effort more than anything else in the state. If your dates are rigid or your budget is tight, keep to Kuching's day trips, which are excellent on their own.

## What mistakes catch visitors out?

- **Leaving your passport behind on the "domestic" leg.** Foreigners still clear Sarawak immigration flying in from the Peninsula.
- **Assuming your Malaysian visa stay resets in Sarawak.** Arriving from Peninsular Malaysia, your clock keeps running from your first Malaysian entry — count from that date.
- **Treating a work or study move as automatic.** A Peninsular job or university place does not entitle you to work or study in Sarawak without the right state pass.
- **Booking a tight onward connection out of Mulu.** Weather cancellations are routine; leave a buffer day.
- **Arriving at Mulu expecting to pay only park entry.** The pass is entry only — guided caves and the Pinnacles cost extra and should be reserved ahead.
- **Turning up unannounced at a living longhouse.** Arrange it in advance and, ideally, go with a guide.
- **Expecting guaranteed orangutans at Semenggoh.** The apes are semi-wild and free to stay in the forest; treat a sighting as a bonus, not a promise.

## What's next

Sarawak rewards travellers who plan around the fact that it is, in a real sense, a place you enter rather than pass into. Carry the right document for your status, count your days from your first Malaysian arrival, base yourself in Kuching, and treat Mulu's flights and a longhouse visit as things to plan around rather than improvise.

Before you book, confirm the current visa position for your nationality and the MDAC requirement directly with the Immigration Department of Malaysia, and reserve Mulu flights and guided activities early — both the aircraft and the licensed cave tours are capacity-limited. From there, the caves, the rainforest, and the communities do the rest.
