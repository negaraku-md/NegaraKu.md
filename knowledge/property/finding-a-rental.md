You find a listing you like on a portal, message the number on it, and someone shows up to unlock the unit. Nothing in that exchange tells you whether the person handing you the keys is legally allowed to be doing this for a fee at all — and almost no one asks.

That's the gap this article closes: where the searching actually happens, who you're dealing with once you contact someone, and how the money around that contact is supposed to work.

## Where the search actually happens

Three portals carry most of the market.

**iProperty** and **PropertyGuru** lean toward agency-managed listings — a named negotiator or agency attached to every unit, with a directory searchable by state or agency name. **Mudah** carries a much larger share of direct owner-to-tenant classifieds alongside agent posts, which is why it turns up more raw, unfiltered listings — and more variance in how current they are.

In practice, most searches end up checking all three, since no single portal has full coverage of the market at any given moment. None of that changes who you're actually transacting with once you make contact — that runs on a separate, legal track.

## Who is allowed to act for a fee

Malaysia regulates who can be paid to arrange a property transaction, including a rental. The **Board of Valuers, Appraisers, Estate Agents and Property Managers Malaysia** — commonly abbreviated **BOVAEP**, and referred to on its own forms and site as **LPPEH** — administers this under the **Valuers, Appraisers, Estate Agents and Property Managers Act 1981**.

Two roles sit under that Act, and they are not interchangeable:

- **A Registered Estate Agent** is the licensed principal — the person or firm the Board has registered to run an estate agency practice.
- **A Registered Estate Negotiator (REN)** is not a separate, independent licence. The Board's own registration form, Form REN-01, requires every applicant to name one **registered agency** and one specific **Supervising Registrant** at that agency before a REN Tag is issued. A negotiator always sits under an agent — never on their own.

That structure means there is always a licensed agent standing behind the person you're messaging on a listing.

## The REN Tag, and why it's worth asking for

When the Board approves a negotiator, it issues a **REN Tag** — a numbered identifier tied to that registration. Form REN-01 also requires the applicant to declare they are at least 18, of sound mind and good character, with no fraud or dishonesty conviction in the two years before applying, and not an undischarged bankrupt.

None of that is visible from a WhatsApp number on a listing. What is visible, if you ask, is the tag itself and the agency behind it — and the Board maintains a searchable register where those details can be checked directly, rather than taken on trust from a business card. Asking "what's your REN number, and which agency are you with" costs one message. Most renters never send it.

## How commission actually works

Estate agency commission in Malaysia — including for lettings — isn't something an individual negotiator sets on the spot. It sits under the Board's **Malaysian Estate Agency Standards (MEAS)**, made under the same 1981 Act, which governs how a registered agency and its negotiators may charge for arranging a tenancy.

The mechanics worth understanding, without pinning down a figure that a standard can revise:

- Commission is normally tied to the rental agreed, not a flat sum unrelated to the deal.
- On a typical residential letting, the fee is commonly split between the negotiator representing the landlord's side and the one representing the tenant's side, when two are involved — rather than one party absorbing the whole cost.
- Because the schedule sits with the Board rather than any one firm, a fee that looks unusually high, or is demanded up front before a tenancy agreement even exists, is a reasonable prompt to ask which schedule it's based on.

If a number matters to your tenancy, get it from the agency in writing, not from a portal ad.

## Common mistakes

**Treating every listing contact as an "agent."** Many are RENs working under someone else's licence — fine, provided that agency and supervising registrant genuinely exist and can be checked.

**Paying anything before a viewing.** A legitimate negotiator arranges viewings before money changes hands; a "booking fee" by transfer, sight unseen, is a recurring scam pattern.

**Assuming Mudah listings are less "official" than portal-agent ones.** Ownership of a unit and registration of the person marketing it are separate questions — check the second regardless of which site the listing came from.

**Not asking for the agency name.** A REN Tag number means little without the agency it's registered under; ask for both together.

## What's next

Once you've shortlisted a unit and a negotiator you can verify, the next steps are the tenancy agreement itself and setting up deposit and rent payments — most of which move through online banking rather than cash. If you don't yet have a Malaysian bank account, see [opening a bank account in Malaysia](/en/money-daily-life/opening-bank-account). If you're renting on the strength of a new job offer rather than as a citizen or PR, get the [Employment Pass](/en/settling-in/employment-pass) sorted in parallel — landlords and agencies routinely ask for proof of a valid pass before signing a longer lease.
