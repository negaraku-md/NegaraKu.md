Few things sour the end of a tenancy like a landlord who keeps the deposit. It is usually a meaningful sum, it often feels unjust, and the temptation is to escalate straight away. Before you do, it helps to understand the one fact that shapes everything about a deposit dispute in Malaysia — because it is not what many tenants assume.

## There is no deposit scheme — the agreement and your evidence are the law

In Malaysia, **a residential tenancy is governed by the ordinary law of contract (the Contracts Act 1950) and the terms of the agreement you signed.** There is **no specific residential tenancy statute in force**, **no deposit-protection or escrow scheme** (no government body or third party holds your deposit — your landlord holds it directly), **no statutory cap** on the deposit or on deductions, and **no dedicated tenancy tribunal.** A dispute over a withheld deposit is decided, if it comes to that, in the **ordinary civil courts.**

(A **Residential Tenancy Act** has been discussed for several years and could change this picture — deposit handling, timelines, the dispute forum — but it is **not yet law.** Confirm its current status before relying on any new protection.)

This reframes the whole problem. There is no form to file with a deposit authority, no statutory deadline, no regulator to complain to. **Your power comes from the agreement, your evidence, and the ordinary civil remedies** — which is why dated records matter more than anything else.

## What a landlord can, and can't, deduct

Your landlord holds the **security deposit** (commonly about two months' rent) and a separate **utility deposit** (commonly about half a month) as security — these amounts are **market convention, not law**, so check your own agreement. The landlord **cannot simply declare the deposit forfeited.** A deduction must have a basis in the agreement and reflect a **real, provable loss** — a landlord generally may keep only what matches an actual, justifiable cost, not an arbitrary windfall.

**A landlord may typically deduct** (where the agreement allows and the loss is proven):

- **Unpaid rent** owing at move-out.
- **Unpaid utilities or bills** — with final meter readings taken and accounts closed.
- **Repairing damage beyond fair wear and tear** that the tenant caused.
- **Agreed cleaning**, where the agreement provides for it and the unit was left below the agreed standard.
- **Missing or broken inventory items** — keys, access cards, listed furniture or fittings.

**A landlord may not deduct for:**

- **Fair wear and tear** — the normal deterioration of ordinary use: faded paint, minor scuffs, worn flooring, small nail holes, the ageing of appliances. This is the landlord's cost, not yours.
- **Betterment** — charging you the full price of a brand-new replacement for an old, already-worn item.
- **Vague, round-number deductions** with no itemisation and no proof.

The dividing line is **damage** (beyond what careful, normal use would cause — a cracked basin, a burn in the worktop, a hole in a door) versus **wear and tear** (what any reasonable tenant would leave behind). There is no statutory checklist; it turns on the facts and, above all, on the **condition at move-in versus move-out.** Which is exactly why evidence decides most disputes.

## Gather your evidence first

In a contract dispute, the party with **dated, specific records wins**, and the party relying on memory loses. Before you send anything, assemble:

1. **The signed tenancy agreement** — especially the deposit clause (amount, what it secures, any return process).
2. **A move-in AND a move-out inventory / condition report** — ideally signed by both parties. The pairing is what proves wear and tear versus damage.
3. **Timestamped photos and videos at both move-in and move-out** — room by room, with close-ups of any pre-existing marks. This is your most persuasive evidence.
4. **Proof the deposit was paid** — the bank transfer or receipt. You cannot reclaim what you can't show you paid.
5. **Proof rent and bills were settled** to the last day, with meters read and accounts closed.
6. **Key-handover confirmation** — that you returned vacant possession, with a date.
7. **Every written message** with the landlord about the deposit and the move-out — keep the originals; don't delete them.

Put it all into one dated bundle. A tidy, evidenced case is more credible and often settles the matter before any formal step.

## The escalation ladder

Escalate in proportion to the sum; most cases should end at the first or second rung.

1. **A written demand.** Ask, politely but firmly and **in writing**, for the deposit to be returned by a reasonable deadline — *or* for an itemised, evidenced account of any deductions. Refer to your evidence; stay factual. Many disputes end here once the landlord sees you have records and know the wear-and-tear line.
2. **A formal letter of demand.** More formal, often drafted by a lawyer, setting out the claim, the basis, the sum and a deadline before legal action. It signals seriousness and creates a clear pre-litigation record — weigh the lawyer's fee against the deposit.
3. **A civil claim.** Malaysia has no separate small-claims *court*, but the **Magistrates' Court** has a **simplified small-claims procedure** that an **individual can use in person, without a lawyer**, for smaller sums; larger claims go as an ordinary civil suit to the Magistrates' or, above its limit, the **Sessions Court.** The **monetary limits, fees and forms change**, so check the current ones with the court (**kehakiman.gov.my**) rather than trusting a figure quoted anywhere. One caution on the forum: the **Tribunal for Homebuyer Claims** is for house *buyers* against developers, and a private residential tenancy deposit is **generally not** within the **Tribunal for Consumer Claims** either — so don't assume a consumer tribunal will hear it; confirm with a lawyer or legal aid.
4. **Legal aid and free advice.** The **Legal Aid Department (Jabatan Bantuan Guaman, JBG)** offers means-tested help, and the **Bar Council Legal Aid Centre** (and state Bar centres) give free or subsidised advice — each subject to its own eligibility criteria.

## Be proportionate — and prevent the next one

Be honest with yourself about the maths: **court can cost more time and money than a small deposit.** For most people, negotiation backed by a clean, dated evidence bundle is the most proportionate route, and strong move-in and move-out records frequently make a landlord back down with no court at all.

Two last points. First, a common mistake: **don't simply withhold your last month's rent to "cover" the deposit** unless your agreement clearly allows it — that can itself be a breach. Second, the real fix is **prevention**: a signed inventory, dated move-in photos and videos, and a clear deposit clause (amount, what it secures, how and when it is returned) agreed at the **start** of every tenancy are what make a later dispute winnable or unnecessary. **Most deposit disputes are lost at move-in, not at move-out.**
