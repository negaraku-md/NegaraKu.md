In October 2026, Formula 1 cars will run at Sepang for the first time in nine years — and the trackside signage will read *Bahrain Grand Prix*. Malaysia is not the host on paper, will not pay the licence fee, and did not lobby its way back onto the calendar. It simply had the one thing Formula 1 suddenly needed: an empty, ready, top-tier circuit.

That single quirk is the whole story of Sepang in miniature. It is a world-class venue Malaysia spent nearly three decades building a reputation around, then walked away from at the top level because the arithmetic stopped working — and which the sport now needs more than the country needs the sport.

## What is Sepang International Circuit, and why did Malaysia build it?

Sepang was Malaysia's statement that it belonged in global motorsport. Work began in December 1996 and the circuit was completed in November 1998 — a 14-month main build — at a cost of **RM286 million**, with well over 1,000 workers on site at peak, according to circuit records compiled by RacingCircuits.info.

The design went to German engineer **Hermann Tilke**. Sepang was his first Formula 1 circuit drawn from the ground up — the template for a generation of Tilke-designed venues that followed across Asia and the Middle East. Prime Minister **Mahathir Mohamad** formally inaugurated it in **March 1999**.

The Grand Prix layout is technically demanding and deliberately spectacular:

| Specification | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| Circuit length | 5.543 km |
| Corners | 15 |
| Straights | 8 (two long high-speed straights) |
| Track width | 16 to 20 metres |
| Spectator capacity | around 130,000 (30,000 in the double-fronted main grandstand) |
| F1 lap record | 1:34.080 — Sebastian Vettel, Ferrari, 2017 |

The capacity figures — around 130,000, with 30,000 in the double-fronted main grandstand — are from RacingCircuits.info; Malay Mail similarly reported a 130,000 seated capacity (and over 170,000 across all zones) in its July 2026 coverage.

The wide track and long straights were chosen on purpose: two DRS-era straights separated by a hairpin make it one of the best overtaking circuits on the calendar, and the palm-leaf grandstand canopies were designed to echo the plantation landscape it was carved out of. The one thing engineers could not design around was the weather — Sepang's equatorial afternoon downpours became a signature of its races.

## What happened during the Formula 1 years?

Malaysia hosted the Formula 1 Malaysian Grand Prix for **19 seasons, from 1999 to 2017**. **Eddie Irvine** won the inaugural 1999 race for Ferrari; **Max Verstappen** won the final one in 2017 for Red Bull, the day after his 20th birthday.

For a decade the race was a genuine calendar highlight — a season-opener or early-season fixture, tropical heat, sudden rain, and the drama that came with it. But the numbers that mattered to the government were the ones in the grandstands, and those were sliding.

By 2016, the weekend drew **88,828 spectators**, with roughly 45,000 on race day at a venue built to hold well over 100,000, per figures cited by Paul Tan's Automotive News. The 2017 farewell rallied a bump to **110,604** across the weekend — because, as the site dryly noted, nothing sells tickets like announcing the show is closing. A one-off spike could not reverse the trend.

## Why did Malaysia drop Formula 1?

Because the bill kept rising while the returns kept falling — and the government said so plainly.

In November 2016, Tourism and Culture Minister **Nazri Aziz** told reporters that Malaysia was spending around **RM300 million a year** to host the Grand Prix with no financial return, and that "F1 attendance is dropping and there is less attraction now." His conclusion, reported by the South China Morning Post, left no ambiguity: the agreement ran to 2018, and after that there would be no more Formula 1 in Malaysia.

In the end the exit came even earlier. The cabinet ended the race after the **2017** edition, a year before the contract's 2018 expiry, in a decision described as mutually agreed with Formula One Management.

The reasoning stacked up on itself:

- **A hosting fee that only went up.** The annual licence fee paid to Formula One Management ran into the hundreds of millions of ringgit — the dominant line item in that RM300 million figure.
- **Falling attendance.** Fewer than half the grandstand filled on race day by 2016.
- **Eroding broadcast value.** The exposure argument weakens when domestic interest and TV audiences soften.
- **Competing priorities.** RM300 million a year is a large slice of a national sports budget for a single weekend.

## How is Formula 1 returning in 2026 without a hosting fee?

Through an accident of geopolitics, not a change of heart.

On **26 July 2026**, Formula 1 and the FIA confirmed that Sepang will host a Grand Prix on **2-4 October 2026**. Crucially, the event is **not** the Malaysian Grand Prix — it is the **Formula 1 Gulf Air Bahrain Grand Prix**, relocated to Malaysia after the Bahrain round was cancelled amid conflict in West Asia. Bahrain remains the contracted host country; Sepang is only the physical venue. F1 chief executive Stefano Domenicali framed it as the sport's ability "to adapt, find solutions, and deliver."

The financial mechanics are what make it remarkable for Malaysia. Because **Bahrain had already paid its FOM licence fee** for the season before its event fell through, Malaysia inherits an exemption from that fee. SIC chief executive **Azhan Shafriman** told Malay Mail that Bahrain's FOM fee sits between US$70 million and US$80 million (roughly RM298 million) — and Malaysia pays none of it, spending only an estimated **RM40 million** to prepare the circuit. Prime Minister **Anwar Ibrahim** put it bluntly: "It is free. If there is any expenditure, it will only be for several improvements to the circuit."

On the ongoing cost, Anwar told reporters the government would spend only the **RM16 million** it already allocates each year to maintain and upgrade Sepang, according to Free Malaysia Today. That 2026 maintenance figure sits above the **RM10 million a year** that then-Youth and Sports Minister Hannah Yeoh cited in August 2025 for keeping the track at FIA Grade 1 standard — the two official figures differ, and public reporting does not reconcile them.

So Malaysia gets the global television exposure and tourist inflow of a Formula 1 weekend without the multi-hundred-million-ringgit fee that made the event politically unpopular in the first place. It is, in effect, the deal Malaysia could never negotiate for itself — handed to it by circumstance.

Here is how the three arrangements compare:

| | F1 host era (1999-2017) | F1 substitute (Oct 2026) | MotoGP (current) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Who pays the sanctioning fee | Malaysia | Bahrain (already paid) | Malaysia |
| Approx. fee | ~RM300 million/year | RM0 to Malaysia | roughly a quarter of F1's, per the sports ministry |
| Malaysia's direct outlay | Full hosting cost | ~RM40 million prep | Hosting fee (a fraction of F1's) |
| Event branding | Malaysian Grand Prix | Bahrain Grand Prix | Malaysian Grand Prix |
| Status | Ended after 2017 | One-off | Contracted to 2031 |

Note: separate from the table, Sepang carries a standing annual circuit-maintenance cost the government cites at RM10 million (Yeoh, 2025) to RM16 million (Anwar, 2026) — a fixed cost of keeping the venue race-ready, not attributable to any single event.

## Why did MotoGP thrive where Formula 1 failed?

Same circuit, same weather, opposite outcome — and the difference is almost entirely about cost and crowd.

MotoGP's sanctioning fee is far smaller than Formula 1's — the sports ministry has put it at roughly a quarter of F1's — and the Malaysian round has become one of the best-attended weekends on the two-wheeled calendar. The **2025** event drew a record **190,977 spectators** across the weekend, per Paul Tan's Automotive News — comfortably more than any F1 crowd Sepang saw in its final years.

The economics follow the crowd. The 2024 edition generated a **RM694 million** economic impact, supported more than **4,400 jobs**, and returned about **6.3 times** its cost, according to figures the Youth and Sports Ministry cited via Bernama. On that basis the cabinet approved a **five-year extension keeping MotoGP at Sepang from 2027 to 2031**, granting full approval on 15 April 2026. Youth and Sports Minister **Mohammed Taufiq Johari** called the renewal "a strategic government decision to ensure Malaysia remains relevant and competitive as a global motorsport hub."

The contrast is the lesson. A venue is only as viable as the event it hosts, and the event is only as viable as the gap between what it costs and what fills the seats. MotoGP's gap runs the right way; Formula 1's did not.

## Will Formula 1 come back permanently?

The 2026 one-off is not a comeback — and Malaysian officials have been careful to say so.

The obstacle has never been the circuit; it has always been the fee. In August 2025, then-Youth and Sports Minister **Hannah Yeoh** ruled out a full return on cost grounds, telling Malay Mail that a permanent revival would run to **RM900 million to RM1.5 billion over three to five years** in hosting and sanctioning fees. Her argument was one of opportunity cost: the roughly **RM10 million** spent annually just maintaining the circuit could instead fund the national Road to Gold athlete programme for a full year, and RM300 million could support sports development across some 20 disciplines. MotoGP, she noted, delivers superior value at a fraction of F1's fee.

(The Youth and Sports portfolio was held by Yeoh when she made these August 2025 remarks; in a cabinet reshuffle on 16 December 2025 she was replaced by Mohammed Taufiq Johari, who held the post by the 2026 MotoGP renewal — the same ministerial title, a different office-holder. Each figure above is attributed to whoever held the post at the time it was stated.)

So what would actually have to change for a permanent return to pencil out? A realistic checklist:

- **A far lower sanctioning fee** — the single biggest lever, and one Malaysia has little control over.
- **A commercial partner to carry the cost**, so it does not fall on the public purse. National oil company Petronas, long associated with the circuit, has publicly denied working on a return.
- **Demonstrated ticket demand** at 2010s-or-better levels to justify the outlay.
- **A calendar slot**, on an F1 schedule that is already full and expanding into new markets.
- **A political case** that a Grand Prix beats the same money spent on athlete development.

None of those is impossible; all of them at once is hard. The 2026 substitute race is best read as a low-risk audition — a chance to show Sepang can still deliver a world-class Formula 1 weekend, without betting nine figures of public money to find out.

## Common misconceptions

- **"The 2026 race means F1 is back in Malaysia."** It is a one-off, and officially the Bahrain Grand Prix. Nothing about the permanent calendar has changed.
- **"Malaysia is paying to bring F1 back."** For 2026 it is not paying the sanctioning fee at all — Bahrain already did. Malaysia's outlay is limited to circuit preparation.
- **"Sepang was abandoned or fell into disuse."** The opposite. It never stopped running MotoGP, endurance racing, testing and track days; it dropped one expensive event, not the venue.
- **"F1 left because the track wasn't good enough."** The circuit is widely rated among the best overtaking venues in the sport. The exit was a budget decision, not an engineering one.
- **"MotoGP and F1 cost about the same to host."** They do not. MotoGP's fee is a small fraction of F1's — the core reason one stayed and the other left.

## What's next

The immediate date to watch is **2-4 October 2026**, when Formula 1 returns to Sepang as Bahrain's stand-in — the first F1 cars on the grid there since 2017, and a live test of whether Malaysia still wants the sport at any price. Watch the crowd figure and the government's tone afterward: a strong turnout could revive the permanent-return debate that cost has so far shut down.

Beyond that, the settled picture is two-wheeled. MotoGP is locked in through **2031**, underwritten by attendance and economics that Formula 1 never matched in its final Malaysian years. Sepang's legacy, for now, is not the sport that put it on the map — it is the venue itself: a Tilke original that outlasted the Grand Prix it was built for, and stayed valuable enough that the world came back to borrow it.
