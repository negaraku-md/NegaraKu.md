Every two years, thousands of youth athletes march into a host state's stadium — not for world medals, but for the pride of their respective states and, for the most talented, a ticket to the national elite stage. That is SUKMA.

## What is SUKMA?

SUKMA (Sukan Malaysia, the Malaysia Games) is a multi-sport games built on a "mini Olympics" concept, pitting youth athletes representing their respective states against one another. It brings dozens of sports together under a single games, complete with an opening ceremony, a parade of state contingents and a medal tally — a scaled-down version of the Olympic Games for Malaysia's younger generation.

Unlike other national championships, SUKMA focuses on athletes at the youth level. This makes it the first arena in which a state's real talent is tested against other states in genuine competitive conditions.

## When did SUKMA begin and who organises it?

The first SUKMA was held in Kuala Lumpur in April 1986. Since then, it has been organised **biennially** — every two years — alternating with the SEA Games, with states taking turns as host.

Its organisation is managed by the **[National Sports Council of Malaysia](/sports/national-sports-council-msn) (MSN)** together with the state sports councils, the Malaysian Schools Sports Council (MSSM), the Malaysian Universities Sports Council (MASUM) and the Royal Malaysia Police Sports Council. The latest edition, **SUKMA XXII Selangor 2026**, is scheduled to run from 15 to 24 August 2026. The dedicated games for athletes with disabilities, **Para SUKMA**, is scheduled separately from 5 to 14 September 2026.

The 2026 edition also set a record of its own: 474 events were contested — the most in SUKMA's history to date.

## From 1986 to today: age limit, champions and records

SUKMA is an **under-21 (U-21)** competition, and that age cap is the core of its identity — the National Sports Council (MSN) defends the limit as a way of keeping the games focused on the development of young athletes. The only exception so far was the 2022 edition (SUKMA XX), which was allowed to raise the cap to under-23 because the 2020 Games were cancelled by the COVID-19 pandemic.

Since the first edition in Kuala Lumpur in 1986 — which gathered about 4,500 athletes from 16 contingents — the games have rotated among the states, from Kuching and Johor Bahru to Alor Setar, Kuala Terengganu and Kota Kinabalu. **Selangor** is the most successful contingent, crowned overall champion nine times, followed by Terengganu and Sarawak. The record for most golds in a single edition also belongs to Selangor, with 80 golds at SUKMA VII in 1998.

## How does SUKMA feed the elite talent pipeline?

SUKMA sits on the first rung of Malaysia's sports development system. Athletes who shine at these games become candidates for promotion to the next levels:

1. **SUKMA** — the state stage for youth athletes to prove themselves.
2. **MSN's talent-development and reserve programmes** — among them the Talent Programme (Program Bakat) and the Reserve Programme (Program Pelapis), tiered programmes that absorb and hone new talent under MSN.
3. **The [Podium Programme](/sports/national-sports-policy-podium-programme)** — the national elite-athlete programme.

The **Podium Programme** was launched on 2 February 2016 under the Ministry of Youth and Sports and is run by the **National Sports Institute (ISN)**. It is designed specifically to prepare elite athletes to achieve world-class performances at major international games such as the Olympic Games, the Asian Games and the Commonwealth Games. Selection of athletes into the Podium Programme is made by ISN based on the selection criteria it sets.

In other words, SUKMA supplies the pool of raw talent, while the reserve and Podium programmes filter and hone that talent into high-performing athletes.

## Talent-pipeline proof: they started at SUKMA

The theory is simple; the proof is in the names. Several well-known national athletes first stood out on the SUKMA stage before representing Malaysia internationally:

- **Pandelela Rinong** (diving, Sarawak) — later became Malaysia's first Olympic diving medallist (bronze, individual 10m platform, London 2012; silver, synchronised, Rio 2016). She has said SUKMA revived her career: after nearly quitting in 2004, her return with three golds at the 2006 Kedah SUKMA rekindled her passion for the sport.
- **Goh Jin Wei** (badminton, Penang) — won the women's singles gold at SUKMA 2014 aged 14, before becoming BWF World Junior champion (2015 and 2018) and Malaysia's first Youth Olympic Games gold medallist.
- **Welson Sim** (swimming, Sarawak) — took gold at SUKMA XV (2012) before competing at the Rio 2016 Olympics and winning at the SEA Games.
- **Khairul Hafiz Jantan** (athletics, Melaka) — set the national 100m record (10.18s) at SUKMA XVIII 2016, breaking Watson Nyambek's 18-year-old mark, then rose to the national squad.

This list is no coincidence: it shows SUKMA's real function as a nursery, where state talent is first tested before being filtered up to higher levels.

## Why do states compete to host?

For a state, hosting SUKMA brings more than pride. Hosting a major games is typically associated with the upgrading or construction of sports facilities such as stadiums, aquatic complexes and training centres, although the scale and long-term benefits vary by state and edition. The advantage of competing on home ground is also often cited as a factor that can help the host contingent's performance, even though the effect is not guaranteed.
