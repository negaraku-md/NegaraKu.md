In May 1992, the whole nation paused for a moment. At Stadium Negara, Kuala Lumpur, a team of young men led by the Sidek brothers defeated Indonesia to lift the Thomas Cup — and as Datuk Rashid Sidek put it, "all of Malaysia went viral and everyone wanted to play badminton." Three decades later, the trophy still has not returned. The story of Malaysian badminton is a story of early glory, a painful drought, and a champion or two carrying a nation's hopes on their shoulders.

Badminton is not merely a sport in Malaysia; it is almost a national language. To understand why, we need to start with the body that watches over it.

## Who governs badminton in Malaysia?

It all begins with the **Badminton Association of Malaysia** (BAM). BAM was founded in Kuala Lumpur on **11 November 1934**, at the time under the name *Badminton Association of Malaya*. The name itself marks the era — this was a pre-independence country, and the sport was already organised enough to have its own national governing body.

Today BAM is the sole governing body for the sport in Malaysia. It is affiliated with three levels of international administration:

- **Badminton World Federation (BWF)** — the world body that sets the rules of play, rankings and the calendar of major championships.
- **Badminton Asia (BA)** — the continental body that organises Asia-level championships.
- **Olympic Council of Malaysia** — the gateway for badminton athletes into the Olympic Games and the Commonwealth Games.

BAM is registered under the country's sports development framework and operates through various committees, councils and national squad coaching programmes. Its headquarters are now located at the **Akademi Badminton Malaysia (ABM)** in Bukit Kiara, Kuala Lumpur — a complex that also serves as the main training hub for the national team. We will return to ABM's role in the player development pathway later.

The key thing to understand: BAM wears two hats at once. It is the administrative body (registering players, managing governance, sending out teams) and at the same time the elite training body (employing coaches, funding national squad players). It is the tension between these two roles that would later spark one of the most controversial episodes in the sport — the departure of Lee Zii Jia.

## How did Malaya become the world's first Thomas Cup power?

The Thomas Cup is the most prestigious men's team championship in badminton — in short, the "men's World Cup of badminton." Its inaugural edition was held in **1949**, and the first nation to lift it was **Malaya**.

That was no accident. From the late 1940s into the 1950s, Malaya produced a line of singles players who dominated the world's courts — names like **Wong Peng Soon**, the first Asian to win the All England, were the pillars of the Thomas Cup squad of that time. The combination of singles and doubles talent made Malaya a champion that was hard to match.

Here are the five times Malaysia (and Malaya before 1957) lifted the Thomas Cup:

| Year | Name of the country at the time | Note |
|------:|------------------------|---------|
| 1949  | Malaya                 | Inaugural champion — the first nation to lift the Thomas Cup |
| 1952  | Malaya                 | Defended the dominance of the Wong Peng Soon era |
| 1955  | Malaya                 | Third title in less than a decade |
| 1967  | Malaysia               | First title as an independent Malaysia |
| 1992  | Malaysia               | The last title to date — at Stadium Negara |

The first three titles came in just six years (1949–1955), a dominance that placed Malaya among the great powers of world badminton alongside Indonesia. After the 1967 title, Malaysia entered a long dry spell — almost a quarter of a century — before the glory of 1992.

## Why is 1992 still the last time Malaysia lifted the Thomas Cup?

The 1992 win was not just another title; it was a generational moment. After 25 years without a Thomas Cup, a team anchored by the **Sidek brothers** lifted the trophy at **Stadium Negara**, in front of a home crowd. For many Malaysians who grew up in the 1990s, that was their first sporting memory.

The problem: it has not been repeated. Datuk Rashid Sidek, one of the heroes of 1992, admitted honestly that he "never thought Malaysia would find it so difficult to bring home the Thomas Cup." He expected the next title to come within 10 to 20 years, given that Malaysia had abundant talent, modern facilities and infrastructure far better than in his day. Instead, the drought has now exceeded **32 years**.

This is the central paradox of Malaysian badminton. The country produces world-class singles players almost every generation — but the Thomas Cup demands depth across *all* categories: two singles and three doubles, won within a single team tie. A team can have one of the world's greatest singles stars and still be knocked out if its doubles are not deep enough. That is what has happened to Malaysia again and again over these three decades.

Chasing the Thomas Cup, in other words, is a test of the system — not just a test of the star.

## Who are Malaysia's All England champions?

If the Thomas Cup measures team depth, the **All England** measures individual greatness. It is the oldest badminton championship in the world, and to become champion there means you are among the best of your era. Malaysia has a long history here, especially in men's singles.

| Player          | Years won at All England (men's singles) | Note |
|-----------------|-----------------------------------------------|---------|
| Wong Peng Soon  | 1950, 1951, 1952, 1955                         | First Asian to win the All England |
| Eddy Choong     | 1953, 1954, 1956, 1957                         | Famous for his *jump smash* |
| Lee Chong Wei   | 2010, 2011, 2014, 2017                         | Four titles; runner-up in 2009, 2012, 2013 |
| Lee Zii Jia     | 2021                                           | Defeated Viktor Axelsen in the final |

Look at the pattern. In the 1950s, Wong Peng Soon and Eddy Choong shared almost every men's singles title — a Malayan dominance that ran parallel to the golden age of the Thomas Cup. Then came a long gap at this pinnacle level, until a player from Bukit Mertajam brought Malaysia's name back to the champions' board: Lee Chong Wei.

## Just how great is Lee Chong Wei's record?

Datuk Lee Chong Wei is the greatest men's singles player Malaysia has ever produced, and one of the most consistent in the history of the sport. According to BAM's official records, his professional career began in 2000 and lasted 19 years. The numbers speak for themselves:

- **All England:** champion four times (2010, 2011, 2014, 2017); runner-up three times (2009, 2012, 2013).
- **Olympics:** three consecutive men's singles finals — and three consecutive silver medals (**Beijing 2008, London 2012, Rio 2016**).
- **World ranking:** a total of **348 weeks** as world number one, including **199 consecutive weeks** (21 August 2008 to 14 June 2012).
- **Commonwealth Games:** five gold medals.
- **Total titles:** 69 across his career, including 47 BWF World Superseries titles — the most in history.

That figure of 199 consecutive weeks — nearly four unbroken years at the top of the world — is perhaps the record hardest to fully appreciate. It means that from August 2008 to June 2012, not a single other player on the planet occupied the number one throne.

Yet there was one line that was never filled in on his list of achievements: **Olympic gold**. Three times to the final, three times home with silver. That reality — the world's best talent without an Olympic crown — became the emblem of Malaysian badminton's paradox as a whole: so close, so often, yet the greatest title slipping away. Lee Chong Wei retired in 2019, closing an era.

## What happened after Lee Chong Wei — the story of Lee Zii Jia?

Every badminton nation dreads the same question: who comes after the legend? For Malaysia, the answer emerged in the form of **Lee Zii Jia**.

Zii Jia made his name in March 2021 by winning the **All England** — defeating world number one Viktor Axelsen in the final, and becoming only the third Malaysian player to win the championship this century and the first since Lee Chong Wei's victory in 2017. For a moment, it looked as though Lee Chong Wei's true heir had arrived.

Then came the decision that stunned everyone. In **January 2022**, Zii Jia resigned from BAM to become an independent player — almost a decade after joining the body at the age of 13. He said he wanted to focus on his own training and ranking, and stressed that "whether we are national players or independent players, we all fly the Jalur Gemilang." BAM initially imposed restrictions, but the two sides eventually reached a settlement that allowed Zii Jia to continue his career as an independent player under his own team.

That move started a major debate in Malaysian sport: should elite players be bound to the national association, or free to manage their own careers? Zii Jia's answer on court came at **Paris 2024**, when he took an **Olympic bronze medal**, defeating India's Lakshya Sen 13-21, 21-16, 21-11 in the third-place playoff on 5 August 2024. With that he became the third Malaysian men's singles player to win an Olympic medal — after Datuk Rashid Sidek (bronze at Atlanta 1996) and Datuk Lee Chong Wei (three silvers).

## Not only singles — how did doubles make history?

While the world focused its attention on the singles stars, one historic success came from the men's doubles category. On **28 August 2022**, in Tokyo, **Aaron Chia and Soh Wooi Yik** defeated three-time world champions Mohammad Ahsan-Hendra Setiawan of Indonesia 21-19, 21-14 to become **BWF world champions**.

That victory broke a sad record: it was Malaysia's first world championship title in the history of the tournament, ending a **45-year** wait. For a country that had for so long produced great players without a world crown, this was an achievement that corrected part of the "so close" narrative.

## How are elite players developed in Malaysia?

None of these big names appeared by chance. Behind them lies a development system centred on the **Akademi Badminton Malaysia (ABM)** in Bukit Kiara, Kuala Lumpur — the complex that houses BAM and serves as the main training hub for the national team.

ABM's role extends beyond Malaysia. It also serves as the base of the **Asian High Performance Badminton Centre**, an initiative to elevate badminton development across Asia by offering elite training to players, coaches and umpires — especially from developing National Olympic Committees (NOCs). In other words, Malaysia not only trains its own players here, but also serves as a reference centre for high-performance training for the continent.

The typical pathway of a talented Malaysian player looks like this:

1. **Grassroots level** — identified through clubs, schools and state-level championships.
2. **Development / junior squad** — brought into BAM's structured training programmes.
3. **National squad (senior)** — trained at ABM with BAM's coaching, sports science and funding.
4. **International circuit** — competing on the BWF World Tour under the Malaysian flag.

This is where the crucial crossroads highlighted by the Lee Zii Jia case appears: at the elite level, a player can remain within the BAM system or choose the independent path.

## Framework: national squad or independent player?

For players (and for fans trying to understand this debate), the choice can be summarised as follows:

| Factor | National squad path (BAM) | Independent player path |
|--------|-------------------------------|---------------------|
| Funding | Largely borne by BAM | Borne by the player / personal sponsors |
| Coaching & facilities | Access to ABM and BAM's support team | Must be arranged and funded independently |
| Schedule control | Set according to national team priorities | Determined by the player |
| Team championships (e.g. Thomas Cup) | Eligible to represent through BAM | Depends on a special agreement with BAM |
| Pressure & expectation | Directly as the "nation's hope" | More personal, but without the association's safety net |

There is no absolutely right answer. The national squad path offers support and a safety net; the independent path offers autonomy but shifts all the financial and logistical risk onto the player. Zii Jia's decision made this second option real and visible for the first time at the very top of Malaysian sport.

## Common misconceptions about Malaysian badminton

Several misunderstandings recur frequently. Here are the most common:

- **"Malaysia has never won the Thomas Cup."** Wrong — Malaysia/Malaya won it five times. The correct statement: Malaysia has not won *since 1992*.
- **"Lee Chong Wei has an Olympic gold."** Wrong. He reached the final three times and took *silver* three times (2008, 2012, 2016) — no gold.
- **"Malaysia has never been a world champion."** No longer true. Aaron Chia and Soh Wooi Yik won the 2022 BWF World Championships in men's doubles.
- **"Independent players don't represent Malaysia."** A misunderstanding. Independent players like Lee Zii Jia still compete under the Malaysian flag; what changes is the administrative relationship with BAM, not their sporting nationality.
- **"BAM is only an administrative body."** Only partly. BAM is also an elite training body that employs coaches and funds squad players — two roles that sometimes come into tension.
- **"Wong Peng Soon and Lee Chong Wei were contemporaries."** Far from it. Wong won the All England in the 1950s; Lee Chong Wei in the 2010s — two generations separated by more than half a century.

## What comes next

Malaysian badminton stands at an interesting crossroads. Its legacy is solid — five Thomas Cups, a long line of All England champions, and now a first world championship title in men's doubles. Yet two greatest goals still elude it: the Thomas Cup since 1992, and the Olympic singles gold that never became its own even with a player as great as Lee Chong Wei.

If you want to dig deeper into this story, some useful next steps:

- **Follow official sources.** The BAM website (bam.org.my) publishes squad lists, results and player milestones; the BWF website keeps rankings and the championship calendar.
- **Understand the calendar.** The Thomas Cup and Uber Cup are held every two years; the All England every March; the Olympics every four years. It is these points that shape the narrative of each cycle.
- **Watch the career-path debate.** Whether the national squad model or the independent player model produces more champions will be a defining question for the generation after Lee Zii Jia.
- **Track the new generation.** The depth of the doubles and the emergence of young singles players will determine whether the Thomas Cup drought finally ends.

For a country that measures its hopes with rackets and shuttlecocks, the real question is not whether Malaysia will produce its next champion — history shows that is almost certain to happen — but whether the system behind it can finally turn "so close" into the crown it has waited longest for.
