    a: "They serve different purposes. The statutory financial statements are a once-a-year, external document prepared under approved accounting standards (in Malaysia, MFRS or MPERS), audited where required, circulated to members and lodged with SSM under the Companies Act 2016. The first time someone hands you a set of **monthly management accounts**, it can be baffling: several pages of statements and schedules, numbers that don't match your bank balance, and no obvious place to start reading. This guide is a map. It explains what the pack *is*, what each part contains, and — the real point — **what the numbers are actually telling you** as the owner of the business.

Start with the most important distinction, because it clears up half the confusion.

## What management accounts are (and are not)

There are three different financial documents in a company's life, and people mix them up constantly:

| | **Management accounts** | **Statutory financial statements** | **Tax computation** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **For whom** | You and your managers, to run the business | Members, SSM, lenders | LHDN |
| **How often** | Regularly — usually monthly | Once a financial year | Once a year, with the return |
| **Fixed format?** | **No** — any layout | Yes — under approved standards (MFRS/MPERS) | Prescribed by tax law |
| **Audited?** | **No — unaudited** | Audited where required | — |
| **Purpose** | **Decisions**, not filing | Statutory reporting and filing | Working out tax |

So **management accounts are an internal, usually monthly, unaudited tool for decisions.** They are *not* the annual statutory accounts you prepare under accounting standards and lodge with SSM under the Companies Act 2016, and they are *not* your tax computation (which starts from accounting profit and adjusts it to reach the figure LHDN taxes). The same well-kept ledger produces all three — good management accounts depend on a well-structured ledger, which is a separate topic (see [Sdn Bhd bookkeeping](/en/accounting/sdn-bhd-bookkeeping)) — but the monthly pack is the running instrument, not the filed document. Expect its numbers to be provisional and the year-end figures to differ.

## What's in a typical monthly pack

No law prescribes the contents, but a typical pack has a recognisable shape. At its core are three statements, surrounded by working-capital detail and a commentary:

- **Profit & loss (income statement)** — for the month *and* year-to-date, usually shown against budget and the prior period. *The owner's question:* did we make money, and how does it compare?
- **Balance sheet (statement of financial position)** — a snapshot *as at* month-end: what you own, what you owe, and the owners' stake. *The owner's question:* is the business getting stronger, and why does cash feel tight even when profit looks fine?
- **Cash position / cash-flow summary** — cash on hand and where it went, sometimes a short forecast. *The owner's question:* can I pay wages, suppliers and tax when they fall due?
- **Working-capital schedules** — most usefully **aged receivables** (who owes you, grouped by how overdue — often the single most useful page for a growing business) and **aged payables** (what you owe suppliers), plus inventory and a gross-margin breakdown by product or line where relevant.
- **Commentary and a few KPIs** — a short written note explaining the material movements, with headline numbers.

Contents vary by business and provider; the durable core is the **three statements plus the working-capital schedules plus a commentary.** Agree a consistent format and cut-off with whoever prepares it, so your months are comparable over time.

## What the numbers mean

### The profit & loss, as a ladder

Read the P&L top to bottom:

- **Revenue** — sales earned in the period. Read the *trend*, not just the level.
- **Cost of sales** — the direct costs of what you sold.
- **Gross profit** (revenue minus cost of sales), and **gross margin** (gross profit ÷ revenue) — how much of each sale is left to cover overheads; a signal of pricing and direct-cost control.
- **Operating expenses** — rent, salaries, admin.
- **Operating profit** — profit from core operations, and then **net profit**, the bottom line after everything.

A crucial caveat on every margin: **a "good" level is entirely industry-dependent.** Compare gross and net margin to your *own trend* and *your sector* — never to an absolute number.

### Profit is not cash

Management and statutory accounts use **accrual accounting**: revenue is recorded when it is **earned** (you raise the invoice or deliver the goods) and expenses when **incurred** — not when cash actually moves. That single fact explains the most common surprise for a new owner: **a month can show a profit while no cash has arrived.** Hold on to "profit is not cash" as you read the rest.

### The balance sheet and working capital

The balance sheet rests on one identity: **assets = liabilities + equity.** Split assets and liabilities into *current* (within about twelve months) and *non-current*, and you get the figure that matters most for survival: **working capital = current assets − current liabilities**, the short-term cushion for meeting near-term obligations. Its right level, too, varies by business and industry.

This is where the "profitable but broke" puzzle resolves. **A profitable, even fast-growing, business can run out of cash** because its profit is tied up in **unpaid invoices and stock**, while wages, suppliers and tax must be paid in cash *now*. Growth can worsen it: more sales mean more cash locked in receivables and inventory before customers pay. For most owners this is the single most important thing the pack can teach.

### The schedules that explain the cash gap

- **Debtor days / receivables ageing** — roughly how long customers take to pay. Rising debtor days mean more cash tied up in unpaid invoices; the ageing buckets show *which* invoices. Think: *"earned, but not yet in the bank."*
- **Creditor days** — how long you take to pay suppliers: a cash-timing lever, but stretch it too far and you strain supplier relationships.
- **Inventory** — stock ties up cash until it sells; slow-moving stock is cash sitting on a shelf.

### Ratios are trends, not scorecards

A few ratios are worth watching — **gross margin %, net margin %, the current ratio** (current assets ÷ current liabilities, a rough liquidity measure), and **debtor days** — but each one indicates something only *relative to your own history and your industry.* There is no universal pass mark, and this guide deliberately quotes none. Read them as **trends**: is this moving the right way versus last month and versus budget, and if it jumped, why? A number that changes is a question, not a verdict.

## How to use the pack

- **Compare, don't just read.** Against prior months (direction) and budget (variance). One month alone says little.
- **Investigate variances.** A figure that moves is a prompt to ask why, not a conclusion.
- **Watch cash and receivables, not just profit.** The pages that predict a cash crunch are the balance sheet, the cash position and the aged-receivables schedule.
- **Ask specific questions.** Why did gross margin move? What's in "other expenses"? Which big invoices are overdue? Is anything here estimated, and does it tie to the bank? A good commentary should answer these.
- **Keep the format consistent**, so months line up over time.
- **Remember what it is not.** Unaudited, internal and estimate-laden mid-year — use it to steer, and confirm big decisions with your accountant.

## A note on the Malaysian context

A few things sit in the background, named here lightly rather than detailed:

- Your monthly pack **feeds the annual statutory accounts** that a company prepares, has audited where required, and lodges with **SSM** under the Companies Act 2016. Those year-end statements are prepared under **MFRS or MPERS**; the internal monthly pack has no prescribed format.
- Even the year-end accounts **may not need an audit** — small private companies can qualify for audit exemption under SSM's criteria — which only reinforces that management accounts are unaudited anyway. (The exact thresholds change; check SSM.)
- Your **accounting profit is not your chargeable income** — the LHDN tax computation adjusts it — so never read the P&L bottom line as your tax bill.
- How transactions are captured is changing as Malaysia phases in **e-invoicing (MyInvois)**, which may improve the raw data behind your pack over time. (The timeline has shifted; check LHDN for current dates.)

---

**Scope and limits.** This is a guide to *reading* a monthly management pack. It is **not** tax, audit or investment advice; it quotes **no** benchmark margins or "healthy ratio" thresholds, because the right level depends on your business and sector; and it is not a bookkeeping or software how-to. Management accounts are internal, unaudited and often estimate-laden mid-year, and can differ from both the statutory year-end figures and the tax figure. For anything tax- or compliance-related, and before any significant decision, talk to your own accountant.
