When a Malaysian company signs off its financial statements, the rules it must follow are not written by the auditor, the tax office or the stock exchange. They are written by the **Malaysian Accounting Standards Board (MASB)** — the country's independent, statutory standard-setter for financial reporting.

MASB is the reason two companies in the same industry present their accounts the same way, and the reason a Malaysian balance sheet can be read alongside one from London or Singapore.

## What is MASB and where does its authority come from?

MASB was established under the **Financial Reporting Act 1997 (Act 558)** as an independent authority to develop and issue accounting and financial reporting standards in Malaysia. It replaced a system in which standards were issued by the professional accountancy bodies, putting standard-setting on a statutory footing instead.

The Act created a two-part framework. MASB does the technical work of writing standards, while the **Financial Reporting Foundation (FRF)** provides oversight — reviewing MASB's performance, approving its budget and arranging its funding. FRF members are appointed by the Minister of Finance, and the structure is designed to include preparers, users, regulators and the accountancy profession.

## What does MASB actually do?

MASB's functions are set out in **section 7** of the Act. In practice they include:

- issuing new approved accounting standards and reviewing or revising existing ones;
- amending, substituting, suspending, deferring, withdrawing or revoking standards and related pronouncements;
- consulting the public on proposed standards and accounting concepts; and
- collaborating with international standard-setters and contributing to a single set of global standards.

Crucially, MASB determines and issues standards for financial statements required under laws administered by three regulators: the **Securities Commission Malaysia**, **Bank Negara Malaysia**, and the **Registrar of Companies** (SSM). Because the Act requires statements lodged with those regulators to comply with the approved accounting standards, the standards effectively carry the **force of law** — they are not merely best practice.

## The two tiers of standards

MASB maintains two separate frameworks, depending on the type of entity.

| Framework | Who uses it | Based on | Key effective date |
|---|---|---|---|
| **MFRS** (Malaysian Financial Reporting Standards) | Non-private entities (listed companies, financial institutions, and their subsidiaries/associates) | IFRS Standards — in substance word-for-word | Mandatory from **1 January 2012** |
| **MPERS** (Malaysian Private Entities Reporting Standard) | Private entities | IASB's *IFRS for SMEs* Accounting Standard | First effective **1 January 2016**; revised **MPERS (2025)** applies from **1 January 2027** |

The MFRS Framework was issued in **November 2011**. Because MFRS is aligned word-for-word with IFRS in effect from 1 January 2012, an entity complying with MFRS can make an explicit and unreserved statement of compliance with IFRS Standards — which is why Malaysian financial statements are internationally comparable. MASB keeps MFRS current by adopting new and amended IFRS Standards as the IASB issues them.

MPERS, issued in February 2014, is a self-contained standard for private entities that do not need the full complexity of MFRS. Private companies generally choose between MFRS and MPERS.

## How MASB fits with the other regulators

It helps to separate the roles. MASB **sets** the accounting standards. The **[Malaysian Institute of Accountants (MIA)](/en/accounting/malaysian-institute-of-accountants)** regulates who may practise as an accountant, under the separate Accountants Act 1967. Auditors of public-interest entities are overseen by the Audit Oversight Board. And the standards MASB issues bite because the Securities Commission, Bank Negara and SSM require compliance in the statements filed with them.
