Malaysia describes its own foreign policy in three words: **independent, principled and pragmatic**. According to the Ministry of Foreign Affairs (Wisma Putra), that policy is founded on the values of peace, humanity, justice and equality, and its overarching aim is to safeguard Malaysia's sovereignty and national interest while contributing to a just and equitable community of nations. For a small, trade-dependent country, staying independent of any bloc is not only a moral stance — it is economic logic.

## What are the core principles?

Malaysia's approach rests on a set of tenets that have stayed remarkably stable across different governments:

| Tenet | What it means in practice |
| --- | --- |
| **Non-alignment / neutrality** | Malaysia does not join military blocs or take a permanent side in great-power rivalry |
| **Non-interference** | It respects other states' sovereignty and does not intervene in their internal affairs, and expects the same treatment |
| **Open economy** | As a trading nation, Malaysia builds relations to protect market access, investment and supply chains |
| **Peace and principled voice** | It prefers diplomatic settlement and regional stability, while still speaking out on injustice, aggression and violations of sovereignty |

Wisma Putra is mandated under the **Federal Constitution** to pursue this policy, aligning it with the objectives of the ASEAN Charter and the United Nations Charter. Top-level decisions, however, rest with the **Prime Minister and Cabinet**; the Foreign Minister — Mohamad Hasan since 12 December 2023 — leads the ministry and represents Malaysia in most diplomatic forums.

## Where does neutrality come from?

The doctrine has a formal anchor. On **27 November 1971**, five ASEAN states — Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines, Singapore and Thailand — signed the **Zone of Peace, Freedom and Neutrality (ZOPFAN)** Declaration in Kuala Lumpur, aiming to keep Southeast Asia free from outside-power interference. More than half a century later, ZOPFAN is still cited by Malaysian ministers as a basis for the country's neutral stance.

## Why ASEAN is the cornerstone

If neutrality is the principle, ASEAN is the main arena where it is practised. Malaysia is a **founding member** of ASEAN, established on **8 August 1967**, and it treats ASEAN centrality as the cornerstone of its foreign policy: acting collectively gives a medium-sized country a far stronger voice in managing relations with major powers than acting alone.

ASEAN is not the only platform. Malaysia's policy is backed by active membership in several bodies:

- **United Nations** — joined on 17 September 1957 as the Federation of Malaya (its 82nd member, via resolution 1134(XII)); a voice on global issues and a contributor to peacekeeping.
- **Organisation of Islamic Cooperation (OIC)** — solidarity with Muslim-majority nations on issues such as Palestine.
- **Non-Aligned Movement (NAM)** and **Commonwealth** — groupings consistent with Malaysia's independent posture.
- **South-South cooperation** — partnership among developing nations based on mutual respect.

## The current framework and roadmap

Two documents set today's direction. In 2021, Wisma Putra published **"Focus in Continuity: A Framework for Malaysia's Foreign Policy in a Post-Pandemic World,"** launched on **7 December 2021**. Building on it, the **Ministry of Foreign Affairs Strategic Plan 2026-2030** was launched by Prime Minister **Anwar Ibrahim** on **23 February 2026**. It carries **8 strategic thrusts, 33 strategies and 139 programmes**, each underpinned by KPIs and a monitoring framework, and is aligned with Malaysia MADANI, the Thirteenth Malaysia Plan (RMK13) and the Public Service Reform Agenda (ARPA). The eight thrusts span bilateral and multilateral diplomacy, a proactive role in ASEAN, maritime sovereignty, consular service delivery, public diplomacy, institutional excellence, and Malaysia's National Authority role under the Chemical Weapons Convention.
