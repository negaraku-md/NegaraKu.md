Malaysia runs the classic playbook of a small trading state: talk to everyone, and align permanently with no one. That is not indecision — it is doctrine. The country's founding foreign-policy principles are neutrality, non-alignment, national sovereignty and non-interference, and the interesting part is not the principles themselves but how consistently a medium-sized, export-dependent nation has translated them into behaviour across six decades and every change of government.

Most reference pages stop at either a country-by-country list of who Malaysia has relations with, or a bullet list of official principles. Neither shows the through-line. This one connects the doctrine to the institution that runs it, the multilateral clubs that amplify it, and the live positions — on Israel and Palestine, and on the balance between Washington and Beijing — where the doctrine becomes visible.

## What is the core doctrine?

Malaysia's approach rests on a handful of tenets that have stayed remarkably stable across different administrations. The posture was consolidated under Prime Minister Abdul Razak Hussein, who shifted the country toward non-alignment and neutrality — emphasising peaceful relations with all countries regardless of their ideology or political system, and upholding the right of every state to control its own domestic affairs.

| Tenet | What it means in practice |
| --- | --- |
| **Non-alignment / neutrality** | Malaysia does not join military blocs or take a permanent side in great-power rivalry |
| **Non-interference** | It respects other states' sovereignty and does not intervene in their internal affairs — and expects the same in return |
| **National sovereignty** | Protecting territorial integrity and the right to decide domestic matters is the non-negotiable core |
| **Principled voice** | It still speaks out on issues of principle — most visibly on Palestine — while preferring diplomatic settlement |

The logic is partly moral and partly economic. For a country whose prosperity depends on open trade and stable sea lanes, refusing to be captured by any single power keeps every market and every partner in play at once.

## Who runs it — and from where?

The doctrine is executed by the **Ministry of Foreign Affairs**, universally known by its headquarters name, **Wisma Putra**. The ministry was founded in **1956**, on the eve of independence, and is headquartered at Wisma Putra, No. 1 Jalan Wisma Putra, Precinct 2, Putrajaya.

Its mandate is to uphold, protect and promote Malaysia's sovereignty, territorial integrity and national interests, and to monitor and analyse regional and global developments. Structurally, it sits under a **Secretary-General**, supported by **three Deputy Secretaries-General**, **six federal departments** and **four federal agencies** — the latter including the ASEAN-Malaysia National Secretariat and the Institute of Diplomacy and Foreign Relations (IDFR).

The political head is the Foreign Minister. The incumbent is **Dato' Seri Utama Mohamad bin Haji Hasan** (widely known as Tok Mat), appointed on **12 December 2023** in a cabinet reshuffle, taking the portfolio over from Zambry Abdul Kadir.

A note on scale: as of a 2017 figure, Malaysia maintained **112 diplomatic missions** worldwide and the ministry employed **3,255 staff**. That figure is dated and should be treated as indicative rather than current.

## Why is ASEAN the cornerstone?

If neutrality is the principle, ASEAN is the arena where it is practised. Malaysia is a **founding member** of the Association of Southeast Asian Nations, created on **8 August 1967** when the foreign ministers of Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines, Singapore and Thailand signed the ASEAN Declaration in Bangkok. Acting collectively through ASEAN gives a medium-sized country a far stronger hand in managing major powers than it would ever have alone — which is why ASEAN centrality is treated as the cornerstone of the entire policy.

Malaysia was also an early architect of the region's neutrality framework. In **1971**, ASEAN adopted the **Zone of Peace, Freedom and Neutrality (ZOPFAN)** Declaration, a concept Malaysia championed; the same year, Malaysia joined the **Non-Aligned Movement** and the **Five Power Defence Arrangements** were established. The ASEAN Charter, which gave the grouping a legal personality, was signed in November 2007 and entered into force on **15 December 2008**.

Malaysia has taken its turn steering the bloc repeatedly, hosting the ASEAN Summit in **1977, 2005, 2015 and 2025**. As 2025 chair, it hosted the **46th ASEAN Summit** on 26-27 May 2025 in Kuala Lumpur and the **47th ASEAN Summit** on 26-28 October 2025 under the theme *Inclusivity and Sustainability* — both chaired by Prime Minister Anwar Ibrahim.

## What are the wider multilateral memberships?

Beyond ASEAN, Malaysia projects its non-aligned posture through a dense web of memberships, each consistent with the same independent stance.

| Body | Malaysia's position |
| --- | --- |
| **United Nations** | Joined 17 September 1957 (as Malaya), admitted unanimously as the 82nd member |
| **UN Security Council** | Four non-permanent terms: 1965, 1989-1990, 1999-2000, 2015-2016 |
| **Organisation of Islamic Cooperation** | Founding member (OIC founded 1969); Tunku Abdul Rahman was its first Secretary-General in 1971 |
| **Commonwealth** | Member since independence in 1957 |
| **Non-Aligned Movement** | Joined 1971 |
| **G-15 and G-77** | Member, aligning with the developing-world South |

Across all of these, Malaysia has established formal diplomatic relations with **189 countries** — a reach that reflects the "talk to everyone" half of the doctrine.

## Where does the doctrine become visible?

Principles are easy to state and hard to test. Two live positions show where Malaysia's stated values translate into concrete, sometimes costly, behaviour.

**Israel and Palestine.** Malaysia has **never recognised Israel** and has no diplomatic relations with it. Malaysian passports carry the statement that they are valid *for all countries except Israel*. Conversely, Malaysia officially recognises **Palestine** as a sovereign state, and its position is that any recognition of Israel would be conditional on Israel's withdrawal from occupied Palestinian territories and the establishment of a Palestinian state within the pre-1967 borders, with East Jerusalem as its capital. The stance is not merely rhetorical: in **December 2023**, Prime Minister Anwar Ibrahim declared that all Israeli-flagged ships would be barred from docking at Malaysian ports.

**The China–US balance.** Malaysia established diplomatic relations with the **People's Republic of China on 31 May 1974**, among the earliest in the region to do so. Since then it has practised a deliberate equidistance — deepening economic ties with Beijing while keeping the door open to Washington and everyone else, declining to convert commerce into a permanent alignment. This hedging is the non-alignment doctrine in its most modern form: engagement without capture.
