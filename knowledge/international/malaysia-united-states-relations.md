Barack Obama toured Masjid Negara, Kuala Lumpur's National Mosque, in 2014; Anwar Ibrahim shook hands with Donald Trump over a signed trade pact in 2025. Two very different American presidents, one durable relationship — and a Malaysian habit of getting close to Washington without ever standing under its security umbrella.

## How far back does the relationship go?

The United States recognised Malaya the moment it became independent. Diplomatic relations were established on **31 August 1957**, when Washington upgraded its Consulate General in Kuala Lumpur to an embassy and the sitting Consul General became chargé d'affaires. When the Federation of Malaysia formed in 1963 by merging Malaya with Singapore, Sabah and Sarawak, US ambassadors were re-accredited to the new federation.

For most of the following decades the relationship was quietly commercial and educational. It gained a formal architecture on **27 April 2014**, when Prime Minister Najib Razak and President Obama — during the first US presidential visit to Malaysia in 48 years — decided to elevate ties to a **Comprehensive Partnership** spanning political and diplomatic engagement, trade and investment, education and people-to-people ties, security and defence, and cooperation on the environment, science, technology and energy. That framework held for roughly a decade before both sides decided it needed an upgrade.

## What changed in 2025?

A great deal, in a single week. On **26 October 2025**, on the sidelines of the ASEAN Summit in Kuala Lumpur, Prime Minister Anwar Ibrahim and President Trump elevated the relationship to a **Comprehensive Strategic Partnership (CSP)** — the top tier of Malaysia's diplomatic classifications. Malaysia's foreign ministry (Wisma Putra) described the CSP as covering trade and investment, advanced technology, defence and security, energy security, regional stability and people-to-people ties.

The headline deliverable was economic. The same day, the two governments signed an **Agreement on Reciprocal Trade** designed to reduce tariff and non-tariff barriers and to align the countries on economic-security matters. A separate, non-binding **Defence Cooperation MoU** followed on 30 October at the ASEAN Defence Ministers' Meeting. As explained below, the trade agreement ultimately never entered into force.

## How big is the trade relationship?

Large, and lopsided. According to US Census Bureau figures, in **2024** the United States exported **US$27.7 billion** of goods to Malaysia and imported **US$52.2 billion**, leaving a US goods trade deficit of about **US$24.5 billion**. That put two-way goods trade near **US$80 billion** a year. Malaysia's own tally, cited by Wisma Putra, put total trade at **RM324.91 billion (US$71.39 billion)** for 2024.

The gap kept widening in 2025. USTR's full-year 2025 figures show US goods imports from Malaysia of **US$59.7 billion** (up 13.7 percent on 2024) against exports of **US$28.9 billion** (up 4.5 percent), pushing the US goods trade deficit to **US$30.8 billion** (up 23.9 percent). USTR put total two-way goods trade for 2025 at an estimated **US$88.5 billion**.

| Direction (goods) | 2024 | 2025 |
|-------------------|------|------|
| US exports to Malaysia | US$27.7bn | US$28.9bn (+4.5%) |
| US imports from Malaysia | US$52.2bn | US$59.7bn (+13.7%) |
| US trade deficit | ~US$24.5bn | US$30.8bn (+23.9%) |

Electronics and semiconductors dominate what Malaysia ships north — the country is a major node in global chip assembly, testing and packaging — which is why the electronics supply chain sits at the centre of the relationship.

## What did the reciprocal trade agreement do — and what happened to it?

As signed, it set the tariff terms and asked Malaysia to open specific doors. On tariffs, the US was to **maintain a 19 percent reciprocal tariff** on most Malaysian imports, while products in the annex to **Executive Order 14346** (of 5 September 2025, "Modifying the Scope of Reciprocal Tariffs..."; the "Potential Tariff Adjustments for Aligned Partners" list that USTR refers to as Annex III) would receive a **zero percent** rate. That annex's eligible zero-rate categories are goods that cannot be grown, mined or naturally produced in the US in sufficient quantities, certain agricultural products, **aircraft and aircraft parts**, and **non-patented articles for use in pharmaceutical applications** — so aerospace and pharmaceutical inputs were part of the zero-rate list, not merely separate commercial deals. Malaysian officials said roughly 1,711 tariff lines were exempted at zero percent, including palm oil, rubber, cocoa, aircraft parts and pharmaceuticals.

In exchange, Malaysia committed to remove a series of non-tariff barriers, including:

- Accepting **US-made vehicles** built to US safety and emissions standards.
- Accepting **US FDA certificates** and prior marketing authorisations for medical devices and pharmaceuticals.
- Recognising the **US food-safety system** for meat, poultry and dairy, opening market access for US sorghum, and adopting regionalisation for pork and poultry.
- **Not banning or imposing quotas** on exports of critical minerals or rare-earth elements to the United States.

The agreement also recorded a Malaysian commitment to facilitate **approximately US$70 billion in job-creating investment** in the United States over ten years, and flagged separate commercial deals in aerospace, energy, telecommunications and semiconductors.

Crucially, the agreement **never entered into force**. Under its Article 7.2 it required both governments to exchange written notices confirming completion of their internal procedures, after which it would take effect within 60 days. As of 5 February 2026, Malaysia's trade ministry (MITI) confirmed those notices had **not** been exchanged and that Malaysia was still reviewing the deal. Events then overtook it: on **20 February 2026** the US Supreme Court ruled (6-3) that the reciprocal tariffs imposed under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) were unlawful; those tariffs terminated on **24 February 2026** and the underlying tariff orders were revoked. On **15 March 2026**, Trade Minister Johari Abdul Ghani declared the agreement **"null and void"** — "It is not on hold. It is no longer there, it's null and void" — because the 19 percent reciprocal-tariff structure it rested on no longer existed. In the interim the US applied a temporary **10 percent tariff under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974** (capped at 150 days), and negotiations continued.

## Is Malaysia a US security ally?

No — and Kuala Lumpur is careful to keep it that way. Malaysia has no mutual-defence treaty with Washington. The **Defence Cooperation MoU** signed on 30 October 2025 had been under negotiation since 2020 and is, in the words of Defence Minister Mohamed Khaled Nordin, **legally non-binding**. It covers capability and capacity development, military training and exercises, and defence science, technology and industry, pursued through the Malaysia-US Strategic Talk (MUSST) that has run since 2008.

Malaysia frames such arrangements as consistent with its **neutral, non-aligned foreign policy** and its commitment to the 1971 Zone of Peace, Freedom and Neutrality (ZOPFAN) declaration. It maintains defence-cooperation arrangements with a range of partners: it signed a bilateral defence-cooperation MoU with **China** in 2016 (subsequently renewed) and a defence-cooperation MoU with **Japan** in 2018, with Japan-Malaysia ties further deepened in June 2026. This hedging posture lets Malaysia cooperate substantively with the US while preserving strategic autonomy and its relationship with Beijing, its single largest trading partner.

## What are the friction points?

Three recurring ones. First, the **trade deficit and tariffs**: the reciprocal deal that would have set a 19 percent baseline duty on most Malaysian exports collapsed in early 2026, leaving the tariff relationship unsettled and subject to renewed negotiation, and Malaysian officials have stressed the trade and defence tracks are separate to avoid the appearance of linkage. Second, **strategic hedging**: Malaysia's determination not to be drawn into a US-China contest means it declines alliance commitments, which can frustrate Washington. Third, **values and domestic politics**: human-rights, Gaza and religious-freedom issues periodically surface in the relationship, though they rarely derail the commercial core.

## What's next

With the reciprocal trade agreement declared void and the IEEPA tariffs struck down, watch how the two governments **renegotiate the tariff relationship** while the temporary Section 122 duty runs its course. Track how the **US$70 billion investment commitment** and the semiconductor and aerospace "commercial deals" translate into announced projects, given the framework they sat within has lapsed. And note whether the deepening economic and defence ties shift Malaysia's careful balance between Washington and Beijing, or simply confirm its long-running strategy of being close to both without belonging to either.
