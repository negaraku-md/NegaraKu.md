On 8 August 1967, five men gathered in the main hall of the Ministry of Foreign
Affairs building in Bangkok and signed a two-page document. One of them,
Malaysia's Deputy Prime Minister Tun Abdul Razak Hussein, was placing Malaysia
within an organisation that would go on to become the central framework of this
country's foreign policy for nearly six decades — and one that Malaysia itself
would chair in 2025.

## At a glance: Malaysia in ASEAN

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Full name | Association of Southeast Asian Nations |
| Established | 8 August 1967, Bangkok, Thailand |
| Founding instrument | ASEAN Declaration (Bangkok Declaration) |
| Malaysian signatory | Tun Abdul Razak Hussein (then Deputy Prime Minister) |
| Current number of members | 11 countries |

| Structure | Three pillars of the ASEAN Community (since 31 December 2015) |
| Secretariat | Jakarta, Indonesia |
| Malaysia's chairmanships | 5 times — 1977, 1997, 2005, 2015, 2025 |

Malaysia is not merely a member of ASEAN. It is one of the five countries that
first built the organisation, and ASEAN's nearly six-decade history is closely
intertwined with the history of Malaysia's foreign policy.

## How did ASEAN begin, and what was Malaysia's role?

ASEAN was born out of the geopolitical anxieties of Southeast Asia in the 1960s
— regional confrontation, Cold War tensions, and the need for newly
independent states to manage their relations without great-power interference.
The solution was a forum for regional cooperation, not a military alliance.

The Bangkok Declaration was signed by five ministers: Adam Malik (Indonesia),
Tun Abdul Razak Hussein (Malaysia), Narciso R. Ramos (the Philippines),
S. Rajaratnam (Singapore) and Thanat Khoman (Thailand). All five later became
known as the Founding Fathers of ASEAN. For Malaysia, this participation
established a principle that endures to this day: priority given to regional
solutions, non-interference in the internal affairs of neighbouring countries,
and decision-making by consensus.

The Declaration set the goals of accelerating economic growth, social progress
and cultural development in the region, as well as promoting regional peace and
stability. It was deliberately brief and non-binding — a feature known as "the
ASEAN Way".

The accession of Timor-Leste is ASEAN's first expansion since 1999. It was
finalised during Malaysia's chairmanship: the decision to admit Timor-Leste as
the 11th member was made at the 46th ASEAN Summit in May 2025, and the
Declaration on the Admission of Timor-Leste was signed at the Opening Ceremony
of the 47th ASEAN Summit in Kuala Lumpur on 26 October 2025.

## What is the ASEAN Charter and why does it matter?

For its first 40 years, ASEAN operated without a formal constitution. That
changed with the ASEAN Charter.

The Charter was signed by the leaders of the 10 member states at the 13th ASEAN
Summit in Singapore on 20 November 2007, and entered into force on 15 December
2008 after being ratified by all member states. For the first time, the Charter
gave ASEAN **legal personality** as an intergovernmental organisation — meaning
ASEAN now became a legal entity in its own right, not just a loose grouping of
countries.

What the Charter does:

- Makes ASEAN's goals, principles and values a legally binding document.
- Preserves core principles such as non-interference, sovereign equality, and
  decision-making through consultation and consensus.
- Establishes the institutional structure — the ASEAN Summit, the Coordinating
  Council, the Community Councils, and the Secretariat.
- Lays the foundation for the formation of the ASEAN Community.

For Malaysia, the Charter further strengthened the framework that allows it to
bring regional issues — from the South China Sea disputes to the Myanmar crisis
— to a formal table rather than through ad hoc channels.

## What are the three pillars of the ASEAN Community?

On 31 December 2015, ASEAN formally established the **ASEAN Community**,
organised around three pillars. Each pillar has its own blueprint.

| Pillar | Focus | What it means for Malaysia |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ASEAN Political-Security Community (APSC) | Peace, stability, dispute settlement, regional norms | A forum for security issues such as the South China Sea and Myanmar |
| ASEAN Economic Community (AEC) | A single market and production base — free flow of goods, services, investment, capital and skills | Access to a regional market of more than 600 million people |
| ASEAN Socio-Cultural Community (ASCC) | Education, health, environment, culture, disaster response | People-to-people cooperation and social protection |

The economic pillar (AEC) is arguably the most tangible in its impact on
Malaysian businesses. The concept is to replace members' separate economies
with a single market and a shared production base, enabling companies to build
industrial networks across the region and join global supply chains. According
to the Singapore government's economic education portal (SG101), the AEC opens
access to around 600 million consumers in the region. The AEC also serves as
the foundation for broader trade agreements such as the RCEP (Regional
Comprehensive Economic Partnership), which integrates ASEAN with partners such
as China, Japan, South Korea, Australia and New Zealand.

## ASEAN's key bodies: who makes the decisions?

ASEAN is often misunderstood as a centralised bureaucracy. In reality, it is a
member-driven organisation, with a rotating chairmanship.

**The ASEAN Summit** is the highest policy-making body, bringing together heads
of state or heads of government. It usually meets twice a year in the country
holding the chairmanship.

**The ASEAN Secretariat** is based in Jakarta, Indonesia, and was established
through the 1976 Agreement on the Establishment of the ASEAN Secretariat. It
coordinates and supports the implementation of ASEAN decisions, but has no
coercive power over member states.

**The Secretary-General of ASEAN** heads the Secretariat. The current
office-holder is Dr. Kao Kim Hourn, the 15th Secretary-General, who took office
in January 2023 and is the first Cambodian national to hold the position.

**The ASEAN chairmanship** rotates each year in the alphabetical order of
member states' names in English. The chairing country hosts the summits, sets
the theme and priorities for the year, and chairs the main meetings. This is
the mechanism that placed Malaysia in the driver's seat in 2025.

## Malaysia's role as ASEAN Chair 2025

Malaysia held the ASEAN chairmanship for the **fifth time** in 2025 — after
1977, 1997, 2005 and 2015. The mantle was received from Laos at the close of
the 44th and 45th ASEAN Summits in Vientiane in October 2024, and the
chairmanship began officially on 1 January 2025. Prime Minister Anwar Ibrahim
led the bloc throughout that year.

The theme was **"Inclusivity and Sustainability"**. The choice of theme was
meaningful: 2025 marked the 10th anniversary of the establishment of the ASEAN
Community, a natural point at which to set the direction for the next two
decades. According to the Ministry of Finance Malaysia and Bernama, Malaysia
hosted more than 300 meetings and programmes across the country throughout the
year (the exact figure needs to be confirmed with official sources).

Among the key developments during Malaysia's 2025 chairmanship, as reported by
official sources and news agencies, were:

- **The accession of Timor-Leste as the 11th member**, finalised at the 47th
  Summit in Kuala Lumpur in October 2025 — ASEAN's first expansion since 1999.
- **The adoption of the ASEAN Community Vision 2045**, themed "Resilient,
  Innovative, Dynamic, and People-Centred ASEAN", adopted at the 46th ASEAN
  Summit in Kuala Lumpur on 26 May 2025, along with the Kuala Lumpur
  Declaration on ASEAN 2045. This Vision charts ASEAN's direction for the next
  20 years.
- **Cambodia–Thailand border peace efforts** involving Malaysia as a mediator
  (the official title and scope of the instrument need to be confirmed).
- The launch of new platforms such as the **ASEAN–GCC** and **ASEAN–GCC–China**
  meetings, expanding the region's diplomatic reach.

Malaysia chaired ASEAN throughout the 2025 calendar year. At the Closing
Ceremony of the 47th ASEAN Summit on 28 October 2025, the chairmanship gavel
was handed to the Philippines; however, the Philippines only formally holds the
chairmanship from 1 January 2026.

## A framework for understanding ASEAN correctly

ASEAN is complex because it is deliberately built loose. To read any ASEAN
issue accurately, ask these three questions in order:

1. **Which pillar?** Security issues (South China Sea, Myanmar) fall under the
   APSC; trade and investment issues under the AEC; disaster, education and
   cultural issues under the ASCC. The pillar determines which meetings and
   officials are involved.
2. **Which body?** Major policy decisions come from the Summit; day-to-day
   coordination from the Secretariat. Do not mistake a Secretariat statement
   for a decision binding on member states.
3. **Is it binding?** Most ASEAN outputs are declarations and action plans, not
   enforceable laws. The Charter is binding; most summit statements are
   political commitments.

## Common misconceptions about Malaysia and ASEAN

- **Thinking Malaysia "joined" ASEAN.** Malaysia founded ASEAN in 1967; it did
  not join later.
- **Assuming ASEAN has 10 members.** That figure was correct up to October
  2025. Since Timor-Leste's accession on 26 October 2025, ASEAN has 11 members.
- **Confusing the Bangkok Declaration with the ASEAN Charter.** The Bangkok
  Declaration (1967) founded ASEAN and is non-binding; the ASEAN Charter (in
  force 2008) is its statutory document.
- **Equating ASEAN with the European Union.** ASEAN does not share a currency,
  has no supranational parliament, and makes decisions by consensus, not by
  majority vote.
- **Thinking the chairmanship confers governing power.** The chairing country
  sets the agenda and hosts meetings, but cannot compel other member states.
  Decisions still require consensus.

## What's next

If you want to go deeper into a particular area, start with the pillar most
relevant to your needs — economic (the AEC and trade agreements such as RCEP),
security (the APSC), or socio-cultural (the ASCC). To verify any ASEAN fact,
consult the official ASEAN Secretariat portal at `asean.org`, and for
Malaysia's position, the Ministry of Foreign Affairs (Wisma Putra) website at
`kln.gov.my`. When reading news about summits, always check whether an outcome
is a political declaration or a truly binding instrument — that distinction
matters in understanding how ASEAN actually works.
