On 8 August 1967, five men gathered in the main hall of the Ministry of
Foreign Affairs building in Bangkok and signed a two-page document. One of
them, Malaysia's Deputy Prime Minister Tun Abdul Razak Hussein, was placing
Malaysia within an organisation that would go on to become the central
framework of the country's foreign policy for nearly six decades — and one
that Malaysia itself chaired in 2025.

## At a glance: Malaysia in ASEAN

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Full name | Association of Southeast Asian Nations (Persatuan Negara-Negara Asia Tenggara) |
| Established | 8 August 1967, Bangkok, Thailand |
| Founding instrument | ASEAN Declaration (Bangkok Declaration) |
| Malaysian signatory | Tun Abdul Razak Hussein (then Deputy Prime Minister) |
| Current number of members | 11 countries |
| Statutory document | ASEAN Charter (entered into force 15 December 2008) |
| Structure | Three pillars of the ASEAN Community (since 31 December 2015) |
| Secretariat | Jakarta, Indonesia |
| Malaysia's chairmanships | 5 times — 1977, 1997, 2005, 2015, 2025 |

Malaysia is not merely an ASEAN member. It is one of the five countries that
first built the organisation, and ASEAN's nearly six-decade history is closely
intertwined with the history of Malaysia's foreign policy.

## How did ASEAN begin, and what was Malaysia's role?

ASEAN was born out of the geopolitical unease of Southeast Asia in the 1960s —
regional confrontation, Cold War tensions, and the need for newly independent
states to manage their relations without interference from major powers. The
solution was a forum for regional cooperation, not a military alliance.

The Bangkok Declaration was signed by five ministers: Adam Malik (Indonesia),
Tun Abdul Razak Hussein (Malaysia), Narciso R. Ramos (the Philippines),
S. Rajaratnam (Singapore) and Thanat Khoman (Thailand). The five later came to
be known as the Founding Fathers of ASEAN. For Malaysia, this involvement
established a principle that endures to this day: priority is given to regional
solutions, non-interference in the internal affairs of neighbouring countries,
and decision-making by consensus (muafakat).

The Declaration set out the goals of accelerating economic growth, social
progress and cultural development in the region, and of promoting regional
peace and stability. It was deliberately brief and non-binding — a
characteristic known as "the ASEAN Way".

## Who are ASEAN's members today?

From five countries in 1967, ASEAN grew to eleven. Below is each member and
its date of accession:

| Member state | Date of joining |
| --- | --- |
| Indonesia | 8 August 1967 (founder) |
| Malaysia | 8 August 1967 (founder) |
| Philippines | 8 August 1967 (founder) |
| Singapore | 8 August 1967 (founder) |
| Thailand | 8 August 1967 (founder) |
| Brunei Darussalam | 7 January 1984 |
| Vietnam | 28 July 1995 |
| Laos (Lao PDR) | 23 July 1997 |
| Myanmar | 23 July 1997 |
| Cambodia | 30 April 1999 |
| Timor-Leste | 26 October 2025 |

Timor-Leste's admission was ASEAN's first expansion since 1999. It was
finalised during Malaysia's chairmanship: the decision to admit Timor-Leste as
the 11th member was made at the 46th ASEAN Summit in May 2025, and the
Declaration on the Admission of Timor-Leste was signed at the Opening Ceremony
of the 47th ASEAN Summit in Kuala Lumpur on 26 October 2025.

## What is the ASEAN Charter and why does it matter?

For its first 40 years, ASEAN operated without a formal constitution. That
changed with the ASEAN Charter.

The Charter was signed by the leaders of the 10 member states at the 13th
ASEAN Summit in Singapore on 20 November 2007, and entered into force on
15 December 2008 after ratification by all member states. For the first time,
the Charter gave ASEAN **legal personality** as an intergovernmental
organisation — meaning ASEAN now became a legal entity in its own right,
rather than a loose grouping of countries.

What the Charter does:

- Makes ASEAN's goals, principles and values a legally binding document.
- Preserves core principles such as non-interference, sovereign equality, and
  decision-making through consultation and consensus.
- Sets out the institutional structure — the ASEAN Summit, the Coordinating
  Council, the Community Councils, and the Secretariat.
- Lays the foundation for the formation of the ASEAN Community.

For Malaysia, the Charter further strengthened the framework that allows it to
bring regional issues — from South China Sea disputes to the Myanmar crisis —
to a formal table rather than through ad hoc channels.

## What are the three pillars of the ASEAN Community?

On 31 December 2015, ASEAN formally established the **ASEAN Community**,
organised into three pillars. Each pillar has its own blueprint.

| Pillar | Focus | What it means for Malaysia |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ASEAN Political-Security Community (APSC) | Peace, stability, dispute resolution, regional norms | A forum for security issues such as the South China Sea and Myanmar |
| ASEAN Economic Community (AEC) | A single market and production base — free flow of goods, services, investment, capital and skills | Access to a regional market of more than 600 million people |
| ASEAN Socio-Cultural Community (ASCC) | Education, health, environment, culture, disaster response | People-to-people cooperation and social protection |

The economic pillar (AEC) is arguably the most tangible in its impact on
Malaysian business. The concept is to replace members' separate economies with
a single market and shared production base, enabling companies to build
cross-regional industrial networks and join global supply chains. According to
the Singapore government's economic education portal (SG101), the AEC opens up
access to around 600 million consumers in the region. The AEC also serves as
the foundation for broader trade agreements such as the RCEP (Regional
Comprehensive Economic Partnership), which integrates ASEAN with partners such
as China, Japan, South Korea, Australia and New Zealand.

## ASEAN's key bodies: who makes the decisions?

ASEAN is often misunderstood as a centralised bureaucracy. In fact, it is an
organisation driven by its member states, with a rotating chairmanship.

The **ASEAN Summit** is the highest policy-making body, bringing together heads
of state or heads of government. It usually meets twice a year in the country
holding the chairmanship.

The **ASEAN Secretariat** is based in Jakarta, Indonesia, and was established
through the 1976 Agreement on the Establishment of the ASEAN Secretariat. It
coordinates and supports the implementation of ASEAN decisions, but has no
coercive power over member states.

The **Secretary-General of ASEAN** heads the Secretariat. The current holder is
Dr. Kao Kim Hourn, the 15th Secretary-General, who took office in January 2023
and is the first Cambodian national to hold the post.

The **ASEAN Chairmanship** rotates each year in the alphabetical order of
member-state names in English. The chairing country hosts the summits, sets the
year's theme and priorities, and chairs the key meetings. This is the mechanism
that placed Malaysia in the driver's seat in 2025.

## Malaysia's role as ASEAN Chair 2025

Malaysia held the ASEAN chairmanship for the **fifth time** in 2025 — after
1977, 1997, 2005 and 2015. The reins were received from Laos at the close of
the 44th and 45th ASEAN Summits in Vientiane in October 2024, and the
chairmanship began officially on 1 January 2025. Prime Minister Anwar Ibrahim
led the bloc throughout that year.

Its theme was **"Inclusivity and Sustainability"** (Keterangkuman dan
Kemampanan). The choice of theme was meaningful: 2025 marked the 10th
anniversary of the establishment of the ASEAN Community, a natural point to set
the direction for the next two decades. According to the Ministry of Finance
Malaysia, Malaysia hosted more than 300 major meetings and programmes across
the country throughout the year; Bernama reported a final total of around 320
ASEAN meetings across all three pillars by year's end.

Among the key developments during Malaysia's 2025 chairmanship, as reported by
official sources and news agencies, were:

- **The admission of Timor-Leste as the 11th member**, finalised at the 47th
  Summit in Kuala Lumpur in October 2025 — ASEAN's first expansion since 1999.
- **The adoption of the ASEAN Community Vision 2045**, themed "Resilient,
  Innovative, Dynamic, and People-Centred ASEAN", adopted at the 46th ASEAN
  Summit in Kuala Lumpur on 26 May 2025, together with the Kuala Lumpur
  Declaration on ASEAN 2045. This Vision maps ASEAN's direction for the next 20
  years. Malaysia's Foreign Minister Datuk Seri Mohamad Hasan described Malaysia
  as the "permanent shepherd" of this Vision in a statement in the Dewan Rakyat
  (6 February 2025) — a descriptive government term for Malaysia's role, not a
  formal statutory office.
- **The Kuala Lumpur Peace Accord** between Cambodia and Thailand, signed on
  26 October 2025 in Kuala Lumpur alongside the 47th ASEAN Summit. Malaysia, as
  ASEAN Chair 2025 under PM Anwar Ibrahim, hosted and mediated the signing
  (co-witnessed by US President Donald Trump); the accord established a border
  observer team and led to the release of 18 detained Cambodian soldiers.
- The hosting of new platforms — the **2nd ASEAN–GCC Summit** and the inaugural
  **ASEAN–GCC–China** Summit, both held in Kuala Lumpur on 27 May 2025 alongside
  the 46th ASEAN Summit — expanding the region's diplomatic reach.

Malaysia chaired ASEAN throughout the 2025 calendar year. At the Closing
Ceremony of the 47th ASEAN Summit on 28 October 2025, the chairmanship gavel
was handed over to the Philippines; however, the Philippines only formally
holds the chairmanship from 1 January 2026.

## A framework for understanding ASEAN correctly

ASEAN is complex because it is deliberately built loosely. To read any ASEAN
issue accurately, ask these three questions in order:

1. **Which pillar?** Security issues (the South China Sea, Myanmar) fall under
   the APSC; trade and investment issues under the AEC; disaster, education and
   cultural issues under the ASCC. The pillar determines which meetings and
   officials are involved.
2. **Which body?** Major policy decisions come from the Summit; day-to-day
   coordination from the Secretariat. Do not mistake a Secretariat statement for
   a decision binding on member states.
3. **Is it binding?** Most ASEAN outcomes are declarations and action plans, not
   enforceable law. The Charter is binding; most summit statements are political
   commitments.

## Common misconceptions about Malaysia and ASEAN

- **Thinking Malaysia "joined" ASEAN.** Malaysia founded ASEAN in 1967; it did
  not join later.
- **Assuming ASEAN has 10 members.** That figure was correct until October
  2025. Since Timor-Leste's admission on 26 October 2025, ASEAN has 11 members.
- **Confusing the Bangkok Declaration with the ASEAN Charter.** The Bangkok
  Declaration (1967) founded ASEAN and is non-binding; the ASEAN Charter (in
  force 2008) is its statutory document.
- **Equating ASEAN with the European Union.** ASEAN does not share a currency,
  has no supranational parliament, and makes decisions by consensus, not by
  majority vote.
- **Assuming the chairmanship confers ruling power.** The chairing country sets
  the agenda and hosts the meetings, but cannot compel other member states.
  Decisions still require consensus.

## What's next

If you want to go deeper into a particular area, start with the pillar most
relevant to your needs — economic (the AEC and trade agreements such as RCEP),
security (the APSC), or socio-cultural (the ASCC). To verify any ASEAN fact,
refer to the official ASEAN Secretariat portal at `asean.org`, and for
Malaysia's position, the Ministry of Foreign Affairs (Wisma Putra) website at
`kln.gov.my`. When reading news about summits, always check whether an outcome
is a political declaration or a genuinely binding instrument — that distinction
matters in understanding how ASEAN actually works.
