When the United States is the largest foreign investor in Malaysia's economy, but China is its largest trading partner, a small and open country cannot afford to take sides. That is why Malaysia's foreign policy has, for decades, clung to one word that is easy to say but hard to keep: neutrality. The ministry tasked with maintaining that balance day after day is Wisma Putra.

## Who actually formulates Malaysia's foreign policy?

Many assume "foreign policy" is the work of a single ministry. In truth it is layered.

The Ministry of Foreign Affairs — better known as **Wisma Putra**, after its building — is mandated to *carry out* and coordinate Malaysia's foreign policy under the Federal Constitution, with the aim of protecting and advancing the country's interests on the international stage. Wisma Putra manages Malaysia's network of embassies, high commissions and consulates around the world, negotiates the country's positions in international forums, and looks after the welfare of Malaysians abroad.

But the highest-level policy decisions do not belong to that ministry alone. They rest with the **Prime Minister and the Cabinet**. The Prime Minister often acts as the country's diplomatic face on official visits and at summits, while the Foreign Minister heads Wisma Putra and represents Malaysia at most ministerial meetings. In Malaysia's history, there have been times when the posts of Prime Minister and Foreign Minister were held by the same person, which shows just how closely the two roles are linked.

The simplest way to understand it: the Cabinet sets *what* Malaysia's position is, and Wisma Putra manages *how* that position is delivered, negotiated and defended day by day.

## What are the core principles that guide Malaysia's foreign policy?

Malaysia's foreign policy is not a random reaction to world events. It clings to several principles that have stayed fairly stable across different governments.

Its main emphases can be summarised like this:

| Principle | What it means in practice |
| --- | --- |
| **Open economy** | Malaysia is a trade-based country; its external relations are built to protect market access, investment and supply chains |
| **Peace and stability** | Prioritising diplomatic solutions and supporting regional stability, especially in Southeast Asia |
| **Non-interference** | Respecting the sovereignty of other countries and not interfering in their internal affairs — and expecting the same treatment |
| **Independence and neutrality** | Taking an independent stance, not joining any great-power military bloc, while rejecting injustice, aggression and violations of sovereignty |

These principles reinforce one another. Because Malaysia depends on trade with many parties at once, siding with one great power means putting the relationship with the other at stake. So neutrality is not merely a moral position — it is also economic logic.

According to the direction set out by Wisma Putra, Malaysia's foreign-policy approach is grounded in the country's interests, values and priorities, in line with the principles and objectives of the Federal Constitution, the ASEAN Charter and the Charter of the United Nations. It emphasises a commitment to bilateral, regional and multilateral engagement through **principled and pragmatic** diplomacy.

## Where did Wisma Putra begin?

To understand why neutrality is so deeply rooted, it helps to look at the origins of the institution that guards it.

According to the Ministry of Foreign Affairs' own historical account, the foundations of its establishment date back to **1956**, a year before independence, when it was known as the *Ministry of External Affairs*. A pioneering group of **11 career diplomats** was sent to Britain and Australia for training — a small step that built the core of this young nation's diplomatic service.

In **1966**, the ministry was renamed the *Ministry of Foreign Affairs* and moved from the Sultan Abdul Samad Building to a new complex called **Wisma Putra** in Bukit Petaling, Kuala Lumpur. It was the building's name that later stuck as the ministry's common title, even though its headquarters is now in Putrajaya.

This institution's journey matters because it passed through early trials — including the Confrontation with Indonesia that greeted Malaysia's birth in 1963 — which taught that regional stability and good relations with neighbours are not a luxury but a necessity for survival.

## What does "neutrality" mean, and where does ZOPFAN come in?

Neutrality in Malaysia's context does not mean withdrawing or not caring. It means *not siding with a great-power bloc* while staying active and vocal.

This idea has an official history. On **27 November 1971**, five ASEAN countries — Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines, Singapore and Thailand — signed the **Declaration of the Zone of Peace, Freedom and Neutrality (ZOPFAN)** at a Special Meeting of ASEAN Foreign Ministers in Kuala Lumpur. Its aim was clear: to keep Southeast Asia free from any form of outside-power interference. The declaration was born in the shadow of the Vietnam War and the great-power rivalry of the Cold War era, and Malaysia was among its driving forces.

More than half a century later, ZOPFAN is still cited as the basis of Malaysia's stance. In July 2025, after the 58th ASEAN Foreign Ministers' Meeting in Kuala Lumpur, Foreign Minister **Mohamad Hasan** stressed that Malaysia's foreign policy remains balanced and resolute in upholding ZOPFAN. He explained the logic bluntly:

- Malaysia "maintains a good balance" because it is a trade-based country.
- The United States is the **largest investor** in Malaysia, while China is its **largest trading partner**.
- Therefore Malaysia "cannot be seen leaning toward any side".

The Foreign Minister also stressed that Malaysia's diplomacy must remain "principled and pragmatic" — two words that capture the country's entire approach: firm on values, but flexible on tactics.

## How did ASEAN become the cornerstone of foreign policy?

If neutrality is the principle, ASEAN is the main arena where that principle is practised.

Malaysia is one of ASEAN's founding countries, established in 1967. Ever since, ASEAN's integrity and *centrality* have been a foundation of the country's foreign policy. The logic: a medium-sized country like Malaysia has a far stronger voice when it acts together with ten neighbours than when it stands alone. ASEAN gives Malaysia a platform to manage relations with the great powers collectively, rather than facing pressure single-handedly.

But ASEAN is not the only arena. Malaysia's foreign policy is supported by active membership in several forums:

- **The United Nations (UN)** — where Malaysia voices its positions on global issues and contributes to peacekeeping missions.
- **The Organisation of Islamic Cooperation (OIC)** — a channel of solidarity with Muslim-majority nations and positions on issues such as Palestine.
- **The Non-Aligned Movement (NAM)** — a gathering of nations that, like Malaysia, choose not to be tied to a great-power bloc.
- **South-South Cooperation** — cooperation among developing countries based on mutual respect.

This combination of forums allows Malaysia to maintain harmonious relations with all countries *regardless of ideology and political system* — a long-standing emphasis in its foreign policy.

## What is the Ministry of Foreign Affairs Strategic Plan 2026-2030?

Stable principles still need an updated roadmap. The current roadmap is the **Ministry of Foreign Affairs Strategic Plan 2026-2030**, launched by Prime Minister **Anwar Ibrahim** on **23 February 2026** during the ministry's MADANI Iftar event.

This five-year plan succeeds the 2021-2025 Strategic Plan and is aligned with the Thirteenth Malaysia Plan (RMK13), the MADANI Economy Framework and the Public Service Reform Agenda (ARPA). Its framework is large: **8 strategic thrusts**, supported by **33 strategies** and **139 programmes** in finer detail, each with key performance indicators (KPIs) as well as a monitoring and evaluation framework.

The eight strategic thrusts are:

| # | Strategic thrust |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Strengthening bilateral diplomacy |
| 2 | Strengthening multilateral diplomacy |
| 3 | Malaysia's proactive role in ASEAN |
| 4 | Safeguarding the sovereignty and integrity of maritime territory |
| 5 | Delivering proactive and effective services to stakeholders |
| 6 | Strengthening strategic communication and innovative public diplomacy |
| 7 | Strengthening institutional excellence and human capital |
| 8 | Strengthening the mandate of the National Authority in implementing the Chemical Weapons Convention (CWC) |

Notice how these thrusts reflect old principles in a new form. ASEAN remains at the centre (thrust 3). Economic diplomacy runs across thrusts 1 and 2. Maritime sovereignty (thrust 4) addresses the reality of overlapping claims in areas such as the South China Sea without naming any party in a one-sided way. Thrusts 5 and 6 raise the practical matters closest to the people — consular services and the welfare of Malaysians abroad. In other words, the plan translates "principled and pragmatic" into a list of measurable tasks.

## How does Malaysia decide its stance on an issue?

No official formula has been published, but the pattern of Malaysia's foreign-policy decisions is fairly consistent. The following simple framework helps explain why Malaysia takes certain positions:

1. **Does it directly affect Malaysia's sovereignty or security?** If yes — for example a territorial claim — Malaysia will firmly defend its interests, usually through diplomatic channels and international law, not confrontation.
2. **Does it involve a choice between great powers?** If so, the default tendency is *neutrality* — maintaining relations with all sides and avoiding being seen to take sides.
3. **Is it an issue of universal principle — injustice, aggression, violation of sovereignty?** Here Malaysia usually speaks out on principle, even against great powers, and often in the collective voice of ASEAN, the OIC or the UN.
4. **Can it be managed collectively?** If so, Malaysia prefers to act through ASEAN or multilateral forums rather than alone, because a collective voice is stronger and less risky.

This framework explains something that seems contradictory: how Malaysia can be neutral between great powers yet outspoken on issues of principle. Both flow from the same logic — protecting the interests of an open country while staying loyal to its declared values.

## What are the most common misconceptions about Malaysia's foreign policy?

Several mistakes keep appearing in public conversation:

- **"Neutrality means Malaysia stands for nothing."** Wrong. Neutrality refers to not joining a great-power military bloc. Malaysia still takes firm positions on principle on many issues.
- **"Wisma Putra makes all foreign-policy decisions."** Not accurate. Wisma Putra carries out and coordinates; the highest policy decisions belong to the Prime Minister and the Cabinet.
- **"Malaysia's foreign policy changes every time the government changes."** Most of it stays the same. The open economy, ASEAN priority and neutrality persist across different administrations; what changes is more the emphasis and style, not the foundations.
- **"Wisma Putra only handles official functions and protocol."** It is far broader — from negotiating positions at the UN to rescuing and helping Malaysians stranded abroad.
- **"ZOPFAN is just a historical document."** It is still cited as the basis of the current stance, as the Foreign Minister himself stressed in 2025.

## Wisma Putra versus other ministries: who does what?

Foreign policy overlaps with several other ministries, and it is easy to get confused about jurisdiction. A quick guide:

| Area | Lead |
| --- | --- |
| Foreign policy, diplomacy, missions abroad, welfare of citizens abroad | **Ministry of Foreign Affairs (Wisma Putra)** |
| Trade and investment policy, negotiating free-trade agreements | **MITI** (Ministry of Investment, Trade and Industry) |
| Promoting the exports of Malaysian companies to foreign markets | **MATRADE** |
| The highest-level foreign-policy decisions | **The Prime Minister and the Cabinet** |

The most useful distinction to remember: Wisma Putra looks after *country-to-country relations* and diplomatic positioning, while MITI and its agencies look after *trade and investment transactions*. On many official visits, the two move in tandem — one negotiating the relationship, the other sealing the economic agreement.

## Next steps

To go deeper on this topic, a few things are worth tracking separately. First, the full text and implementation of the **2026-2030 Strategic Plan** — how its 139 programmes are measured year on year — will be the real test of whether the "principled and pragmatic" rhetoric turns into results. Second, the issue of **maritime sovereignty** (the plan's fourth thrust) deserves its own explanation, as it is the most sensitive area in regional relations. Third, Malaysia's role in **ASEAN**, including its turn at the chairmanship, gives important context to how the country acts collectively.

In the meantime, the most useful working method is simple: when you read news about Malaysia's stance in an international crisis, ask two questions — is this a matter of sovereignty, a choice between great powers, or a universal principle; and is Malaysia speaking alone or through ASEAN? Those two questions explain almost every decision that comes out of Wisma Putra.
