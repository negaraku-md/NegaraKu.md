These two countries share a common language, religion, and origin — but they also share the longest land border in Borneo, oil deposits beneath the Sulawesi Sea, and millions of workers who cross over in search of a living. Malaysia–Indonesia relations are no ordinary diplomatic affair; they are a complicated family relationship, in which the very closeness is at once a source of cooperation and a source of tension.

The word *serumpun* — from the same root or clan — captures the spirit of this relationship. But beneath the rhetoric of brotherhood lies a very practical bilateral agenda: who looks after Indonesian workers on the estates and in the homes of Malaysians, where the boundary line actually lies, and who has the right to drill for oil in the overlapping areas. This guide lays out all three agendas with figures and dates that can be checked.

## What unites and divides two nations of shared roots?

The Malaysia–Indonesia bond is built on a shared heritage of the Malay/Indonesian language, Islam, and the history of the Nusantara. This bond has also been tested by a bitter history — most notably *Konfrontasi* in the mid-1960s, when Indonesia opposed the formation of the Federation of Malaysia — before relations were restored and grew into one of the closest bilateral pairings in ASEAN.

Today, three flows connect the two countries every day:

- **The flow of people** — Indonesian workers form the largest group of foreign workers in Malaysia.
- **The flow of goods** — bilateral trade worth billions of ringgit crossing the Strait of Malacca and the Borneo border.
- **The flow of boundary issues** — land and maritime lines inherited from the treaties of the British and Dutch colonial era still awaiting technical resolution.

Each of these flows brings benefits along with friction. The sections that follow dissect each one.

## How are migrant worker (TKI) matters managed?

Indonesian nationals — known in their home country as *Pekerja Migran Indonesia* (PMI), or formerly TKI — have long been the backbone of the workforce on Malaysia's oil palm estates, construction sites, and households. The scale is large: an estimated **2.7 million Indonesian workers** are in Malaysia, but only about **one-third** of them are legally registered, according to Indonesian embassy estimates. It is this gap — unregistered workers exposed to smuggling and abuse — that is the source of recurring tension.

The turning point came on **1 April 2022**, when Malaysia and Indonesia signed a Memorandum of Understanding (MoU) on the placement and protection of Indonesian domestic workers. It was signed at Istana Merdeka, Jakarta, by Indonesia's Manpower Minister Ida Fauziyah and Malaysia's Human Resources Minister Datuk Seri M. Saravanan, witnessed by President Joko Widodo and Prime Minister Ismail Sabri Yaakob. The MoU restored a formal protection framework after the previous MoU was reported to have expired in 2016.

### The One Channel System

The central pillar of the 2022 MoU is the **One Channel System**. It integrates the digital platforms of the two countries — Indonesia's **SIAPKerja** application with Malaysia's **Centralised Foreign Workers Management System (FWCMS)** — so that the entire process of placing, monitoring, and repatriating workers runs through one official channel.

Among its key objectives:

- Channel the entire recruitment process for domestic workers through a single official channel, replacing off-channel recruitment that is difficult to monitor.
- Set a **minimum wage of RM1,500 a month** for Indonesian domestic helpers.
- Impose penalties on employers and agents who breach employment contracts.
- Curb illegal visa conversions and the smuggling of workers.

The implementation of this single-channel principle briefly sparked a dispute in mid-2022. Indonesia claimed that Malaysia's continued use of the **Maid Online System (MOS)** undermined the single-channel principle, and consequently temporarily froze the sending of workers from 13 July 2022; Malaysia, for its part, took the position that the MoU did not require the abolition of existing systems. The issue showed that the unification of the recruitment channel remained subject to interpretation by both sides.

The RM1,500 wage protection was reported to apply upon contract renewal as well, not only for new hires — making this MoU an important reference point for domestic worker standards in the region.

## Where do land and maritime boundaries remain unresolved?

The Malaysia–Indonesia boundary in Borneo is inherited from colonial-era treaties — chiefly the 1891 Boundary Convention and the 1915 Boundary Agreement between the British and the Dutch. Problems arise because the old mapping was incomplete and geographical features such as rivers shift over time. Areas not yet formally agreed are known as **Outstanding Boundary Problems (OBP)**, and joint technical committees have been working to resolve them since the 1970s.

Major progress has been made recently. After **more than 45 years** of negotiation, the OBP on the land boundary in the **Eastern sector (North Kalimantan–Sabah)** has been fully agreed. At the **45th Joint Committee** meeting held on **17–18 February 2025**, the two countries signed an MoU on the *Demarcation and Survey of the International Boundary* to confirm that line.

Not everything is settled, however. Issues still under negotiation include:

- **The tidal zone** off the east coast of Sebatik Island — the segment where the two countries' land meets on an island that has been split in two since colonial times.
- **The gap in the Sulawesi Sea** — the area from the low-water line up to a reference point (known as Point M) that has not yet been agreed.
- **The Western sector OBP** (Sarawak–West Kalimantan), which has been handed to the Joint Boundary Committee to continue.

### The lesson of Sipadan and Ligitan

An important backdrop to the maritime negotiations is the case of **Sipadan and Ligitan Islands**. When the two countries failed to agree, the dispute was referred to the **International Court of Justice (ICJ)**. On **17 December 2002**, the ICJ ruled that both islands belong to **Malaysia**, on the basis of the principle of *effectivités* — evidence of effective administration such as the 1917 Turtle Preservation Ordinance and the maintenance of lighthouses in the 1960s, without objection from the Dutch or Indonesian side at the time.

That ruling resolved the status of the two islands but **did not fix the maritime boundary** in the nearby Ambalat area — leaving the larger dispute open.

## How is Ambalat being resolved without waiting for the boundary?

The **Ambalat** block in the Sulawesi Sea, near the maritime boundary east of Kalimantan, is among the most sensitive issues. It stems from Malaysia's **1979** claim map, which Indonesia rejected. What makes it important: this block, covering roughly **15,235 square kilometres**, is believed to contain **up to 1 billion barrels of oil and 40 trillion cubic feet of gas**, with projected output of around 40,000 barrels a day for several decades.

Recognising that a legal boundary settlement could take decades, both leaders opted for a pragmatic approach. On **27 June 2025** at Istana Merdeka, Jakarta, Prime Minister Anwar Ibrahim and President Prabowo Subianto agreed to **develop Ambalat jointly** (*joint development*) while the boundary aspects continue to be negotiated.

> "If we wait for everything to be finalised, it could take decades," said Anwar, justifying the decision to begin joint development immediately.

This model mirrors the existing Petronas–Pertamina cooperation, such as at the Masela gas block. It allows the two countries to exploit the resource without first having to determine who owns the boundary line — a clever separation between the question of sovereignty and the question of economics.

## What came out of the 13th Annual Consultation?

The main mechanism driving the bilateral agenda is the **Annual Consultation** between the heads of government of the two countries. The 13th session was held in **Jakarta on 29 July 2025**, with a joint statement issued by the Ministry of Foreign Affairs (Wisma Putra) dated the same day.

Among the key outcomes of the meeting:

- **Accelerating boundary negotiations** on land and sea, with the Joint Boundary Committee tasked to continue work in the Western sector.
- **Enhancing cross-border trade**, including the opening of the ICQS (Immigration, Customs, Quarantine, Security) Complex at Serikin, Sarawak, and encouraging trade in rice and maize.
- **Reaffirming** compliance with the 2012 fisheries MoU that governs fishermen's activities in the border area.
- **Emphasising the welfare of Indonesian migrant workers** as a continuing priority.

The consultation also placed the bilateral relationship in the **ASEAN** context: both leaders stressed the importance of ASEAN centrality and resilience in an increasingly uncertain international environment.

## How do trade and the economy bind the two countries?

Beyond boundary politics, the economy is a stable adhesive. In 2024, Malaysia's total trade reached a record **RM2.9 trillion**. Within that figure, Indonesia remains **one of Malaysia's important trading partners in ASEAN**: Malaysia's exports to Indonesia rose by about **RM3.5 billion (6.9%)** compared with the previous year, driven largely by petroleum products.

Trade data show Malaysia exported goods worth around **RM54.5 billion** to Indonesia in 2024 (up roughly 7%), placing Indonesia **8th** on the list of Malaysia's top export destinations. Mineral fuels and refined oil lead, followed by machinery, plastics, and electrical equipment. Cooperation in the halal sector has also been identified as a driver of future growth.

## Employer's guide: hiring an Indonesian domestic worker

If you wish to hire an Indonesian domestic helper legally, this brief decision framework helps:

1. **Use the official channel only.** Recruitment must go through the One Channel System (SIAPKerja–FWCMS) and registered recruitment agencies. Avoid "direct" offers or unofficial platforms.
2. **Provide the RM1,500 minimum wage.** This is the standard under the 2022 MoU, including at contract renewal.
3. **Confirm a written contract.** A clear contract protects both parties and avoids penalties for breaching terms.
4. **Check documents and visas.** Ensure the worker enters on the correct visa; illegal visa conversion makes the employment invalid.
5. **Keep compliance records.** Wage payments, leave, and working conditions must be documented throughout the employment period.

### Common mistakes to avoid

- **Hiring workers outside the One Channel System** because it is cheaper or faster — this makes the employment invalid and exposes the employer to action.
- **Assuming all Indonesian workers are registered.** Given that only about one-third have legal status, document verification is mandatory.
- **Confusing the island settlement with the maritime settlement.** The 2002 ICJ ruling involved only Sipadan and Ligitan, not Ambalat.
- **Assuming the boundary is already "fully settled".** The Eastern sector land boundary is indeed agreed, but Sebatik, the Sulawesi Sea, and the Western sector are still under negotiation.
- **Viewing the joint development of Ambalat as a surrender of sovereignty.** It is a temporary economic model; the legal boundary question remains open for negotiation.

## What's next

Malaysia–Indonesia relations are moving towards making *serumpun* something practical, not mere rhetoric. A few things to watch in the years ahead:

- **The resolution of the Western sector OBP** (Sarawak–West Kalimantan) and the tidal segment of Sebatik Island — a test of whether the momentum of the Eastern sector can be repeated.
- **The implementation of joint development at Ambalat** — how the Petronas–Pertamina framework translates into actual production without undermining either side's sovereignty claim.
- **The effectiveness of the One Channel System** in reducing the number of unregistered workers and streamlining the recruitment channel.
- **The opening of cross-border infrastructure** such as ICQS Serikin and its impact on local trade.
