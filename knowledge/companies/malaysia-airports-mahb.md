Almost every time a traveller touches down in Malaysia, they arrive at an airport run by one company. Malaysia Airports Holdings Berhad (MAHB) operates Kuala Lumpur International Airport — now branded KLIA Terminal 1 and Terminal 2 — and, by its own count, 39 airports across the country, plus one overseas gateway in Istanbul. In 2025 it also became something unusual for a national airport operator: no longer a public company.

## Overview

MAHB is the dominant airport operator in Malaysia. Its core business, as set out in the company's corporate profile, is managing, operating, maintaining and developing airports, earning revenue from aeronautical charges (landing, parking and passenger service charges paid by airlines and passengers) and from commercial activities such as duty-free and retail concessions, food and beverage, advertising, car parking and property. The group is headquartered at Persiaran Korporat KLIA in Sepang, Selangor, next to its flagship airport.

Historically, MAHB was a government-linked company (GLC) majority-held by Khazanah Nasional, the government's strategic investment fund. That changed in 2025, when a Khazanah-led consortium took the company private (see below).

## History: corporatisation and the 1999 listing

MAHB's lineage begins with a structural reform of Malaysian aviation. In 1991 the government corporatised airport operations, separating them from the Department of Civil Aviation (DCA), which remained the regulator. The newly formed operating company — Malaysia Airports Berhad — was licensed to run, operate and maintain the country's airports in the early 1990s.

The holding company, Malaysia Airports Holdings Berhad, was incorporated and listed on the Main Board of the Kuala Lumpur Stock Exchange (now Bursa Malaysia) in 1999. The company describes itself as the first airport operator to list in Asia. For roughly 25 years thereafter MAHB traded publicly under the ticker 5014, with Khazanah as its anchor shareholder.

## Airports operated

MAHB's network spans the full range of Malaysian aviation, from a major international hub to small rural strips.

| Scope | What MAHB operates |
| --- | --- |
| Flagship | Kuala Lumpur International Airport (KLIA) — Terminal 1 (formerly KLIA) and Terminal 2 (formerly klia2) |
| Malaysia network | 39 airports per MAHB's corporate profile: 5 international, 17 domestic and 17 STOLports (short take-off and landing strips serving remote areas) |
| International | Istanbul Sabiha Gökçen International Airport (SAW), Turkey |

KLIA is the centrepiece. Its two terminals were rebranded in 2023: the original Main Terminal Building became **KLIA Terminal 1**, and the low-cost terminal klia2 (opened 2014) became **KLIA Terminal 2**. The then-Transport Minister framed the rename as a marketing and competitiveness move; the two terminals are linked by a shuttle service.

Beyond KLIA, MAHB runs Malaysia's other international gateways (such as those serving Penang, Kota Kinabalu, Kuching and Langkawi), a tier of domestic airports, and a set of STOLports that keep remote communities — particularly in Sabah and Sarawak — connected.

Abroad, MAHB operates Istanbul Sabiha Gökçen International Airport, Turkey's second-largest airport after Istanbul Airport, which it has held since the mid-2010s. Sabiha Gökçen is a large low-cost and point-to-point hub; MAHB reported it handled on the order of 41 million passengers in 2024, making it a material part of the group's traffic.

## The 2024–25 privatisation

In May 2024, a consortium moved to take MAHB private. The bidding vehicle, **Gateway Development Alliance (GDA)**, was led by the two existing Malaysian institutional shareholders — Khazanah Nasional and the Employees Provident Fund (EPF) — alongside two foreign investors: Global Infrastructure Partners (GIP, part of BlackRock) and the Abu Dhabi Investment Authority (ADIA).

GDA offered **RM11 per share** for the stock it did not already control. The deal drew public debate over foreign ownership of strategic infrastructure, with critics urging the government to reconsider; proponents argued it would let MAHB invest for the long term away from quarterly market pressure.

The transaction completed in early 2025. According to reporting at the time, GDA had acquired about **98.68% of MAHB's shares as at 12 February 2025**, crossing the threshold to compulsorily acquire the remaining shares from dissenting holders under Section 222 of the Capital Markets and Services Act 2007. Trading in MAHB shares was suspended on 20 February 2025, and the company was **delisted from Bursa Malaysia's Main Market on 25 February 2025** — ending roughly a quarter-century as a listed company.

On the reported post-deal ownership, Khazanah emerged as the largest shareholder, followed by EPF, with GIP and ADIA holding minority stakes and the government retaining a special share; precise percentages and the final valuation differ across reports and should be checked against official disclosures (see verificationNeeded). The upshot for readers is clear: MAHB is now a privately held company, majority-controlled by Malaysian government-linked institutions, with foreign co-investors.

## Scale and significance

MAHB is one of Malaysia's most strategically important companies. Airports are critical national infrastructure — gateways for tourism, trade and connectivity — and MAHB operates almost all of them. For the full year 2024 (figures reported January 2025), MAHB said its airports handled roughly **135 million passenger movements** group-wide, about 96.1% of pre-pandemic 2019 levels, with KLIA alone handling in the region of 57 million passengers. These figures are time-varying and should be re-verified against the company's traffic snapshots and annual report.

Because MAHB sits at the intersection of transport policy, tourism strategy and sovereign investment, its performance and governance attract close attention from government, airlines and the travelling public alike.

## Why it matters

For most people, MAHB is invisible until it isn't — it is the landlord, operator and first impression of the country for arriving visitors. Its near-monopoly over Malaysian airport operations means service quality, capacity planning and charges at MAHB airports shape the cost and experience of flying to and within Malaysia. The 2025 take-private also matters as a case study in how Malaysia manages strategic assets: keeping majority control in government-linked hands (Khazanah and EPF) while bringing in global infrastructure investors, and moving the operator off the public market to pursue long-horizon investment.

## What's next

- **Capacity and KLIA expansion.** Watch for MAHB's investment plans at KLIA and other airports now that it operates away from quarterly market scrutiny — terminal upgrades and capacity expansion to meet recovering and growing traffic.
- **Ownership and governance under private control.** Follow how the Khazanah-led consortium governs MAHB, the role of GIP and ADIA, and any regulatory conditions attached to foreign co-investment in strategic infrastructure.
- **Verification of deal details.** For exact shareholding percentages, the final acquisition level, the deal valuation and current traffic, consult MAHB's official announcements and annual report rather than relying on older press figures.
