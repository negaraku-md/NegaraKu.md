Malaysia runs a largely **tax-funded universal public healthcare system** managed by the **Ministry of Health (MOH, *Kementerian Kesihatan Malaysia*)**. It is organised in three tiers — primary care at government health clinics, secondary care at district and general hospitals, and tertiary care at specialist hospitals. For Malaysian citizens, fees are heavily subsidised under the **Fees Act 1951** and the **Fees (Medical) Order 1982**: **RM1** for an outpatient clinic visit and **RM5** for a specialist consultation. The public system operates alongside a large, mostly out-of-pocket private sector.

## How the system is structured

The MOH is both the main **funder** and the main **provider** of public healthcare, financed principally from general federal taxation rather than social health insurance. Care is delivered through three tiers:

| Tier | Facilities | Role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary | *Klinik Kesihatan* (health clinics), *Klinik Desa* (rural clinics), community clinics | First contact: outpatient care, maternal and child health, screening, chronic-disease follow-up |
| Secondary | District and general hospitals | Inpatient care, common specialties, emergency departments |
| Tertiary | Specialist and referral hospitals, special medical institutions | Complex and sub-specialty care (e.g. cancer, cardiology, respiratory medicine) |

As of **31 December 2023**, the MOH operated **149 hospitals and special medical institutions** with roughly **45,964 beds**, plus over **3,000 health clinics** nationwide, according to *MOH Health Facts 2024*. State health departments run services locally, while university teaching hospitals and military facilities add further public capacity.

## What it costs citizens

User charges are set by the **Fees (Medical) Order 1982 [PU(A) 359/1982]**, made under the Fees Act 1951. For Malaysian citizens the headline rates are **RM1 for an outpatient visit** and **RM5 for a specialist clinic visit**. Additional charges may apply for certain materials, implants, or specialised scans.

The Order also **exempts** several groups from these fees, including government servants, government pensioners and their families, persons with disabilities, and others whose medical privileges are provided for under written law. Non-citizens pay higher scheduled rates. Because subsidies cover the bulk of true costs, the public system carries most of the country's inpatient workload.

## How it is financed

Public healthcare is funded chiefly through the annual federal budget. **Budget 2025** allocated the MOH **RM45.27 billion** — about **10.7%** of the federal budget — split into roughly RM38.5 billion operating and RM6.7 billion development expenditure. Total national health spending was about **4.6% of GDP in 2023** (public and private combined).

A notable feature is **high out-of-pocket spending**: around **38.6% of current health expenditure** in 2023 (World Bank), reflecting heavy reliance on private clinics and hospitals for those who can pay. The 2023 **Health White Paper for Malaysia** sets out a long-term reform agenda to strengthen primary care, sustain financing, and reduce this out-of-pocket burden.

## Targeted programmes and digital access

Alongside universal subsidised care, the MOH runs targeted schemes:

- **PeKa B40** — free health screening (focused on non-communicable disease detection) for lower-income citizens aged **40 and above** who receive Sumbangan Tunai Rahmah (STR) cash aid, plus their spouses; administered by ProtectHealth.
- **MADANI Medical Scheme** — benefits for welfare recipients to treat minor ailments at panel clinics, operating across a set of pilot districts.
- **MySejahtera** — the national health app for vaccination records, appointment booking, and screening access; **MyUBAT** supports medicine refills via counter, postal, or drive-thru collection.
