On the corner of many an old town there is always a Chinese medicine shop: the wall of wooden cabinets is packed with its grid of tiny drawers, and the air is a mix of angelica root (当归), dried tangerine peel (陈皮) and liquorice (甘草); behind the counter, an old master listens to the customer describe where it hurts while weighing out the medicinal materials one by one into kraft paper, then folding them into a single packet. Shops like this have existed in Malaysia's Chinese communities for more than a hundred years.

TCM — Traditional Chinese Medicine — came south with the Chinese migration of the 19th century, taking root among the tin mines, the ports and the new villages. This article explains, in a descriptive and neutral way, the history of TCM in Malaysia, its main therapies, well-known firms, and the regulatory framework it operates within today. Any claims of efficacy in this article are attributed to tradition and to practitioners themselves; this article is cultural and public information, and is **not medical advice**.

## What "TCM" and the "Chinese Medicine Shop" Are

In everyday local usage, "TCM" (中医) usually refers to two things at once: a medical tradition originating in China (centred on observation, listening, questioning and pulse-taking, pattern differentiation, and bodily regulation), and the practitioners who provide this service (TCM practitioners). The "Chinese medicine shop" (中药行, also called 中药店) is the retail and dispensing outlet that accompanies it — selling medicinal herbs, Chinese patent medicines and health products, many also offering compounding and general consultation, and some with a registered TCM practitioner in attendance.

One point to keep clear: **a shop that sells medicinal materials** and **an individual's practice registration** are two different concepts. A Chinese medicine shop may operate as pure retail, or it may also offer TCM consultation; whereas a "TCM practitioner" as a practitioner is subject to a separate set of registration and regulatory rules (see below).

## History: The Chinese Medicine Shop That Came South with Migration

The large-scale migration of Chinese people to Malaya happened mainly from the 19th to the early 20th century, following tin mining and trade. Knowledge of Chinese medicine and Chinese herbs came south with these migrants: in the mining districts, the port towns and later the Chinese new villages, the Chinese medicine shop was often among the earliest Chinese establishments to appear — a place to buy medicine and also a place where neighbours sought advice, rested, and exchanged news of home.

The firm most often mentioned in this history is **Eu Yan Sang (余仁生)**. According to historical records, its founder Eu Kong (余广) opened his first Chinese medicine shop in 1879 in Gopeng (务边), Perak; at a time when miners relied heavily on opium to dull pain and fatigue, he offered Chinese medicine as an alternative for them instead. The business was later taken over by his son Eu Tong Sen (余东旋) and expanded into tin mining, rubber, finance and property, with branches across Malaya, Singapore, Hong Kong and southern China. The two characters "仁生" are often read as meaning "a benevolent heart that saves the world".

The native-place origins of practitioners also form part of this history: the Chinese herb and medicine trade has historically been closely tied to Chinese people of **Hakka and Cantonese** origin, and many old-established medicine shops and resident physicians came out of these kinship-and-place networks.

## Main Therapies (Descriptive)

Traditional Chinese Medicine comprises a number of long-established practices. The following is description only, with no guarantee of efficacy or scope of application for any of them:

- **Chinese herbal medicine (herbs)**: medicinal materials from plants, minerals and animals are combined into formulas, decocted or made into patent medicines to be taken.
- **Acupuncture (针灸)**: fine needles are inserted into specific points on the body's surface; it is one of the best-known TCM therapies.
- **Tuina (推拿)**: manual techniques of pressing, kneading, pushing and grasping on the body.
- **Cupping (拔罐)**: cups are used to create negative-pressure suction on the skin, often applied to the back and shoulders.

> NegaraKu.md describes these practices only as facts of tradition and of industry practice, and does not judge or endorse their medical meaning, efficacy or safety. The strength of scientific evidence varies by therapy and by condition.

## Regulation: The Traditional and Complementary Medicine Act 2016

Malaysia historically took a largely self-regulating approach to traditional and complementary medicine, until the passing of the **Traditional and Complementary Medicine Act 2016 (Act 775)** changed that picture. The Act established the **Traditional and Complementary Medicine Council** (coordinated by the Traditional and Complementary Medicine Division under the Ministry of Health), regulates the relevant services, and lists several traditional medical systems as **recognised practice areas**.

| Recognised practice area | Notes |
| --- | --- |
| Traditional Malay Medicine | Perubatan Tradisional Melayu |
| **Traditional Chinese Medicine (中医)** | Traditional Chinese Medicine |
| Traditional Indian Medicine | Including the Siddha, Ayurveda and other lineages |
| Homeopathy | Homeopathy |
| Osteopathy | Osteopathy |
| Chiropractic | Chiropractic |
| Islamic Medical Practice | Islamic Medical Practice |

Under this framework, TCM practitioners must **register** with the Council and hold a valid practising certificate. Registration applications opened in **March 2021**; after a period focused on education and transition, the Act's **enforcement phase took effect from March 2025**. According to the Ministry of Health and media reports, those who practise without a licence, or who fail to display a valid practising certificate, may face a fine or imprisonment. It must be stressed: **there is a clear statutory line between recognised TCM practice and modern (Western) medicine** — TCM practice is not equivalent to, and does not replace, the diagnosis and treatment of a registered modern-medicine doctor.

## TCM Education

Alongside the registration regime, local TCM education has also moved toward formalisation. Several higher-education institutions now offer a **Bachelor of Chinese Medicine (Honours) programme**, some of which have obtained accreditation from the **Malaysian Qualifications Agency (MQA)** — for example **Southern University College** in Skudai, Johor, whose Bachelor of Chinese Medicine (Honours) programme obtained provisional accreditation in 2016 and was later fully accredited.

For those who intend to practise, obtaining a degree is only the first step: to register and practise as a TCM practitioner, one must still meet the requirements of the Traditional and Complementary Medicine Council. The accreditation status and entry requirements of each programme should be taken from the institution's official website and the MQA's official register.

## Used Alongside Modern Medicine

In Malaysia, TCM is often **used alongside** modern medicine (integrative use): some people see a TCM practitioner for bodily regulation while receiving modern-medicine treatment; others use acupuncture and tuina to relieve certain discomforts. This parallel use is very common, but it does not change the statutory line described above.

This article does not give personalised advice on "whether to see a TCM practitioner or a modern-medicine doctor". A prudent approach is to tell every practitioner truthfully about all the therapies and medicines you are using (including Chinese medicine), so that each is informed; for emergencies or serious or persistent health problems, consult a registered modern-medicine doctor or relevant medical professional promptly.

## Common Misconceptions

**Equating "selling Chinese medicine" with "registered TCM practice".** The retail and dispensing of a Chinese medicine shop is distinct from the individual practice registration obtained under Act 775; being able to buy medicinal materials does not mean the shop has a registered TCM practitioner in attendance.

**Assuming TCM is unregulated in Malaysia.** Since Act 775 of 2016, TCM has been a recognised practice area, practitioners must register, and the Act also provides enforcement and disciplinary mechanisms.

**Treating traditional claims as proven efficacy.** This article takes a descriptive stance toward each therapy: the claims of tradition and practitioners, and scientific evidence, are two different levels and should not be conflated.

**Assuming TCM can replace modern medicine.** There is a clear line between recognised TCM practice and modern medicine; many people choose to use both together, rather than replacing one with the other.
