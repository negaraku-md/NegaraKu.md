A person anxious after a risky night can walk into a government Klinik Kesihatan in Malaysia, get an HIV test for free and confidentially, and — if needed — go home with preventive medication within a few hours. Yet that same path also intersects with questions of confidentiality, law, religion and stigma that make HIV one of the most sensitive health topics in the country.

This article factually explains where the services can be obtained and what context surrounds them, without taking sides on any view.

## Where can you get an HIV test?

HIV testing is provided **free and confidentially** at government health clinics (Klinik Kesihatan) across Malaysia. In addition, testing can also be done through the network of non-governmental organisations (NGOs) under the **Malaysian AIDS Council (MAC)**, an umbrella body established in 1992 to coordinate NGO efforts related to HIV and AIDS.

HIV self-testing is also available and serves as an entry point to referrals for prevention packages. Those who test negative can be referred to PrEP; those who test positive are referred to antiretroviral treatment.

NGOs providing related services include:

- **PT Foundation** (Selangor/Kuala Lumpur)
- **Kuala Lumpur AIDS Support Services (KLASS)**
- **Family Health Development Association (FHDA)** — Penang
- **Sabah AIDS Support Services Association (KASIH)**
- **Sarawak AIDS Concern Society (SACS)**
- **SEED Malaysia** and **KOMITED Malaysia** (Pahang)

## What is the state of HIV in Malaysia?

Malaysia's epidemic pattern has changed. In the early days, the majority of infections came from needle sharing among injecting drug users. According to the Malaysian AIDS Council, **since 2011 sexual transmission has become the main mode of infection**. The epidemic is now concentrated in four key target groups: people who inject drugs (PWID), female sex workers (FSW), transgender people (TG), and men who have sex with men (MSM).

Figures reported by the Ministry of Health give a picture of the current scale:

| Indicator | Figure | Year/Source |
|---|---|---|
| New HIV cases per year | Around 3,000 | Reported 2023 (MOH) |
| Cases among those aged 20–39 | 77% | Data reported by the Minister of Health |
| New infections (homosexual/bisexual contact) | ~60% | Data for the relevant year |
| New infections (heterosexual contact) | ~36% | Data for the relevant year |
| 95-95-95 target achieved | 83-66-82 | 2021 |

The figure "83-66-82" means 83% of people living with HIV know their status, 66% of those who know are receiving ART treatment, and 82% of those treated achieve a reduction in viral load. Malaysia aims to end AIDS as a public health threat by **2030**.

## What are PrEP and PEP, and how do you get them?

**PrEP** (pre-exposure prophylaxis) is a medication taken by people who do **not** have HIV but are at high risk, to prevent infection. According to the Malaysian AIDS Council, it is provided **free for Malaysian citizens aged 18 and above**, subject to periodic consultations and STI testing as part of the prescription.

**PEP** (post-exposure prophylaxis), on the other hand, is taken **after** possible exposure to HIV and **must be started within 72 hours** to be effective.

On 1 December 2022, the Ministry of Health announced free PrEP at selected public health clinics. This two-year programme began in January 2023, initially at **18 health clinics** in Selangor, the Klang Valley, Johor, Penang and Sabah, targeting around **9,000 to 10,000** members of the target groups. Before this programme, PrEP at private clinics cost around **RM80 to RM140 a month**, while at UMMC the price was reduced to around **RM30**. The programme is also supported by The Global Fund and run together with the Malaysian AIDS Council.

For those who have been confirmed positive, **antiretroviral treatment (ART)** is provided **free or heavily subsidised** at Ministry of Health facilities. Consistent treatment enables the viral load to be suppressed to an undetectable level.

## What is the legal and confidentiality context?

Malaysia **has no specific law** criminalising the transmission of HIV. Instead, general provisions of the **Penal Code** may apply to the spread of diseases dangerous to life:

- **Section 269** — a negligent act likely to spread infection; punishable by up to 6 months' imprisonment and/or a fine.
- **Section 270** — a malignant act likely to spread infection; punishable by up to 2 years' imprisonment and/or a fine.

According to the HIV Justice Network, there have been **no known HIV prosecutions** to date under these provisions.

On confidentiality, the **Medical Act 1971** enforces patient confidentiality, and the Malaysian Medical Council's Code of Ethics requires fair treatment. However, the Malaysian AIDS Council notes that a **legal gap** exists: there is no explicit prohibition against discrimination in medical settings and no specific penalty for providers who disclose HIV status without consent. A case reported in February 2025 — a paediatric specialist allegedly refusing to treat a newborn after the father disclosed his HIV status — became an example raised by advocates. Differing views exist on whether a specific anti-discrimination law is needed or the existing framework is sufficient.

## What is the premarital HIV screening policy?

A distinctive feature of Malaysia is **mandatory premarital HIV screening for Muslim couples**. Johor introduced it in 2001, and by early 2009 the requirement had expanded nationwide for all Muslim couples wishing to marry. It is administered by the **State Islamic Religious Departments**. Non-Muslim couples are not required to undergo it, although proposals to extend it were raised in 2018. This policy has also been the subject of differing views — some support it on public health and religious grounds, while others raise concerns about rights and cost-effectiveness.

Remember that PEP is time-sensitive — within 72 hours of exposure. For further official information, refer to the Malaysia Ministry of Health and Malaysian AIDS Council websites.
