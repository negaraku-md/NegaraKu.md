A single plastic card determines whether a child with autism receives early therapy, or whether an adult who has lost a leg can afford a prosthetic. In Malaysia, that OKU card is the gateway to almost every service and form of aid designed for persons with disabilities — and understanding how it works saves time, money and delayed treatment.

In 2024, a total of 805,509 persons with disabilities (OKU) were registered in Malaysia, representing 2.4 percent of the total population, according to the Department of Statistics Malaysia (DOSM). Yet that figure is far below the global estimate that roughly one in seven people live with some form of disability — a sign that many remain unregistered and have yet to access their entitlements.

## What does the OKU card unlock in the public health system?

The OKU card is issued by the Department of Social Welfare (JKM) to Malaysian citizens confirmed to have a disability by a medical officer. It is not merely an identification marker — it is a basic condition for most monthly allowances, device aid and service priorities.

JKM recognises seven main categories of disability:

- Physical disability or limb loss
- Learning disabilities (including dyslexia, autism, Down syndrome, ADHD and global developmental delay)
- Hearing disability or deafness
- Blindness or severe low vision
- Speech disability
- Mental disability requiring psychiatric confirmation
- Multiple disabilities

Registration is free and voluntary, but encouraged because it enables the government to identify and channel appropriate aid. Applications can be made manually at a JKM counter or online through the MyDaftar OKU platform. The required documents include the OKU Registration Form, a confirmation report from a medical specialist at a government or private hospital or clinic, and a copy of the MyKad (adults) or birth certificate (children under 12).

Treatment at government hospitals and clinics themselves is open to all citizens and heavily subsidised, so the OKU card is not required simply to be admitted to a ward. The real value of the card is access to the allowances, assistive devices and rehabilitation programmes described below.

## How does community-based rehabilitation (PDK) work?

Not all rehabilitation happens inside hospitals. The Community-Based Rehabilitation Programme (PDK) is a rehabilitation and intervention initiative carried out within the local community for all OKU categories, with a specific focus on children and teenagers.

PDK is run jointly by JKM and the local community, often through Voluntary Welfare Organisations (PSK) that manage centres in their respective areas. Parents or caregivers who wish to register their child can contact the PSK that operates the nearest PDK centre.

Activities at a PDK centre cover eight main areas of intervention:

- Development of gross and fine motor skills
- Language and social development
- Self-care skills training
- Vocational skills training
- Music therapy
- Sports and recreational activities

This community model matters because it brings therapy closer to families, reduces the cost of travelling to specialist hospitals, and builds local support — especially in rural areas far from a hospital's Medical Rehabilitation Unit.

## Who pays for assistive and prosthetic devices?

Wheelchairs, prosthetic legs and hearing aids are expensive, and this is where government aid is felt most. Assistive/Prosthetic Device Aid (BAT) under JKM funds the purchase of prosthetics and support devices for less-affluent target groups.

Devices covered include:

- Manual or electric wheelchairs (for those who cannot walk)
- White canes and braille machines (for low vision)
- Prosthetics such as legs and arms (for limb loss)
- Hearing aids (for hearing loss)

To qualify, an applicant must be a Malaysian citizen and resident, fall within a JKM target group, have income not exceeding the current Poverty Line Income (PGK), and be supported by a medical officer (government or private) or a JKM officer depending on the type of disability. Aid is provided according to the fixed actual price of the device. Applications are processed through the JKM eBantuan portal.

Besides BAT, the PeKa B40 scheme under the Ministry of Health Malaysia (KKM) also provides medical equipment aid of up to RM20,000 for the B40 group, including items such as wheelchairs, alongside transport incentives to obtain treatment. This scheme is income-based rather than OKU-specific, but many B40 OKU qualify for both.

## What monthly financial aid is available?

Beyond devices and therapy, JKM channels monthly cash allowances that support the cost of living and care. The following table summarises the main OKU-related aid:

| Aid | Rate | For whom | Key conditions |
|---|---|---|---|
| OKU Worker's Allowance (EPOKU) | RM450 per month | Employed OKU | Citizens aged 16 and above, registered OKU, monthly income not exceeding RM1,500, not a resident of a JKM institution |
| Aid for OKU Unable to Work (BTB) | RM300 per month | OKU unable to work full-time | Citizens aged 16–59, registered OKU, confirmed unable to work through a JKM interview, household income below PGK |
| Bedridden OKU Care Aid (BPT) | RM500 per month | Caregivers of bedridden OKU / chronic patients | Paid to a confirmed caregiver |
| Assistive/Prosthetic Device Aid (BAT) | According to device price | OKU requiring assistive devices | Income below PGK, supported by a medical officer or JKM |

EPOKU and BTB cannot be received simultaneously — EPOKU incentivises those who work, while BTB supports those unable to work full-time. All of these aids require valid OKU registration, again underscoring the OKU card's role as the gateway.

## How do KKM and JKM share the work?

OKU healthcare sits at the intersection of two agencies, and understanding the division of labour helps families know where to go.

The Ministry of Health Malaysia (KKM) provides diagnosis, medical treatment, surgery and clinical medical rehabilitation — including physiotherapy, occupational therapy and speech therapy at a hospital's Medical Rehabilitation Unit. KKM medical officers also confirm the disability required for OKU registration and recommend devices.

The Department of Social Welfare (JKM), under the Ministry of Women, Family and Community Development, manages OKU registration, community PDK programmes, device aid, monthly allowances and welfare institutions.

In practice, the usual family pathway is: a KKM doctor confirms the disability → JKM registers and issues the OKU card → the OKU accesses ongoing therapy through PDK and/or hospital medical rehabilitation, and applies for devices and allowances through JKM. Both agencies share the same historical origin: the community-based rehabilitation model in Malaysia began as a joint effort between JKM and KKM since the 1980s.

The scale of this support is large. In 2024, DOSM reported that 262,618 OKU received JKM aid worth RM1.1 billion, with the unable-to-work category recording the largest number of recipients (120,844 people), followed by the OKU worker's allowance (106,055 people) and bedridden or chronic patient care (34,476 people).

## What to know next

- **Not yet registered?** Gather your MyKad or birth certificate, one passport-sized photo and a medical confirmation report, then apply at the district JKM counter or through MyDaftar OKU. Registration is free.
- **Need therapy for a child?** Contact the PSK that operates the nearest PDK centre for early intervention, while referring to a hospital's Medical Rehabilitation Unit for clinical assessment.
- **Need an assistive device?** Obtain a letter of support from a medical officer, then apply for Assistive/Prosthetic Device Aid (BAT) through the JKM eBantuan portal.
- **Check allowance eligibility.** Compare the conditions of EPOKU (employed) and BTB (unable to work) — you can only receive one.

Rates and eligibility conditions may change according to the annual budget. Confirm the latest figures with JKM (hotline 03-8000 8000) or the official JKM eBantuan portal before making an application.
