In Malaysia's old estate lands, and in the Tamil streets of its towns, you can find a herbal shop — rows of dried herbs, powders (choornam) and oils lined up on its shelves. These are no accident; they are traces of a medical tradition more than a century and a half old.

**Siddha medicine** is an ancient system of medicine that arose within the Tamil tradition. This article introduces it in the Malaysian context — as a cultural heritage and as a legally regulated field — in a **descriptive** spirit. It is not medical advice; it states neither the benefit of any treatment nor any dosage.

## The tradition and its core concepts

The knowledge of Siddha medicine is said by this tradition to have come **from the Eighteen Siddhars (Pathinen Siddhar)** — in particular **from Agathiyar (Agastya)**. It is a body of knowledge deeply rooted in the Tamil language and in Tamil culture.

Some of the core concepts this tradition puts forward (given here only as descriptions of the tradition's own account):

- **The three doshas** — the tradition explains the body's functioning as the balance of three elements: **Vatham, Pittham and Kabam**.
- **Sarakku** — preparations based on herbs, minerals and metals, which the tradition says are purified in various ways before use.
- **Nadi pariksha (pulse reading)** — a traditional diagnostic method that assesses a person's condition by examining the pulse at the wrist.

> NegaraKu.md only **describes** these concepts and practices; it does not confirm or evaluate their medical efficacy.

## How it reached Malaysia

Malaysia's present-day practice of Siddha medicine is, studies say, largely the result of **colonial-era Tamil migration**. In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, Tamils who came from South India to the rubber estates brought this tradition with them, along with their dietary habits and healing knowledge.

Some studies point to an even older connection — suggesting, on the basis of archaeological evidence from Kedah's **Bujang Valley**, that the tradition may have been introduced earlier through ancient trade links, and that in 1870 a man named **Periya Thambi** taught Siddha medicine to 50 students in Malaya. These early claims are not fully verified; researchers continue to debate them.

## The estate era and community practice

At a time when isolated estate communities had little access to modern medical facilities, home remedies and herbal knowledge were part of everyday life. Particular herbs, oils and powders were passed down from generation to generation. In the towns and in Tamil settlements, herbal shops supplied the materials of this tradition.

According to one study, as Tamils migrated in large numbers from the estates to the towns, this practice gradually declined. The way of life and the environment of Malaysia's Tamil community have greatly shaped the standing of Siddha medicine. (For the social history of the Tamil community, see [Tamils in Malaysia](/en/arts-culture/tamil-in-malaysia) and [the estate Tamil community](/en/arts-culture/estate-tamil-community).)

## Legal regulation

In Malaysia, traditional and complementary medicine (T&CM) is regulated under the **Traditional and Complementary Medicine Act 2016 (Act 775)**. This Act provides for the regulation of practitioners and for the establishment of a **Traditional and Complementary Medicine Council**. Enforcement came into effect in stages; from 1 March 2021, further provisions came into force.

The **recognised practice areas** under this Act are a defined set — Traditional Malay Medicine, Traditional Chinese Medicine, **Traditional Indian Medicine**, Homeopathy, Osteopathy, Chiropractic, and Islamic Medical Practice. **Siddha medicine** is regarded as part of the field of Traditional Indian Medicine (sources note that this field encompasses Ayurveda, Siddha and Unani).

Practitioners who wish to work in these areas must **register with the Council** and obtain a valid practising certificate. After an interim transitional period, those who are not registered are prohibited from providing services in the recognised practice areas. This clarifies the **line between registered T&CM services and modern medicine**: T&CM is regulated as a complementary practice, not as a substitute for modern medical treatment.

## Relationship with Ayurveda and Unani

Siddha, Ayurveda and Unani all fall, in Malaysia's regulatory framework, under the single umbrella field of **Traditional Indian Medicine**. Even so, they are distinct traditions: Siddha is rooted in the Tamil tradition; Ayurveda developed within a different cultural-linguistic tradition; and Unani arose from a blend of West Asian and Indian traditions. Although some of their concepts overlap, they are not one and the same.

## Current status

Although Siddha medicine is now legally recognised in Malaysia, academic studies note that its growth faces certain challenges — the small number of trained Siddha practitioners, hospitals and teaching universities is seen as a key obstacle. Nevertheless, researchers note with optimism the arrival of a new generation of practitioners. The trend of integrating T&CM into the modern healthcare system in a coordinated way is also gradually growing.

To compare the regulation of another of Malaysia's major traditional medical traditions, see [Traditional Chinese Medicine in Malaysia](/en/healthcare/traditional-chinese-medicine-in-malaysia).

## Common misconceptions

- **"Siddha and Ayurveda are the same."** No — they are distinct traditions; although both fall under the field of Traditional Indian Medicine in Malaysia's regulatory framework, they are different systems.
- **"Siddha medicine is a substitute for modern medicine."** No — it is regulated purely as a complementary practice; for serious health problems it is appropriate to consult registered medical doctors.
- **"Anyone can provide services as a Siddha practitioner."** No — under Act 775, registration is mandatory to work in a recognised practice area.
