    a: "It depends on the track. In the public (MOH) system you generally do: it is a gatekept system, so a medical officer at a Klinik Kesihatan assesses you and, if specialist care is needed, writes a referral letter (surat rujukan) to a hospital specialist clinic, which then gives you an appointment — many hospitals do not take walk-ins for specialist clinics. The wrinkle is insurance: if you want your private care paid cashlessly, your insurer or its panel may still require a referral letter before they will issue a guarantee letter for a planned consultation or admission. Emergencies need no referral at all: go to the emergency department."

  - q: "What should I bring to a specialist appointment?"
    a: "Treat it as a preparation checklist. Bring the referral letter (surat rujukan or your GP's letter); identification (MyKad, or passport for non-citizens); any previous medical records you hold — earlier results, imaging films or discs and their reports, discharge summaries, and clinic cards or booklets; a current list of your medicines with their doses (bring the boxes if unsure); and information on any allergies you have been told about.   - q: "Can I move between the public and private systems with the same referral?"
    a: "You can move between them, and carrying a letter and your records helps, but do not assume a referral transfers automatically. Going the other way, from public to private, you can usually self-refer, and a clinical summary of your condition, treatment and the reason for transfer should travel with you. In both directions the practical point is the same: because records are not yet shared across all facilities nationally, the more of your own history you bring — letters, results, imaging, medication list — the smoother the hand-over. A referral is a hinge moment in anyone's care: your doctor has decided — or suggested — that your problem needs a specialist, and suddenly you are navigating a different part of the health system. This guide picks up exactly there. It does **not** tell you whether you need a specialist; that judgement belongs to your clinician. What it does is explain **how referrals actually work in Malaysia, and what to prepare**, so the hand-over from clinic to specialist is as smooth as it can be.

The first thing to know is that Malaysia runs **two tracks** — public and private — and a referral behaves differently in each.

## The public (MOH) pathway: gatekept

The government system, run by the Ministry of Health (MOH), is **gatekept and tiered**. You normally enter at the primary-care layer — a **Klinik Kesihatan** (government health clinic) — where a medical officer assesses you. If specialist care is judged necessary, they write a **referral letter (surat rujukan)** to a hospital's **specialist clinic (klinik pakar)** at a district, state or tertiary hospital.

Two features follow from this:

- **The referral letter is usually required.** Many hospitals do not accept walk-ins to their specialist clinics, so without a referral you may be turned away for non-emergency care. (Treat this as the general rule, and check your specific hospital.)
- **The hospital sets your appointment.** The specialist clinic gives you a **date** — you do not pick a slot freely — because these clinics are appointment-based to manage demand. When you arrive, you **register (pendaftaran)** first, then wait to be called.

Expect, too, that the referral is usually to a **department rather than a named doctor**, so the specific specialist you see may vary; and that specialist care is often a **series** — you may leave with a follow-up (temujanji susulan) date, or be referred back to primary care once things are stable.

## The private pathway: direct, but watch your insurer

In the private system you have more freedom: you can often **see a specialist directly**, either self-referred or sent on by a private GP, without first passing through a primary-care gatekeeper.

The complication is **insurance**. If you want cashless treatment, it typically runs on a **guarantee letter (GL)**: you present your medical card and identification, the hospital requests a GL from your insurer, and the insurer approves cover (or not). Two things about this are worth planning around:

- **Some insurers require a referral letter** (from a panel GP or clinic) before they will issue a GL for a planned consultation or admission — so a referral can matter for payment even where the hospital would see you without one.
- **Coverage is often tied to panel hospitals and panel specialists.** A specialist or hospital outside your insurer's panel may not be covered, and a deposit may still be asked for.

These rules **differ by insurer and policy**, and GL approval is never guaranteed, so the single most useful thing you can do before private specialist care is **confirm with your own insurer**: is a referral needed, and are this hospital and this specialist on the panel?

## One rule for both tracks: emergencies skip referral

If it is an emergency, **do not go through referral at all** — go straight to the **emergency department (Jabatan Kecemasan / A&E)**. The referral pathway is for planned, non-emergency specialist care.

## Why you should carry your own records

Malaysia does **not yet have a single national medical record** linking every clinic and hospital; a unified system is a multi-year rollout still under way. In the meantime, **you are often the courier of your own history** between facilities — which is the strongest reason to arrive well-prepared.

## What to bring

Treat this as a preparation checklist (none of it is medical advice):

- **The referral letter** — your *surat rujukan* or GP's letter. Without it, a public specialist clinic may turn you away.
- **Identification** — MyKad, or passport for non-citizens — needed at registration, and with your medical card if you are going private.
- **Your previous records** — earlier results, imaging (films, discs and reports), discharge summaries, and any clinic cards or booklets you hold.
- **A current medication list** — the names and doses of what you take (bring the boxes or strips if you are unsure).
- **Allergy information** — anything you have been told you react to.
- **Insurance details, if private** — your medical card and policy details, and a check beforehand that both the hospital and specialist are on your insurer's panel.
- **A written list of questions** — consultation time is short; a list helps you use it well.
- **Your appointment slip or reference** (public) — bring what the hospital gave you.

A companion or translator, a notebook for instructions, and a means of payment for any nominal public fee or private deposit are also worth having.

## Don't assume one app does it all

Malaysia's digital-health tools are growing, but each has a **current, limited role**, and it is best not to assume a single app runs your whole referral. The government appointment app has been used mainly for **primary-care and dental** bookings; wider hospital specialist-clinic booking and **electronic referrals** are described as **planned** rather than fully in place; and a unified national record is **still being rolled out**. A government health **portal** exists chiefly to provide health *information*, not to manage your referral. Because all of this is changing, check what **your** clinic or hospital currently uses — its own appointment letter, website and counter staff are the authoritative source for that facility.
