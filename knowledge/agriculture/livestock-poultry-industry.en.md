The Malaysian plate depends on chicken. It is the cheapest protein in the country — and that is why when the sheds wobble, it becomes a national matter, not merely a farm matter.

Malaysia's livestock industry is really a tale of two fates. On one hand, chicken and eggs are so efficient that the country produces more than it needs. On the other, beef, mutton and much of the animal feed still have to be imported. This framework defines every recurring supply crisis.

## How self-sufficient is Malaysia in protein supply?

The answer varies by type of meat. Eggs are the success story: the self-sufficiency ratio (SSR) for hen/duck eggs reached 108.9% in 2022, meaning Malaysia produces more than it eats. Duck meat, meanwhile, recorded 130.2%. But for chicken meat and red meat, the country is not self-sufficient — DOSM notes that only duck meat and hen/duck eggs exceeded 100% in 2022, placing chicken meat and others below that level.

Reliance on imports is most pronounced for red meat. Cattle and goats require large grazing areas and long rearing cycles — a structural weakness compared with chicken, which can be harvested in a matter of weeks. This is why supermarket beef shelves are often stocked with imported product.

|---|---|---|

| Duck meat | 130.2% | Surplus production |
| Chicken meat | Below 100% | Partly dependent on imports |
| Beef, mutton | Well below 100% | Heavily dependent on imports |

## Why is the price of chicken so easily disrupted?

The key weakness of this industry is animal feed. Corn and soybean — the main ingredients of chicken feed — are largely imported, and account for roughly 70% of a chicken producer's operating costs according to the USDA GAIN report. When global grain prices surge and supply chains are disrupted, local farm costs rise immediately even when there is no domestic problem.

It was this structure that triggered the 2022 crisis. In response to inflation, the government imposed a ceiling price on chicken. But when actual costs exceeded the ceiling price, farmers reduced production or sold at higher prices unofficially — and supply was affected.

## What happened in the 2022 chicken crisis?

The crisis became the most famous episode in modern Malaysian food supply history. According to the USDA GAIN report, the cause was rising production costs — especially imported animal feed — together with export dynamics and global supply chain disruptions at the time.

The government responded with three tools at once:

- **Subsidies.** Production subsidies were first introduced on 5 February 2022 with an initial allocation of RM528.52 million. The rate was later raised to RM0.80 per kilogram of chicken and RM0.08 per egg by October. The total chicken and egg subsidy for February–December 2022 reached RM1.233 billion (about USD265.16 million).
- **Ceiling prices.** The retail ceiling price for whole chicken was raised to RM9.40 per kilogram by October 2022, with separate ceilings for grade A eggs (RM0.45), grade B (RM0.43) and grade C (RM0.41).
- **Export ban.** From 1 June 2022, the government banned exports of all chicken products while easing import conditions. The ban was relaxed in phases — products such as nuggets and sausages in mid-June, and live commercial broilers on 10 October 2022.

This export ban also affected the regional market — Singapore, which normally imports much of its fresh chicken from Malaysia, had to find other sources (general context, beyond the scope of the cited USDA report). The lesson of this crisis is clear — price controls without addressing input costs merely shift the pressure, they do not eliminate it.

## How does the DVS safeguard livestock biosecurity?

The Department of Veterinary Services (DVS), under the Ministry of Agriculture and Food Security, is the nation's guardian of animal health. Its role encompasses disease surveillance, farm certification, livestock movement control and — when outbreaks emerge — the culling of infected animals to break the chain of transmission.

Biosecurity is not merely a technical issue. For an industry where a single outbreak can wipe out thousands of animals within days, it determines the stability of supply and price. Two diseases test this system repeatedly: avian influenza and African swine fever.

## What are the threats of avian influenza and African swine fever?

**Avian influenza (H5N1).** Malaysia has been hit by highly pathogenic avian influenza several times. The 2017 H5N1 outbreak in Kelantan — the first in nearly ten years — began on 28 February 2017 at a village coop near Kota Bharu, where 15 of 26 village chickens died and the rest were culled. The outbreak was reported to the World Organisation for Animal Health (OIE/WOAH) on 8 March 2017 (CIDRAP). The DVS's standard response includes culling, restrictions on the movement of chickens and intensified surveillance.

**African swine fever (ASF).** This disease is more alarming because there is no effective vaccine and the mortality rate is nearly 100% in pigs. ASF was declared **endemic** in Malaysia in August 2025 by the Minister of Agriculture. The 2025 Selangor outbreak involved 76,000 pigs across 57 farms around Kuala Langat and Sepang, while the Penang outbreak reported on 9 July 2025 affected three farms in Kampung Selamat — 578 animals were culled and 124 died. As a supply measure, the DVS approved pork imports from nine countries involving 130 slaughterhouses (CodeBlue).

An important distinction: avian influenza threatens the chicken sector that is the backbone of the nation's protein and carries public health implications (potential transmission to humans), whereas ASF affects only pigs and does not transmit to humans — but both test the same biosecurity capacity.

## What's next?

The structural weaknesses of Malaysia's livestock industry — dependence on imported animal feed and imported red meat — will not disappear anytime soon. National food security policy now focuses efforts on reducing this dependence, including encouraging the production of local feed ingredients such as grain corn.
