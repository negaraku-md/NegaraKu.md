Imagine a poor family moved to a freshly cleared forest, given a plot of land and a monthly wage, then asked to wait for years until their rubber or oil palm trees matured. That was the offer that changed the lives of more than 100,000 rural Malaysian families — and its engine was FELDA.

## What is FELDA and why was it established?

The Federal Land Development Authority (FELDA) was established on **1 July 1956** under the **Land Development Ordinance 1956**. Its aim was clear and urgent in the early years of independence: to eradicate rural poverty by opening up new land for agriculture and settling poor families as settlers.

The scheme was the early post-independence era's policy answer to the land and poverty problems that gripped rural communities. The first scheme, the **Air Lanas Land Development Scheme in Kelantan**, opened in **March 1957** and settled about 400 settlers across 1,680 hectares. From that seed, FELDA grew into one of the largest rural resettlement programmes in the world.

The scope of these schemes changed not only the economy of families but also the nation's social geography — dozens of small towns and new communities sprang up in areas that were once forest. (The broader economic-policy narrative concerning the racial composition of the settlers is discussed separately; this article stays with the institutional and historical aspects.)

## How did the FELDA land settlement model work?

The model was structured and long-term in nature. Each settler family received a plot of planted land to grow rubber or oil palm, plus a housing lot in a clustered village. The government paid wages to settlers throughout the period while the crop was still immature, and settlers then repaid the land development costs in instalments, like a mortgage.

Once the crop began to yield, settlers sold fresh fruit bunches or rubber through the FELDA system, earning a more stable income than subsistence farming. This clustered structure also allowed the government to provide schools, clinics, roads and water supply centrally.

## Why did FELDA stop taking in new settlers?

By **1990**, FELDA's original task — settling poor families on new land — had essentially been completed. Roughly **114,400 families** had been settled across around **500,000 hectares** of scheme land. After that year, FELDA no longer opened up new settler placements and shifted into a statutory body generating its own income through a range of subsidiaries.

Among the corporate entities formed to manage settler services and commercial ventures were the FELDA Investment Cooperative (KPF) and Felda Holdings Berhad. This transition marked FELDA's change from a settlement agency into a large-scale plantation and investment group.

## How is FELDA land owned and inherited?

Here lies one of the most enduring issues. Settler land is subject to the **Group Settlement Areas Act 1960**, which was designed to keep each lot as a viable economic unit. Its two main restrictions have now become a source of generational tension:

- **Limit on the number of owners.** The Act limits the number of individuals who can be registered as owners of a lot, even where a settler leaves many heirs. (A proposal to set a specific limit of two heir names is part of an amendment being studied in 2026 — see the section below — and is not the current legal position set out in the referenced sources.)
- **Prohibition on subdivision.** A lot cannot be broken up or subdivided, so heirs cannot inherit the land according to their respective shares under ordinary inheritance law.
- **The administrator practice.** When a settler dies, an administrator is often appointed to hold the land on behalf of the other heirs — a trust that can give rise to disputes if not carried out fairly.

As a result, many in the second generation are trapped: they inherit an interest in the land but cannot own it separately, build their own homes, or settle the estate easily. This issue has been the focus of policy research and reform demands for many years.

## What is FELDA's relationship with FGV Holdings?

To increase returns from its vast plantations, FELDA established its investment arm, **Felda Global Ventures (now FGV Holdings)**. It was listed on Bursa Malaysia on **28 June 2012** in an initial public offering worth **RM9.93 billion** — reported as the world's second-largest IPO that year after Facebook.

At the time of listing, FGV managed around **500,000 hectares** worked by about **112,600 settlers**, plus roughly 355,864 hectares of leased land in Malaysia. It subsequently expanded its holdings through acquisitions in Malaysia and Indonesia.

The relationship between settlers and FGV remains complicated. The share performance and some non-plantation investments raised questions about how far the listing truly benefited settlers — a debate that continues in public discourse and policy inquiry.

## What comes next

The second-generation land issue is now back on the policy table. In July 2026, the government announced a review of the **Group Settlement Areas Act 1960**, with Deputy Prime Minister Ahmad Zahid Hamidi stating that the amendments target the housing needs of the new generation. Among the proposals being studied: limiting the registration of heirs to two names with one representative administrator, and allowing more than one housing unit on a single housing lot subject to state approval.

At the same time, the issuance of land grants to settlers is nearing completion: as of July 2026, **109,104 of 112,638 settlers (96.86%)** had received their land grants.

For settlers and their second generation, sensible practical steps include confirming the status of the family's land grant, understanding the restrictions of the 1960 Act before planning an estate, and following the progress of the amendments under study. Monitor the official FELDA website and government announcements for details of the final amendments.
