Ask a bank for "a business loan" and you will be offered a menu, not a single product. Each facility is built for a different job — buying a machine, bridging a 60-day invoice, paying an overseas supplier, posting a tender bond. Picking the wrong one is how businesses end up paying term-loan interest on a short-term cash-flow gap. Here is the menu, in plain terms.

## Working-capital and general facilities

**Term loan (TL).** A lump sum disbursed once and repaid over a fixed tenure by scheduled instalments of principal and interest. It suits one-off capital spending — equipment, premises, renovation, an expansion or acquisition. SME term loans commonly run up to about five years, longer when secured by property.

**Overdraft (OD).** A revolving facility attached to your business current account: you can draw the account into debit up to an approved limit, and interest is charged daily only on the amount actually overdrawn. There is no fixed repayment schedule, which makes it ideal for smoothing day-to-day cash flow — but the flexibility usually comes at a higher rate.

**Revolving credit (RC).** A committed short-term line drawn down in fixed tranches (say one-, three- or six-month periods) up to a limit, repaid and redrawn as the limit "revolves". It sits between a term loan and an overdraft: a committed line for recurring working-capital needs without the open-ended cost of an OD.

## Trade finance facilities

Trade finance exists to bridge the gap of time and trust between a buyer paying and a seller shipping.

**Letter of credit (LC).** The bank's irrevocable undertaking, on the buyer's application, to pay the seller once the seller presents shipping documents that comply with the LC's terms. It substitutes the bank's credit for the buyer's and is governed internationally by the ICC's UCP 600. Malaysian importers open LCs to pay overseas suppliers.

**Trust receipt (TR).** Short-term import financing. When documents arrive under an LC, the bank pays the supplier and releases the goods to you against a trust receipt — under which you hold the goods *as trustee for the bank* (the bank keeps title) and repay from the sale proceeds by a set date, commonly within 120-180 days.

**Bankers' acceptance (BA).** A bill of exchange drawn to finance a specific purchase or sale of goods and "accepted" by a bank, which undertakes to pay its face value at maturity. The bill is sold in the money market at a discount to face value, and that discount is the cost of financing. Tenors run conventionally from about 21 to 365 days.

**Bank guarantee (BG).** Not a cash advance but the bank's written promise to pay a third party a sum on demand if you fail to perform an obligation. Businesses use BGs for performance bonds, tender and bid bonds, and security deposits to suppliers, landlords or authorities such as Customs.

**Invoice financing / factoring.** Financing advanced against your unpaid invoices — typically 70-90% of invoice value upfront — to unlock cash tied up in credit sales. Under **recourse** factoring (the common form here) you must buy back an invoice your customer fails to pay, so you keep the default risk; under **non-recourse** factoring the financier takes that risk, for a higher fee.

## Trade finance and EXIM Bank

For cross-border trade, **EXIM Bank** — the Export-Import Bank of Malaysia, a government-owned [development financial institution](/finance/development-financial-institutions-malaysia) — fills gaps the commercial market leaves. Alongside project and buyer financing, its distinctive product is **export credit insurance and takaful**, which protects an exporter (or its bank) against a foreign buyer's non-payment — cover that lets Malaysian firms trade into unfamiliar markets.

## Equipment and asset financing

To acquire plant, vehicles or machinery without paying cash upfront, businesses use **hire purchase** or **leasing**. Under hire purchase you pay by instalments and own the asset at the end; Malaysia's Hire-Purchase Act 1967 governs consumer goods and specified vehicle classes, while commercial plant and machinery outside that scope is typically financed by a lease or an equipment facility governed by the contract itself. Under a lease, the lessor keeps ownership and you pay rentals for use.

## The Islamic equivalents

Every facility above has a Shariah-compliant counterpart, named with an "-i" and built on a nominate contract:

- **LC-i, TR-i, BG-i** — Islamic letters of credit, trust receipts and bank guarantees, using Wakalah (agency), Murabahah (cost-plus sale) and Kafalah (guarantee).
- **Accepted Bill-i (AB-i)** — the Islamic bankers' acceptance, on Murabahah for imports or Bai' al-Dayn (sale of debt) for exports.
- **AITAB (Al-Ijarah Thumma Al-Bai')** — the standard Islamic vehicle and hire-purchase structure: an Ijarah lease followed by a sale that transfers ownership.
- **Tawarruq** (commodity murabahah) — widely used for cash and working-capital financing.

## Which facility for which need

| Your need | Facility |
| --- | --- |
| Buy equipment, premises or expand | Term loan; hire purchase / leasing for assets |
| Smooth day-to-day cash flow | Overdraft or revolving credit |
| Pay an overseas supplier | Letter of credit, then trust receipt |
| Finance a specific trade purchase | Bankers' acceptance |
| Post a tender or performance bond | Bank guarantee |
| Unlock cash from unpaid invoices | Invoice financing / factoring |
| Protect against a foreign buyer defaulting | EXIM export credit insurance / takaful |

## What's next

Match the facility to the need, then compare the all-in cost (rate plus fees) on the bank's product disclosure sheet. If collateral is the obstacle, a [government guarantee scheme](/finance/government-sme-financing-schemes-malaysia) can bridge it; for the bigger picture of how SMEs get financed, start with the [SME financing guide](/finance/sme-business-financing-malaysia).
