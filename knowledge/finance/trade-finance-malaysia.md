Trading across borders adds a problem domestic business does not have: the buyer and seller are in different countries, under different laws, and often have never met. The seller does not want to ship before being paid; the buyer does not want to pay before the goods arrive. Trade finance is the set of bank instruments that bridges that gap of time and trust. This guide follows the chain for an importer and for an exporter, and shows how to choose the right settlement method.

The individual instruments — letters of credit, trust receipts, bankers' acceptances, bank guarantees and their Islamic equivalents — are defined in our [business loan facility types](/finance/business-loan-facility-types-malaysia) guide. Here we focus on how a trader strings them together.

## The importer's chain

An importer buying from an overseas supplier typically moves through four steps:

1. **Open a letter of credit (LC).** You ask your bank (the *issuing bank*) to open an LC in favour of the seller. The LC substitutes the bank's creditworthiness for yours, so the seller ships against a bank's promise rather than trusting you directly.
2. **Shipment and documents.** The seller ships and presents the shipping documents (bill of lading, invoice, packing list, insurance) through its bank to your bank. If the goods arrive before the documents, your bank can issue a **shipping guarantee** so you can take delivery from the carrier without the original bill of lading.
3. **Finance the trust gap.** On a deferred-payment (usance) LC, your bank accepts the time draft — creating a **bankers' acceptance** — giving you a financing window before cash is due. To release the goods to you *before* you have paid, the bank finances you against a **trust receipt**: it keeps title while you sell the goods and repay from the proceeds.
4. **Settle.** You repay the bank at maturity, closing the chain.

The risk this addresses is the importer's **working-capital gap** between taking delivery and getting paid by its own customers — the gap the trust receipt is named for.

## The exporter's chain

An exporter faces the mirror-image problem — paying to produce goods, then waiting to be paid after shipping them:

- **Pre-shipment financing** funds you to buy raw materials and produce the goods *before* shipment, against a confirmed order or an export LC.
- **Post-shipment financing** bridges the wait *after* shipment: your bank **negotiates, purchases or discounts** the export bill, advancing its value (less charges) immediately instead of waiting for the overseas buyer's bank to remit. Payment under an LC then flows from the importer, through the banks, back to you.

Everything here depends on **compliance**: payment under an LC is released only if your documents match the credit's terms exactly.

## EXIM Bank: the export credit agency

Where commercial trade finance stops, **EXIM Bank Malaysia** — the government-owned, official **export credit agency** and a [development financial institution](/finance/development-financial-institutions-malaysia) — fills the gap. It offers pre- and post-shipment trade financing and guarantees, but its **distinctive product is export credit insurance and takaful.**

That cover protects a Malaysian exporter (or its financing bank) against a **foreign buyer's non-payment** — from **commercial risks** (buyer insolvency, payment default, refusal to accept goods) and **political risks** (transfer restrictions, war, civil disorder, default by a public-sector buyer). It is what lets a Malaysian firm sell into an unfamiliar market, or on open-account terms, without carrying the full risk of not being paid. EXIM also runs dedicated SME export-development programmes; confirm the current scheme on its site, as these are periodically refreshed.

## The rules behind it: UCP 600 and Incoterms

Two international rulebooks from the **International Chamber of Commerce (ICC)** sit under every trade deal:

- **UCP 600** standardises how banks issue, examine and honour **letters of credit**. Its core principle: a credit is independent of the sales contract, and **banks deal only in documents, not goods** — they pay against a complying presentation.
- **Incoterms 2020** standardise who bears **delivery, cost and risk** between buyer and seller (for example who pays freight and insurance, and where risk passes). They do not govern payment; they must be written into the sales contract to apply.

The Incoterm in your contract must line up with your LC wording and invoice — a mismatch is one of the commonest reasons documents get rejected.

## Choosing a settlement method

Not every deal needs a letter of credit. Match the method to how much you trust the counterparty and the country risk. From **safest for the exporter** to **riskiest**:

| Method | Who it favours | When to use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cash-in-advance | Exporter (safest) | New or higher-risk buyer who will accept it |
| Letter of credit | Balanced, bank-backed | Parties don't know each other, or higher-risk country |
| Documentary collection | Middle ground | Some trust; cheaper than an LC, but no bank payment guarantee |
| Open account | Importer (buyer-friendly) | Trusted repeat buyer — often paired with export credit insurance |

**Open account** — ship now, get paid in 30 to 90 days — is cheapest and the most competitive offer to a buyer, but it carries the highest non-payment risk. This is exactly where EXIM's export credit insurance or takaful fits: it lets you offer open-account terms while covering the risk of not being paid.

## Common pitfalls

- **Document discrepancies** — because banks pay only against a complying presentation, a typo, a late shipment date or a mismatched goods description can mean the bank refuses to pay even though the goods shipped fine. Prepare documents to the LC's exact wording.
- **Fraud** — an LC protects against non-payment, not against a dishonest counterparty shipping worthless or absent goods. Inspection certificates and known counterparties matter.
- **Currency risk** — invoicing in a foreign currency exposes you to exchange-rate movement between contract and settlement; banks and EXIM offer hedging.
- **Wrong method** — shipping on open account to an unknown buyer with no insurance, or paying LC costs for a trusted repeat buyer where a documentary collection would do.

## The Islamic version

Malaysian banks offer Shariah-compliant equivalents of every instrument (the "-i" variants, covered in the [facility-types guide](/finance/business-loan-facility-types-malaysia)), built on contracts such as **Murabahah** (cost-plus sale), **Bai' al-Dayn** (sale of debt, for discounting trade bills) and **Wakalah** (agency, for LC-i). EXIM itself runs Islamic and conventional facilities side by side.

## What's next

Map your deal: are you the importer or exporter, do you know the counterparty, and how risky is the country? Pick the settlement method from the table, line up the Incoterm with your LC, and talk to EXIM about credit insurance if you want to sell on open account.
