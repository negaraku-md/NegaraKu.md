Few businesses pay cash for a machine, a fleet of vans or a production line. They finance it — and the way they finance it quietly decides who owns the asset, who gets the tax benefit, and what happens when the term ends. Picking the wrong structure can cost more than a slightly higher rate would. Here is how to choose.

## Three ways to finance an asset

| | Hire purchase | Finance lease | Equipment term loan |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Who owns it** | Financier, until your last payment | Lessor keeps ownership | You, from day one |
| **At the end** | You own it automatically | Return, renew or buy at a residual | Already yours |
| **Tax benefit** | You claim capital allowance | You deduct the rental | You claim capital allowance |
| **Deposit** | Usually a deposit | Often little or none | A margin of finance |
| **Best for** | Assets you will keep for years | Assets you need briefly or that date fast | When you want outright ownership |

The decision comes down to two questions: **how long will you keep the asset**, and **do you want the tax shield from capital allowances** (hire purchase or loan) **or the simplicity of a fully deductible rental** (lease)?

## What the Hire-Purchase Act 1967 actually covers

This surprises many business owners. The **Hire-Purchase Act 1967 (Act 212)** applies **only to the goods listed in its First Schedule** — section 1(2) says so expressly. That Schedule is:

1. **all consumer goods** — defined in the Act as goods bought for **personal, family or household purposes**; and
2. **specified motor vehicles**: invalid carriages, motorcycles, motor cars (including taxis and hire cars), **goods vehicles up to 2,540 kg** maximum permissible laden weight, and buses.

**The inference that matters for business** (and we flag it as inference, not a direct statutory line): industrial and commercial **plant and machinery bought for business use is not "consumer goods,"** and **heavy goods vehicles over 2,540 kg** are not listed — so both fall **outside** the Act. Financing of such equipment is governed by the **general law of contract**, and is commonly documented as a **finance lease** rather than a statutory hire-purchase agreement. That is why your office photocopier can be on a true Hire-Purchase Act agreement while your factory's production line is on a lease.

## The 2026 reform: fairer term charges

A significant change took effect recently. The **Hire Purchase (Amendment) Act 2026**, in force since **1 June 2026**, **abolished the flat-rate interest method and the Rule of 78** — the old approach that front-loaded interest and penalised early settlement. Term charges now move to an **effective-interest, reducing-balance** basis, so interest is charged on what you still owe and settling early is fairer. The amendment also enables **digital execution** of agreements and adds identity checks against fraud. The exact maximum term-charge limits sit in the accompanying **Term Charges Regulations 2026.**

## Finance lease vs operating lease

For assets outside the Hire-Purchase Act, the usual vehicle is a lease — and there are two flavours, distinguished by who carries the risks and rewards of ownership:

- a **finance lease** effectively funds a purchase over time (the lessor is really just financing it); and
- an **operating lease** is true rental for a period, after which you hand the asset back.

Note the accounting: under **MFRS 16**, a **lessee** now brings almost all leases **on-balance-sheet** as a right-of-use asset and a lease liability — the old off-balance-sheet operating lease has largely gone for lessees. The finance-versus-operating distinction now matters mainly for the lessor's books.

## The tax angle

This is where the choice earns or costs real money, so it is worth getting right with an adviser. At a principle level:

- **Hire purchase** — you, the **hirer**, are treated as the economic owner and **claim capital allowances** on the asset, based on the **capital portion of the instalments** paid each year; the **interest element is separately deductible**.
- **Lease** — you, the **lessee**, **deduct the rental** as a business expense; the lessor, as owner, claims the capital allowances.

One important caveat: for **non-commercial passenger vehicles** there is a cap on qualifying expenditure (commonly cited at RM50,000, rising to RM100,000 only for a new vehicle costing up to RM150,000); **commercial vehicles such as lorries and vans are not capped the same way.** The exact percentages, accelerated-allowance eligibility and caps are set by the **current LHDN Public Rulings** and change — treat the above as the principle and confirm the figures with a tax adviser.

## The Islamic version: AITAB and Ijarah

The Shariah-compliant structure is **AITAB (Al-Ijarah Thumma Al-Bai')** — an **Ijarah (lease)** for the hire term, **thumma al-bai' (then a sale)** by which you buy the asset at the end. Vehicle hire purchase in Malaysia is **very commonly AITAB**, offered by Islamic banks and Islamic windows and overseen under Bank Negara's Ijarah standard. A pure **Ijarah** (operating-style Islamic lease) omits the final sale — you keep paying rental and return the asset.

## Government and development-bank help

Several schemes lower the cost of equipment financing for SMEs:

- **MIDF** (Malaysian Industrial Development Finance) runs soft-financing schemes for **machinery, equipment and automation** (such as its automation and modernisation schemes), typically at concessional rates over multi-year tenures.
- **Agrobank** finances **agricultural machinery and equipment** through Islamic facilities.
- Other [development financial institutions](/finance/development-financial-institutions-malaysia) such as SME Bank also provide asset and term financing for SMEs.

Scheme rates, caps and tenures change, so verify the current terms on the official sites, and see our guide to [government SME financing schemes](/finance/government-sme-financing-schemes-malaysia).

## What's next

Decide how long you will hold the asset and whether you want capital allowances or a deductible rental, then price hire purchase against a lease and an [equipment term loan](/finance/business-loan-facility-types-malaysia) on the same asset. Check whether a [government soft-financing scheme](/finance/government-sme-financing-schemes-malaysia) applies, confirm the tax treatment with your adviser, and — for vehicles and smaller equipment — remember the 2026 rules mean early settlement is now fairer than it used to be.
