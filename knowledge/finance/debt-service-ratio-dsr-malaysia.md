If one number decides whether your loan is approved, it is your debt service ratio. Banks use it to answer the only question that really matters to them — can you afford the repayments? Understand how it is built and you can improve it before you apply, instead of guessing why you were turned down.

## The formula

> **DSR = total monthly debt commitments ÷ monthly income × 100%**

"Total monthly debt commitments" means your existing instalments **plus the new instalment you are applying for.** That last part is why the same salary can pass for a small loan and fail for a large one — the loan you want is counted against you.

## Gross or net income?

Bank Negara Malaysia's responsible-lending rule frames affordability around income **after statutory deductions** — EPF, SOCSO and monthly tax (PCB). So the regulatory basis is **net** income.

In practice it varies: some banks use net, others gross. And most treat **variable income** — commission, overtime, bonus, rental — more cautiously than a fixed salary, averaging or discounting it or asking for a longer track record. How much of your income the bank **recognises** can matter as much as the ratio.

## What counts as a commitment

Commonly included:

- housing-loan or property-financing instalments;
- car or [hire-purchase](/finance/equipment-asset-financing-malaysia) instalments;
- personal-loan and personal-financing instalments;
- **credit-card minimum commitment** (see below);
- study loans, including **PTPTN**;
- other fixed financing visible on your [CCRIS](/finance/credit-reporting-ccris-ctos-malaysia) record, and sometimes guarantees you have given.

The **credit-card** figure is the tricky one: how a bank converts your card into a monthly commitment is **not standardised** — some use a percentage of your outstanding balance, some a percentage of your credit limit regardless of usage, some the actual minimum payment. (The standard minimum payment itself is 5 percent of the outstanding balance, or RM50, whichever is higher.) This is why the same card can be counted differently by two banks.

Generally **not** counted as debt: living expenses, utilities, rent you pay, and insurance premiums — though the bank separately expects you to keep a buffer for them.

## Why there is no single cap

Here is the point most people get wrong: **there is no Bank Negara-mandated DSR cap.** BNM requires a "prudent" ratio but sets no number. Each bank fixes its **own** ceiling. Commonly cited bands sit around **60 to 70 percent**, lower for lower-income applicants and higher for high earners.

Why the difference? Because a ratio is not affordability — **the ringgit left after debt is.** Look at three illustrations (examples only, not bank policy):

**Example A — lower income.** Net income RM3,000. Existing commitments RM1,000 (car RM650 + PTPTN RM150 + card RM200) → DSR 33.3%. Apply for a new car loan at RM900/month → total RM1,900 → **DSR 63.3%**, leaving **RM1,100** a month. A bank using a ~60% ceiling for this band likely says no.

**Example B — middle income.** Net income RM6,000, existing RM2,600 → DSR 43.3%. Add a RM1,400 instalment → RM4,000 → **DSR 66.7%**. Now approval turns purely on whether that bank's ceiling is 65% or 70% — same applicant, different bank, different answer.

**Example C — higher income.** Net income RM15,000, existing RM5,500 → DSR 36.7%. Add a RM4,000 property instalment → RM9,500 → **DSR 63.3%** — the *same ratio as Example A* — but with **RM5,500** left each month instead of RM1,100.

Examples A and C sit at an identical 63.3%, yet one leaves RM1,100 and the other RM5,500. That is exactly why a high earner is allowed a higher ratio: the money left over still covers living costs with a buffer.

One more nuance: the **gross-versus-net** choice alone can move you across a line. Commitments of RM2,000 are 50% of RM4,000 gross but 57.1% of about RM3,500 net.

## How to lower your DSR

Reduce the numerator or raise the denominator:

- **Settle small or nearly-finished loans.** Removing one instalment cuts the ratio immediately — the fastest lever.
- **Pay down credit-card balances.** If your bank counts a percentage of the outstanding, clearing a balance removes that assessed commitment.
- **Lengthen the new loan's tenure.** A longer tenure means a smaller monthly instalment and a lower DSR — but **more total interest** over the life of the loan (and personal-financing tenure is capped at 10 years). A trade-off, not a free win.
- **Add a joint applicant or guarantor** to combine incomes.
- **Document more income.** Keep payslips, EPF statements and, for the self-employed, filed accounts and tax returns — income the bank cannot verify does not count.
- **Add nothing new** in the months before you apply.

## The rules behind it

DSR sits inside Bank Negara's **Responsible Financing** framework, which defines the "prudent debt service ratio" banks must observe without fixing a figure. The **2025 Policy Document on Personal Financing** (issued 30 September 2025) reinforces it: banks must run an **affordability assessment**, **verify your income**, and may **not** offer pre-approved personal financing; personal-financing tenure is **capped at 10 years**; and from **1 January 2027**, taking new personal financing above **RM100,000** requires completing a **financial-education module** (provided by the lender or AKPK).

## DSR vs DTI

If you have read international guides, "DSR" is Malaysia's term for what elsewhere is usually called **DTI (debt-to-income)** — effectively the same household measure of monthly debt against income. Malaysian banks and Bank Negara use "DSR."

Before applying, calculate your own DSR including the instalment you want, and if it is near a bank's likely ceiling, lower it first using the levers above.
