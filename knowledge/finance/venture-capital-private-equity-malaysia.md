Most Malaysian companies grow on retained profit, a bank overdraft, or an SME loan. A small but important group grows on somebody else's equity instead — a fund that buys a slice of the business, pushes it to scale, and sells the slice years later for a multiple. That is what venture capital and private equity do, and Malaysia regulates, funds and taxes the activity in ways that surprise people who assume it works like the United States.

Here is how the money is governed, who supplies it, what it costs in tax, and what a founder should understand before taking it.

## Venture capital and private equity — the same shape, different stage

Both are equity investment by a professionally managed fund. The difference is stage and control.

**Venture capital** backs young, high-growth companies — usually technology — taking a minority stake and betting that a few winners will pay for the many that fail. **Private equity** buys into larger, established companies, often taking control, and creates value by restructuring, consolidating or professionalising the business before an exit.

In Malaysia the two are regulated under one framework but as separate categories, and the numbers reflect their different scale: at the end of 2024, private equity accounted for the larger share of committed funds and venture capital the smaller.

## Who regulates it: the SC's registration regime

The key thing to understand is that running a VC or PE fund in Malaysia does **not** require the full Capital Markets Services Licence that ordinary fund managers need. Instead, the Securities Commission Malaysia (SC) operates a lighter **registration** regime, set out in its *Guidelines on the Registration of Venture Capital and Private Equity Corporations and Management Corporations*, made under **section 377 (read with section 76) of the Capital Markets and Services Act 2007**.

Registration comes in four categories, splitting the fund itself from the company that manages it:

| Category | Full name | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| **VCC** | Venture Capital Corporation | The venture fund — holds the money, makes the investments |
| **VCMC** | Venture Capital Management Corporation | The manager that runs the VC fund |
| **PEC** | Private Equity Corporation | The private-equity fund vehicle |
| **PEMC** | Private Equity Management Corporation | The manager that runs the PE fund |

A manager registers with the SC before it manages VC or PE money; applications are filed through the SC's electronic application system. The regime has been progressively liberalised to make Malaysia a more attractive base — reportedly removing an older cap on the number of investors and streamlining the minimum-funds requirement for management corporations — though the exact current thresholds should be read off the SC's guidelines directly.

## What it costs in tax — and why the old answer is wrong

This is the single most misreported fact about Malaysian VC, so it is worth being precise.

**The old regime (2022).** Three gazette orders set the earlier terms:

- **P.U.(A) 115/2022** exempted a VCC from income tax on its statutory income (excluding certain deposit interest), for five years of assessment or the remaining life of the fund, whichever was shorter — conditional on at least half its funds going into seed, start-up or early-stage companies.
- **P.U.(A) 116/2022** exempted a VCMC on its share of profits, management fees and performance fees, from the year of assessment 2018 to 2026.
- **P.U.(A) 117/2022** gave investors a tax deduction for investing in a VCC — up to RM20 million, held for three years, over a window running to 31 December 2026.

**The current regime (from June 2025).** In June 2025 the government revamped and extended the incentive. In broad terms, a qualifying VC company is now taxed at a **concessionary 5% rate** for up to ten years (subject to a minimum share of its funds going into local venture companies), and a VC or PE management company is taxed at **10%**, from the year of assessment 2025 to **2035**. The first-certification deadline was pushed out to 31 December 2035.

The practical takeaway: venture capital in Malaysia is **no longer tax-free** — it is taxed at a low, concessionary rate. Any page still telling you VC income is fully exempt is quoting the superseded regime. Because the implementing gazette order was still being finalised, confirm the exact rate, conditions and order number with the SC or a tax adviser before you rely on them.

## Who actually supplies the money

Malaysia's VC and PE capital comes from three overlapping pools: government funds, private domestic and regional funds, and family offices and corporates.

On the **government** side, the landscape was reorganised in July 2024 when the sovereign fund **Khazanah Nasional** took full ownership of the two main state vehicles:

- **MAVCAP** (Malaysia Venture Capital Management Berhad) — the largest government-owned VC firm, investing directly and through funds into technology startups.
- **Penjana Kapital** — which runs **Dana Penjana Nasional**, a *matching* fund-of-funds: it commits government money into VC funds on the condition that private investors match it, multiplying the capital available to founders.
- **Cradle Fund** supports the earliest stages — pre-seed and seed grants and early equity for tech startups.

Khazanah has since signalled a **National Fund-of-Funds** to channel further billions into the VC/PE ecosystem, part of a policy push — the Malaysia Venture Capital Roadmap 2024–2030 and the KL20 action plan — to make Kuala Lumpur a regional startup-funding hub by 2030.

On the **private** side, the industry association **MVCA** (Malaysian Venture Capital & Private Equity Association) represents managers and publishes statistics; well-known managers active in the market include large regional PE houses and specialist VC funds.

## How a company raises it — and what changes when you do

For a founder, taking VC or PE money is not a loan; it is selling part of the company. That has consequences a bank facility does not:

- **You give up equity and some control.** Investors take shares, usually with rights attached — board seats, veto rights over major decisions, preferences on exit. Read the term sheet as carefully as the valuation.
- **You take on a clock.** A fund must eventually return money to *its* investors, so it needs an exit — a trade sale or a listing — typically within a set number of years. Your timeline becomes tied to theirs.
- **You need to be fundable in the first place.** VC backs scalable, high-growth models; PE backs established cash-generative businesses. A steady local services company is often a poor fit for either, and better served by an SME loan or grant.

If equity investment is not the right fit, Malaysia's grant and SME-financing channels — covered in the related guides — are usually the better first stop.

## What's next

If you are a **founder**, decide honestly whether your business is a venture or private-equity fit before you chase either; if it is, prepare for dilution, governance rights and an exit timeline, not just a cheque. If you are setting up a **fund**, your first step is SC registration under the correct category (VCC, VCMC, PEC or PEMC), and your tax position now runs on the concessionary-rate regime rather than the old exemption — get the current gazette terms confirmed before you model returns.

For the wider funding picture, see the companion guides on SME financing and on equity crowdfunding and peer-to-peer lending, which cover the routes open to companies that are not a VC or PE fit. And because the VC tax terms changed in 2025 and the implementing order was still settling, treat any specific rate here as a figure to confirm against the SC before you act on it.
