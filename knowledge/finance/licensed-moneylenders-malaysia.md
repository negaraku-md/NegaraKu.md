When a bank says no and cash is short, the next advert is never far away: "fast loan, no documents, approved today." Some of these lenders are properly licensed and legal. Others are loan sharks, and a growing number are not lenders at all — just a bank account waiting for your "processing fee." Knowing which is which is the difference between a regulated loan and a trap.

## What makes a moneylender "licensed"?

A moneylender is a business that lends money for profit outside the banking system. In Peninsular Malaysia it must hold a licence issued under the **Moneylenders Act 1951 (Act 400)** by the **Ministry of Housing and Local Government (KPKT)**. In 2021 KPKT rebranded the licensed trade as the **Credit Community (Komuniti Kredit)**, so official material uses both names — but the governing law and licensing requirement are unchanged.

A licence matters because it brings the lender under real rules: capped interest, limited charges, a prescribed loan agreement, and conduct restrictions (for example, a licensed lender may not turn up at your home or workplace to collect). An unlicensed lender is bound by none of these and operates as a criminal.

## The legal interest caps

This is the single most useful fact a borrower can carry. Under **section 17A** of the Act, a licensed moneylender may charge interest of no more than:

- **12% per annum on a secured loan**, and
- **18% per annum on an unsecured loan**

— roughly 1% and 1.5% a month, and compound interest is not allowed. If an agreement charges more, that interest term is **void and unenforceable**, and the lender commits an offence (a fine and possible imprisonment). Separately, under **section 23**, a licensee generally may not add charges for loan-related expenses beyond stamp duty and the attestation fee stated in the prescribed agreement. So a licensed loan should have a transparent, capped cost — no surprise "admin", "insurance" or "guarantee" fees stacked on top.

## How to verify a licence before you borrow

Never take a lender's word for it. KPKT runs a verification tool, the **i-Kredikom** app, which lets anyone check a moneylender's status by **company name, licence number, or company registration number** and confirm the licence is valid and active. It covers **Peninsular Malaysia**; in **Sabah, Sarawak and Labuan**, licensing is handled by the respective state or territory authority, so check there instead. If you cannot confirm a current, active licence — or the lender resists being checked — walk away.

## Licensed lender vs. "Ah Long"

An **Ah Long** is an unlicensed, illegal moneylender — a loan shark. The distinction is not cosmetic: dealing in illegal moneylending is a crime, and the loan is unenforceable. Loan sharks charge interest far above the statutory caps and enforce it through intimidation that is well documented in Malaysia — splashing red paint on homes and cars, hanging shaming banners, arson threats, and harassing not just the borrower but relatives and co-workers who never took the loan. These acts are offences under the Moneylenders Act and the Penal Code. A licensed lender, by contrast, is barred from this conduct and from visiting you to collect.

A related, separately licensed channel is **pawnbroking** under the **Pawnbrokers Act 1972** (its Islamic equivalent, **Ar-Rahnu**, charges a safekeeping fee rather than interest) — a short-term loan against a pledged item such as gold. It is a legitimate option worth knowing about, though governed by its own rules.

## Spotting a loan scam

The fastest-growing danger is not the loan shark but the fake lender. The pattern is consistent, and one rule catches most of it: **a legitimate lender never asks you to pay a fee before the loan is disbursed.** Treat these as red flags:

- An **upfront fee** — "processing", "insurance", "stamp duty", "GST", or "security deposit" — demanded *before* you receive any money.
- Being told to pay to **release, unlock or insure** a loan that has supposedly already been approved.
- **Impersonation** of a real bank, a licensed lender, or a government body — copied logos, a fake "officer", a near-identical website or name.
- "Approval" with **no documents and no credit check**, plus pressure to act now.
- A request to pay into a **personal (individual) bank account** rather than a company account.

## Where to check and report

Malaysia has three official tools. Use them before you pay, and the moment something goes wrong:

- **Semak Mule** (**semakmule.rmp.gov.my**, run by the police) lets you check whether a **bank account number or phone number** is linked to scam reports *before* you transfer anything. Caveat: a "no record found" result is not a guarantee of safety — a fresh scam account may not be flagged yet.
- **BNM's Financial Consumer Alert** list (**bnm.gov.my/financial-fraud-alerts**) names unauthorised companies and websites posing as licensed financial institutions. It is a guide, not an exhaustive list — and note that Bank Negara regulates banks, not KPKT-licensed moneylenders, so for a moneylender's licence you still use i-Kredikom.
- The **National Scam Response Centre (NSRC)**, hotline **997**, is the rapid-response line: call it as soon as you realise you have been scammed and it can act to freeze the receiving (mule) account. Call 997 yourself — scammers sometimes pose as NSRC officers in inbound calls.

## What's next

If you are considering a non-bank loan: verify the licence on i-Kredikom, confirm the all-in cost sits within the 12%/18% caps, and read the prescribed agreement before signing. If the lender wants money upfront, it is not a lender. And before borrowing outside a bank at all, it is worth checking whether a cheaper route fits — a bank facility, a [government SME financing scheme](/finance/government-sme-financing-schemes-malaysia), or free help from AKPK if the real problem is [existing debt](/finance/debt-consolidation-restructuring-malaysia).
