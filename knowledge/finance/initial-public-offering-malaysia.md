Taking a company public is one of the biggest financing decisions its owners will ever make — it raises permanent capital, puts a market price on the business, and hands shareholders a way out. It also submits the company to a level of scrutiny and cost that catches many founders by surprise. In Malaysia, an initial public offering (IPO) means listing on Bursa Malaysia, and the route you take depends entirely on how big and how proven your company is.

Here is how a company actually gets listed — which market to aim for, the hurdles each one sets, how the approval works, and what it costs.

## Three markets, three sizes of company

Bursa Malaysia runs three markets, and choosing the right one is the first decision.

| Market | Who it is for | Approved by | Profit requirement |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Main Market** | Larger, established corporations | Securities Commission (SC) | Yes — profit or market-cap test |
| **ACE Market** | Small and mid-sized companies, as a stepping stone to the Main Market | Bursa Malaysia (sponsor-driven) | **No minimum profit requirement** |
| **LEAP Market** | Micro, small and medium enterprises | Bursa Malaysia | No — but sophisticated investors only |

The distinction that matters most for a first-time issuer: the **Main Market is approved by the Securities Commission**, while the **ACE and LEAP markets are approved by Bursa** through an adviser or sponsor. The LEAP Market is restricted to sophisticated investors as defined under the Capital Markets and Services Act 2007, which makes it a niche fundraising platform rather than a true public listing.

## The Main Market: three ways in

A company can qualify for the Main Market through any one of three admission tests.

**The profit test.** This is the classic route. The company needs:

- aggregate after-tax profit of **at least RM30 million** over the most recent three full financial years;
- after-tax profit of **at least RM15 million** in the most recent financial year; and
- operation in the same core business over at least that profit track record.

These are the current official figures — worth stressing, because a large number of secondary guides still quote the old **RM20 million / RM6 million** thresholds. Those are outdated; use RM30 million / RM15 million.

**The market-capitalisation test.** For a company that is large but not yet consistently profitable, the alternative is a total market capitalisation of **at least RM500 million** on listing, having been incorporated and generated operating revenue for at least one full financial year.

**The infrastructure-project test.** A third route exists for companies that own the right to build and operate a large infrastructure project — generally with project costs of not less than RM500 million and a concession of at least 15 years remaining.

Beyond whichever test it uses, a Main Market applicant must also show working capital for at least 12 months from the prospectus date, positive operating cash flow over the three-year track record, no accumulated losses (for a profit-test listing), and continuity of management over the three years before submission.

## ACE and LEAP: for companies not yet big enough

The **ACE Market** deliberately sets *no* minimum operating-track-record or profit requirement. Instead, suitability is judged by a **Sponsor** — an approved adviser that vouches for the company and must continue to sponsor it for at least three full financial years after listing. This is what makes ACE a realistic path for a promising but young company.

The **LEAP Market** likewise has no profit requirement, requires an Approved Adviser, and — because it is open only to sophisticated investors — carries the lightest disclosure burden of the three, at the cost of a much smaller investor pool.

## How many shares must the public hold?

A listing has to create a genuine public float. The **public-spread** rules require:

- **Main Market:** at least 25% of total shares in public hands, held by a minimum of **1,000 public shareholders** each holding at least 100 shares;
- **ACE Market:** at least 25% in public hands, held by a minimum of **200 public shareholders**;
- **LEAP Market:** at least 10% of ordinary shares at admission.

There is also a minimum offering to the general public — at least 5% of the enlarged issued shares for a company with enlarged capital below RM200 million, or at least 2% at or above RM200 million.

## The Bumiputera equity condition

An IPO in Malaysia carries a national-equity condition. For a **Main Market** listing, **12.5% of the enlarged number of issued shares** must be allocated to Bumiputera investors approved or recognised by the Ministry of Investment, Trade and Industry (MITI), at the point of listing. In addition, at least half of the shares balloted to the Malaysian public must be made available to Bumiputera public investors. For licensed financial institutions, the allocation is handled by the Ministry of Finance instead.

On the **ACE Market**, the 12.5% obligation is **deferred** — it must be met within one year of achieving a Main Market profit track record, or five years after ACE listing, whichever is earlier. Companies with **MSC status, BioNexus status, or predominantly foreign-based operations are exempted** from the requirement altogether.

## The process, step by step

The listing runs primarily through the SC (for the Main Market) and ends with admission by Bursa:

1. **Appoint advisers.** The company engages an SC-approved Principal Adviser, plus reporting accountants, solicitors, an underwriter and other specialists.
2. **Pre-submission consultation.** Advisers are encouraged to consult the SC before filing, and to make concurrent submissions to other authorities such as MITI.
3. **Submit the IPO application** to the SC, with a registrable prospectus.
4. **SC review.** The SC assesses compliance with the Equity Guidelines, corporate-governance and adverse-record checks, prospectus disclosure, asset valuations and the Bumiputera requirement. It may expose the prospectus publicly for 15 working days for feedback.
5. **SC decision.** The applicant then has **six months** from the decision letter to implement the listing.
6. **Prospectus registration and listing.** The SC registers the prospectus under **section 232 of the Capital Markets and Services Act 2007**, and Bursa admits the company.

The SC works to a **timeline charter of 40 working days** for large-capitalisation companies and **60 working days** for Main Market applicants. The whole exercise, from appointing an adviser to listing day, **normally takes four to twelve months**. Once listed, promoters face a **moratorium** on selling their shares — the entire holding is locked up for six months on the Main Market; on the ACE Market that is followed by a further staggered release over three years.

## What it costs

An IPO is expensive, and most of the cost is professional rather than regulatory. Bursa's own guidance puts total listing costs at roughly **RM3 million to RM7 million for the ACE Market** and **above RM5 million for the Main Market**, depending on size and complexity.

The largest single component is usually **underwriting, placement and brokerage fees, generally 1% to 3%** of the value of the shares. On top of that sit reporting-accountant, legal and adviser fees, and the regulatory fees — a Main Market SC processing fee of RM100,000 plus 0.05% of market value (capped at RM3 million), or an ACE Market Bursa processing fee starting at RM80,000, plus initial and annual listing fees.

## What's next

If you are weighing an IPO, start by being honest about which market you actually qualify for: the Main Market profit test (RM30 million aggregate, RM15 million most recent year) is a high bar, and the ACE Market's sponsor route exists precisely for companies not yet there. Then budget realistically — several million ringgit and the best part of a year — and line up an SC-approved Principal Adviser early, because they drive the whole process.

Before you commit, verify the current thresholds and the SC Equity Guidelines edition directly with your adviser, since admission criteria and the Bumiputera allocation rules are periodically revised. And if a public listing is not the right fit yet, the companion guides on venture capital and private equity, and on SME financing, cover the private-capital routes that often come first.
