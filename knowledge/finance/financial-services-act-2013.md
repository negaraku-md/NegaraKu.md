The **Financial Services Act 2013 (FSA)**, cited as **Act 758**, is the principal statute governing conventional financial institutions in Malaysia. Administered by **Bank Negara Malaysia (BNM)**, it provides for the regulation and supervision of financial institutions, payment systems and other relevant entities, and for the oversight of the money market and foreign exchange market — all in pursuit of **financial stability**. It came into force on **30 June 2013**.

## Why the FSA was introduced

Before 2013, Malaysia's financial sector was governed by several separate, ageing statutes. The FSA modernised and unified this framework by **repealing and consolidating four laws** into a single, principle-based act:

| Repealed law | Coverage before the FSA |
| --- | --- |
| Banking and Financial Institutions Act 1989 (BAFIA) | Banks and finance companies |
| Insurance Act 1996 | Conventional insurers and intermediaries |
| Payment Systems Act 2003 | Payment systems and instruments |
| Exchange Control Act 1953 | Foreign exchange administration |

A parallel law, the **Islamic Financial Services Act 2013 (IFSA, Act 759)**, came into force on the same day and consolidated the Islamic Banking Act 1983 and the Takaful Act 1984 for the Shariah-compliant sector.

## Legislative timeline

- **18 March 2013** — royal assent
- **22 March 2013** — commencement (publication)
- **30 June 2013** — the operative provisions came into force

## What the FSA regulates

The act sets out a comprehensive supervisory regime. Its principal object, in **section 6**, is to promote financial stability; in pursuing this, BNM is to foster the safety and soundness of financial institutions and the integrity and orderly functioning of the money market and foreign exchange market. Key areas include:

- **Authorisation and licensing** — banking, insurance and investment banking business require a **licence granted by the Minister of Finance** on BNM's recommendation, while other regulated activities (e.g. money-changing, insurance broking, payment system operation) require **BNM approval**.
- **Prudential requirements** — minimum capital, corporate governance and risk-management standards for licensed institutions.
- **Ownership and control** — approval thresholds for acquiring significant shareholdings in licensed persons, and limits on control.
- **Financial groups** — supervision of financial holding companies and related entities.
- **Payment systems** — BNM may designate systemically important payment systems and set standards for their safety and reliability.
- **Business conduct and consumer protection** — rules on fair dealing, disclosure and prohibited conduct toward financial consumers.
- **Supervisory intervention and enforcement** — powers to examine institutions, issue directions, and take remedial action.

According to the summary of the statute, the FSA is structured into **18 Parts** containing around **281 sections** and **16 schedules**.

## Offences and penalties

The FSA carries substantial penalties to deter unauthorised activity. Under **section 8**, carrying on a licensed or approved business without authorisation is an offence punishable, on conviction, by **imprisonment for up to 10 years or a fine of up to RM50 million, or both**. Further offences — such as failing to meet minimum capital requirements or breaching authorisation conditions — attract their own imprisonment terms and multi-million-ringgit fines.

## How it fits with other regulators

The FSA governs the institutions BNM supervises. Capital markets (securities, unit trusts, the stock exchange) fall under the **Securities Commission Malaysia** and the Capital Markets and Services Act 2007, while deposit and takaful/insurance protection is provided by **PIDM**. Together these form Malaysia's layered financial-regulation architecture.
