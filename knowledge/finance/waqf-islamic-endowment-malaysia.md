Give a man a fish and he eats for a day. Endow a fishpond that can never be sold, and the whole village eats forever. That, in a sentence, is waqf.

A **waqf** (in Malay, *wakaf*) is a gift that is designed never to run out. You take an asset — a plot of land, a building, a sum of money — and you lock the asset itself away permanently, so that only the *benefit* it throws off is spent on a chosen purpose. The pond stays; the fish feed the poor. The land stays; the rent funds a school. The principle is old, but in Malaysia it sits inside a very particular legal machine, and that machine is what trips people up.

## So who actually owns a waqf?

Nobody, in the ordinary sense — and that is the point.

Once an asset is dedicated as waqf, Islamic jurisprudence treats it as no longer belonging to the donor, the beneficiary or the manager. The donor (the *waqif*) gives up ownership; the asset is, in effect, held for God and its benefit flows to the stated cause. Two things follow. The **corpus** — the asset itself — is preserved and, as a rule, cannot be sold, given away or inherited. Only the **usufruct** — the income or use — is applied to the purpose.

That permanence is what makes waqf powerful and what makes it hard to administer. An asset meant to last forever needs a trustee that also lasts forever. In Malaysia, that trustee is the state.

## Why is waqf a state matter?

Because the Federal Constitution says so. Religion of Islam — including the administration of waqf — falls on the State List, the slice of law reserved to each of the thirteen states (and, for the federal territories, to the federal government acting in that role). There is no single national waqf authority with legal title.

Instead, each state's **State Islamic Religious Council** — the *Majlis Agama Islam Negeri*, or SIRC — is named in that state's administration-of-Islamic-law enactment as the **sole trustee** of all waqf property in the state. When you create a waqf in Selangor, the Selangor council becomes the legal custodian; in Johor, the Johor council, and so on. A donor does not appoint a private trustee of their own choosing. This is also why the rules, forms and even the registers differ from state to state — waqf is thirteen systems, not one.

For how these councils are structured and what else they oversee, see [State Islamic Religious Councils](/en/states/state-islamic-religious-councils/).

## The main forms of waqf

Classically, waqf comes in two shapes, and Malaysian practice has since added several financial ones.

| Type | What it means | Typical example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Waqf am** (general) | Dedicated to charitable purposes broadly; the trustee decides how the benefit is applied | A plot endowed for the general good of the Muslim community |
| **Waqf khas** (specific) | Dedicated to a named beneficiary or purpose fixed by the donor | Land endowed for a particular mosque, school or cemetery |
| **Cash waqf** (*waqf tunai*) | A sum of money endowed instead of property, often in small amounts | Pooled contributions collected via banks or mosques |
| **Corporate waqf** | A company or its shares/assets structured to generate waqf benefit | A bank channelling returns to endowed projects |
| **Waqf shares** (*saham wakaf*) | Donors buy units/shares whose value is then dedicated as waqf | A share scheme funding a hospital or education fund |

The move beyond land is relatively recent. A **national fatwa in 2007** affirming the permissibility of cash waqf opened the door, and banks, state councils and federal agencies have since launched cash-waqf schemes that let anyone endow as little as a few ringgit — the modern equivalent of the villager who could never have dedicated a whole fishpond but can now chip in for one.

## The federal coordinators: JAWHAR and YWM

If the states hold the trusteeship, why is there anything federal at all?

Because waqf had a reputation for sitting idle — land registered a century ago, undeveloped, sometimes encroached upon. To push development and share expertise, the federal government created two bodies under the Prime Minister's Department:

- **JAWHAR** — the *Jabatan Wakaf, Zakat dan Haji* (Department of Awqaf, Zakat and Hajj) — established in **2004** to coordinate and strengthen the administration of waqf, zakat and hajj across the states.
- **Yayasan Waqaf Malaysia (YWM)** — a foundation set up in **2008** to develop waqf property, raise funds and run national waqf initiatives.

Both are coordinating and development arms, not trustees. They cannot override a state council's legal title; they help the councils do more with what they hold. The two bodies have more recently been reported as preparing a **National Waqf Master Plan for 2025 to 2030**, presented to the state councils as a shared roadmap.

## Where the capital market comes in

Waqf used to be the business of mosques and land offices. Now it also lives on the Securities Commission's rulebook.

In **November 2020** the Securities Commission Malaysia launched the **Waqf-Featured Fund Framework**. It applies to existing and newly launched **unit trust and wholesale funds** and lets a fund be structured so that investors keep their units while directing **all or part of the fund's income distribution to waqf**. The framework names eligible waqf recipients and imposes disclosure requirements, so investors can see how much is being endowed and where it goes. It flowed from a priority the Commission had flagged in its 2017 Islamic Fund and Wealth Management Blueprint, and the detailed rules sit inside the revised Guidelines on Unit Trust Funds and on Unlisted Capital Market Products. For the wider sector it belongs to, see [Islamic finance in Malaysia](/en/economy/islamic-finance/).

On the banking side, institutions such as Bank Muamalat have run corporate and cash-waqf programmes — funding education and community projects — typically collecting on behalf of the relevant state council rather than acting as trustee themselves.

## The land problem

For all the innovation, the oldest headache is still land.

Malaysia holds thousands of hectares of waqf land — academic studies have cited figures in excess of 8,000 hectares — yet a long-standing complaint is how much of it is undeveloped. The recurring obstacles are familiar: title and registration that was never cleaned up, parcels too small or oddly located to develop, shortages of capital, and in some cases encroachment. Much of the development effort of JAWHAR, YWM and the councils is aimed squarely at turning dormant land into income-producing assets — the only way a waqf actually delivers the benefit it was created for.

## Common misconceptions

- **Thinking waqf is just donating money.** A donation is spent; a waqf preserves the asset and spends only its benefit, in perpetuity.
- **Expecting to pick your own trustee.** The State Islamic Religious Council is the trustee by law; you create the waqf within the state's framework.
- **Assuming one national set of rules.** Waqf is administered state by state, so forms, registers and processes vary.
- **Treating JAWHAR or YWM as the owner.** They coordinate and develop; legal title stays with the state council.
- **Believing only the wealthy can endow.** Cash waqf and waqf-share schemes let ordinary savers contribute small amounts.

## What's next

Waqf in Malaysia is in a long transition — from a dormant stock of old land titles toward a live instrument of Islamic social finance, with cash waqf, corporate waqf and waqf-featured funds pulling it into the modern economy. If you are thinking of endowing, your first stop is your own state's religious council, because that is where the trusteeship and the paperwork live; if you are a fund or a company, the Securities Commission framework is the route into the capital market. Watch too for the National Waqf Master Plan, which aims to give the thirteen state systems a shared direction.
