Walk through any conversation about a hard-to-get loan in Malaysia and someone will mention an "agent" who can make it happen. Loan agents and financing advisers are a real and useful part of the market — but it is also a part with almost no rules, where the same word can mean a diligent professional or an outright scammer. Here is what they actually do, how they are paid, and how to use one without getting burned.

## What a loan agent actually does

A loan agent — also called a loan broker, mortgage broker or financing adviser — is an **intermediary** between you and lenders. A good one will:

- **assess your profile** and tell you, honestly, whether you are bankable and for how much;
- **package and submit** your application, with the documents presented the way lenders expect;
- **match you to the right lenders** — the ones whose credit appetite fits your situation — rather than letting you collect rejections one bank at a time;
- **advise on the levers** that decide approval: your [CCRIS and CTOS records](/finance/credit-reporting-ccris-ctos-malaysia), your [debt service ratio](/finance/debt-service-ratio-dsr-malaysia), and your documentation;
- sometimes **negotiate** rate or terms.

This is genuinely valuable for people whose files are hard for a bank to read quickly — **SMEs and the self-employed**, whose income does not sit neatly on a payslip, and anyone who has **already been rejected once** and does not understand why.

But be clear about the limit: a broker sells **convenience and know-how, not privileged access.** They cannot get an unbankable file approved, cannot override a bank's credit decision, and cannot guarantee approval. Everything a broker does, you can in principle do yourself — for free.

## How they get paid: the success-fee model

The market norm is a **success fee**: a percentage of the amount **approved and disbursed**, charged only *after* the loan comes through. Reported rates vary widely — business-loan brokers are commonly cited in the low single-digit percentages, while harder, sub-prime consumer cases are quoted much higher — but they are all **privately negotiated and set by no regulator.**

For **home loans**, the arrangement is often different: the **lender pays the referrer** a commission, so the borrower may pay the broker nothing directly.

Two rules protect you here:

1. **Agree the fee in writing before you engage anyone**, and know who is paying it.
2. **A success fee is due only after disbursement.** A broker fee is a charge *on top of* the loan's own cost — it is separate from, and additional to, any interest or profit rate the lender charges.

## Are loan agents regulated? Mostly not

This is the part most people get wrong, so it is worth stating plainly.

**Loan and mortgage broking — the packaging-and-submitting role — is largely unregulated in Malaysia.** There is **no dedicated "loan agent" licence**, no fee cap, and **no broker register** you can check. A party only becomes regulated when it does something more than broking:

- if it **lends money itself**, it needs a **moneylender's licence** from the Ministry of Housing and Local Government (KPKT) under the Moneylenders Act 1951 — see [licensed moneylenders and loan scams](/finance/licensed-moneylenders-malaysia);
- if it carries on **"financial advisory business,"** it needs **Bank Negara approval under the Financial Services Act 2013.**

That second point is a common trap. A licensed **"financial adviser"** *is* a regulated role — but that regime is built around **financial products such as insurance and takaful**, not loan broking. A firm calling itself a "financing adviser" in the loan-packaging sense is generally **not** an approved financial adviser and is not operating under that licence. Same English word, different thing.

The consequence for you: because the broker tier itself is unlicensed, there is **no conduct code, no fee cap and no complaints channel specific to loan brokers**, and no single licence to verify. The only official thing you can confirm is that the **company legally exists** (registered with SSM) — and, if it will lend to you, that it holds a **KPKT moneylender licence.**

## The one rule that stops most scams: never pay upfront

Because the field is unregulated and the promise ("I can get you approved") is seductive, loan scams are rampant — and they share one tell.

**A legitimate adviser is paid on success, after the loan is disbursed. A scammer asks for money first.** The classic pattern is an **upfront fee** dressed up as a "processing", "approval", "insurance", "stamp duty", "GST", "security deposit" or "release" fee — demanded *before* any loan is approved or paid out. Legitimate lenders deduct any genuine charges *from* the disbursed amount; they do not ask you to pay in advance to "unlock" a loan.

The usual delivery is **impersonation**: fake officers and copied logos posing as a real bank, as **Bank Negara**, or as a known advisory or comparison brand. Bank Negara's own scam notices make its position clear — it **never asks the public to transfer money** for clearance, safekeeping or to release a loan.

## How to vet an adviser

Before you hand over a cent or a document:

- **If they will lend to you, verify the KPKT moneylender licence** (via i-Kredikom) — detailed in the [licensed-moneylenders guide](/finance/licensed-moneylenders-malaysia).
- **Confirm the company is SSM-registered**, and that any account you pay into is the **company's**, not an individual's.
- **Never pay an upfront fee.** A genuine success fee is due only after disbursement.
- **Never surrender** your MyKad, online-banking username or password, or your banking **TAC/OTP**. No legitimate broker needs these.
- **Cross-check** the name and account against Bank Negara's **[Financial Consumer Alert list](https://www.bnm.gov.my/financial-fraud-alerts)** and **Semak Mule** (a "no record" result is not a guarantee of safety, only the absence of a report). If you have been scammed, call the National Scam Response Centre on **997**.

## When to use one — and the free alternatives first

Using a broker is reasonable when your case is genuinely complex (self-employed, SME, irregular income), when you have been rejected and do not know why, when you have no time to approach several banks, or when the broker is **lender-paid** so there is no direct cost to you.

But start with the **free** options, which do much of what a paid broker does:

- **imSME (imsme.com.my)** — CGC's online marketplace: one application, matched to many banks and development banks.
- **myKNP (bnm.gov.my/MyKNP)** — Bank Negara's free financing advisory, especially after a rejection.

A government-backed scheme still requires the bank's own credit assessment regardless of who packages the file, so paying a broker buys you convenience, not a different answer.

## What's next

If your file is straightforward, try imSME or apply directly before paying anyone. If it is complex and you choose an adviser, agree the fee in writing, pay nothing upfront, and verify the firm. And whether you use a broker or not, the thing that actually moves the needle is your own credit standing — so fix the fundamentals first: understand [why loans get rejected](/finance/why-loan-rejected-malaysia), [improve your credit score](/finance/improve-credit-score-malaysia), and know the difference between a licensed lender and [a loan scam](/finance/licensed-moneylenders-malaysia).
