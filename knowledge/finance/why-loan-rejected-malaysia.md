A rejection letter rarely explains itself. But bank lending in Malaysia is not a lottery — it follows one question the bank must answer before it lends: can this borrower comfortably repay, throughout the loan's life? When the answer looks shaky, you get a "no". Understand how that question is scored and most rejections become fixable.

## How a Malaysian bank assesses you

Bank Negara Malaysia (BNM) requires every bank to lend responsibly — to satisfy itself that you can afford the repayments before granting financing. Its newest **Policy Document on Personal Financing** (dated 30 September 2025) sharpens this: lenders must run an **affordability assessment**, must **obtain and verify your income**, and may **not** offer pre-approved personal financing. (From 2027, borrowers taking more than RM100,000 must also complete a financial-education module.)

In practice the bank weighs three things:

1. **Your income** — verified, documented, and stable enough to count.
2. **Your existing commitments** — captured in your debt service ratio.
3. **Your credit history** — pulled from CCRIS and from private agencies like CTOS.

## The debt service ratio (DSR)

DSR is the number that most often decides a consumer application. It is simply:

> **DSR = total monthly debt commitments ÷ monthly income**

"Total monthly debt commitments" means your existing instalments — home, car, personal and study loans, credit-card minimums — **plus the new instalment you are applying for**. Income is usually taken net of statutory deductions, though practice varies by bank.

The crucial point, often misstated: **there is no single BNM-mandated DSR cap.** Each bank sets its own ceiling under its credit policy. Commonly cited bands cluster around **60-70%**, with banks applying a lower limit to lower-income applicants and allowing a higher one for high earners. Because the proposed instalment is included, the same salary can be approved for a small loan and rejected for a large one.

## Your credit reports: CCRIS and CTOS

**CCRIS** (the Central Credit Reference Information System) is operated by Bank Negara. It is a **factual record** — your credit facilities and your repayment conduct over the **last 12 months**, including any account placed under "special attention" (an impaired or closely monitored loan). It assigns **no score**, and — a persistent myth worth killing — **Bank Negara blacklists no one** and makes no lending decision. You can obtain your own CCRIS report free through the **eCCRIS** channel.

**CTOS** is different: a **private credit reporting agency**, licensed under the **Credit Reporting Agencies Act 2010** (whose regulator is the Registrar of Credit Reporting Agencies, under the Ministry of Finance — not Bank Negara). CTOS compiles a report and a **credit score from 300 to 850**, where higher means lower risk. After a run of court cases, the **Federal Court in 2024 upheld** CTOS's right to set its own score. For the fuller picture of how these records work, see our guide to [credit reporting: CCRIS and CTOS](/finance/credit-reporting-ccris-ctos-malaysia).

## The common reasons loans are rejected

Almost every rejection traces back to one of these:

- **DSR too high** — your commitments, plus the new instalment, exceed the bank's limit. The single most common consumer reason.
- **Poor CCRIS conduct** — arrears in the rolling 12-month record, or a special-attention account.
- **A thin or empty credit file** — too little history to assess (common for first-time borrowers; shows as "no score").
- **Unverifiable income** — income the bank cannot document to its standard, acute for the self-employed and cash earners.
- **High credit utilisation** — heavy credit-card balances relative to limits, which hurts both DSR and your score.
- **Adverse records** — bankruptcy, litigation or default records surfaced through CTOS.

**For a business or SME**, add: a **young company** (many banks want two to three years of trading and accounts), **weak or unverifiable financials**, poor **personal credit of the directors or guarantors**, and **insufficient collateral or cash flow** for the facility sought.

## How to improve your chances

Rejections are usually reversible with preparation:

- **Check both reports first.** Get your CCRIS (free, via eCCRIS) and your CTOS report before applying, and fix any error or arrears.
- **Clear arrears and stay clean.** Twelve months of on-time payments materially rebuilds your profile.
- **Lower your DSR.** Pay down credit-card balances and settle small loans before applying; this is the fastest lever.
- **Document your income.** Keep payslips, bank statements, EPF statements and — for business owners — filed accounts and tax returns in order.
- **For SMEs, build a track record.** Operate a proper business account, keep financials current, and consider a [government guarantee scheme](/finance/government-sme-financing-schemes-malaysia) if collateral is the gap.
- **Use free advice.** If a bank has rejected you, Bank Negara's **myKNP** service (delivered for SMEs by CGC) will explain why and help you improve.

## What's next

Pull your CCRIS and CTOS, identify which of the reasons above applies, and fix it before reapplying rather than applying again blindly (each application leaves a footprint). If the deeper problem is debt you cannot service, look at [debt consolidation and restructuring](/finance/debt-consolidation-restructuring-malaysia) — including free help from AKPK — before taking on more credit.
