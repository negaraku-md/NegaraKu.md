  - q: "If the AI tool belongs to another company, is the data protection their problem?"
    a: "No. Under the Personal Data Protection Act 2010, your business is the data controller for the client data it decides to process, and that responsibility does not transfer to the tool's provider just because the tool is theirs. The provider acts as a processor on your behalf; you must still satisfy the PDPA principles — including ensuring the processor gives sufficient security guarantees — and you remain answerable for the data. In short: using someone else's AI does not outsource your legal responsibility."
  - q: "Does it matter where the AI tool's servers are?"

But be careful: data that can still identify someone when combined with other information may not be truly anonymous, and partial details can re-identify a person. For anything sensitive, or where you cannot strip the identifiers, treat it as personal data and apply the full checklist rather than assuming anonymisation removes the obligation."
  - q: "I signed an NDA with my client — does the PDPA checklist cover that too?"
    a: "No — treat them as separate gates you both have to pass. It takes two seconds to paste a client's email, their complaint, or a contract
full of names into a chatbot and ask it to summarise or reply. That convenience is
exactly the risk: in those two seconds a business can disclose personal data to a
third party in breach of Malaysia's data-protection law, break a confidentiality
duty it owes the client, or violate an NDA it signed — without anyone deciding to.

The useful mental model is this: **using someone else's AI tool does not outsource
your legal responsibility.** Under the **Personal Data Protection Act 2010 (PDPA,
Act 709)**, your business is the **data controller** for the client data it
chooses to process, and that responsibility stays with you even though the tool
belongs to someone else. This is a short checklist to run before the data goes in.

## 1. You still owe the seven PDPA principles

Feeding client personal data into an external AI tool is **processing** under the
PDPA, and usually a **disclosure or transfer** to the provider. So the Act's
**seven Personal Data Protection Principles** still apply. In plain terms, before
you put the data in, ask:

- **Lawful basis and notice** — do you have consent or another lawful basis, and
  did your privacy notice tell the client their data might be processed by
  third-party tools or vendors?
- **Disclosure** — sending data to an AI provider is a disclosure; is it within
  the purposes and recipients you notified?
- **Security** — is the tool secure enough, and is there a written arrangement so
  the provider (your processor) gives sufficient security guarantees?
- **Retention** — does the tool keep your inputs, for how long, and can you delete
  them? Don't let data persist longer than needed.
- **Data integrity** — AI output can be wrong or invented; you remain responsible
  for the accuracy of any personal data you rely on.
- **Access** — you must still be able to find, retrieve and correct a client's data
  on request, which is hard if it is scattered inside opaque third-party systems.

## 2. Check where the data goes — cross-border transfer

Many AI tools **store or process data on servers outside Malaysia.** Entering
client personal data into such a tool is a **cross-border transfer**, which the
PDPA regulates. Under the framework introduced by the 2024 amendment, a transfer
abroad is permitted only if **at least one condition is met** — for example the
destination provides **adequate or substantially similar protection**, the data
subject has given **explicit consent**, the transfer is **necessary for a
contract**, or you have **documented safeguards** such as contractual clauses.

So find out **where the tool processes the data**, and make sure a condition
applies — don't assume consent alone always covers it. The exact conditions sit in
the Commissioner's cross-border transfer guideline; confirm the current position
on **pdp.gov.my**.

## 3. Is any of it sensitive personal data?

The PDPA treats **sensitive personal data** — including health, religious or other
beliefs, political opinions, offences, and (since the 2024 amendment) **biometric
data** — more strictly, generally requiring **explicit consent** among narrower
grounds. Do **not** feed health records, religious information, biometric data or
similar into a general AI tool without explicit consent and a careful read of the
tool's terms. The bar is higher here, and the harm from a leak is greater.

## 4. Read the provider's own terms — not the marketing

Whether a tool is safe to use with client data is answered by its **Terms of
Service, Privacy Policy and any Data Processing Addendum**, not its homepage. The
durable questions to ask of **any** tool:

- **Training:** does the provider use your inputs to **train or improve its
  models**, and is there an opt-out or a tier where training is off by default?
- **Retention and deletion:** how long are inputs and outputs kept, and can you
  delete them (and copies)?
- **Sub-processors and location:** who are the sub-processors, and **which
  countries** are the servers in? (back to the cross-border check.)
- **Business tier:** is there a **business or enterprise plan with a data-processing
  agreement**, contractual confidentiality and a no-training commitment? Free and
  consumer tiers often lack these.
- **Security and breach:** encryption, access controls, recognised security
  standards, and a commitment to **notify you of a breach** in time to meet your
  own obligations.

## 5. PDPA is not the only duty — confidentiality and NDAs

Even when the PDPA is satisfied, a separate duty may still forbid putting the data
in. Many professionals owe **confidentiality obligations independent of, and often
stricter than, the PDPA** — for example lawyers under the **Legal Profession Act
1976**, accountants under the **Malaysian Institute of Accountants (MIA)**
by-laws, doctors under the **Malaysian Medical Council** code, and licensed
financial institutions under the **Financial Services Act 2013**. And a
**confidentiality clause or NDA** in your client engagement can independently
prohibit disclosing their information to any third party, including an AI provider,
without consent. **Check both** the data-protection position and your
confidentiality obligations.

## The safe default

When in doubt, do less:

- **Minimise and anonymise first** — strip names, identity-card numbers, contact
  and account details, and put in only what the task truly needs.
- **Use a business tier** with a data-processing agreement, no-training terms and
  defined retention when any client data is involved.
- **Update your notice or get consent** where third-party processing isn't already
  covered.
- **Keep a record** — which tool, what data, what basis, what safeguards — so you
  can show accountability and answer an access request.
- **Write a short internal AI-use policy** telling staff what may and may not go
  into AI tools, which tools are approved, and the anonymisation rules.

## A note on the 2024 amendment

The PDPA was amended in **2024**, with changes phased in across **2025**: mandatory
**data breach notification**, a duty to appoint a **Data Protection Officer (DPO)**
for larger-scale processing, a **data portability** right, and the **new
cross-border transfer** framework above. The exact in-force dates, the DPO
thresholds and any penalty figures are moving and partly set by guidelines rather
than the Act itself — so treat the shape of the duties as durable, and **confirm
the current detail on pdp.gov.my** before relying on a specific figure or deadline.
