Walk into any phone shop in Malaysia, hand over cash for a SIM card, and the
staff will still stop you before you can use it. They need to see your MyKad —
or, if you're not a citizen, your original passport. Skip that step and the
line simply won't activate. This isn't a shop policy; it's a national rule
enforced by the telecom regulator, and it catches new arrivals more often than
almost anything else about getting connected.

## Prepaid or postpaid — the real difference

**Prepaid** means you load credit or buy a data pack before you use it. There's
no contract, no credit check and no monthly bill — when the credit runs out,
the line stops working until you top up again. It's the option most people in
Malaysia use, and it's also the version foreigners and short-term visitors
almost always end up with, since it needs no local billing history.

**Postpaid** bills you at the end of a monthly cycle for what you've used,
typically bundled with a fixed monthly fee, more data, and sometimes a phone
on an instalment plan. It usually requires a longer-term commitment and a
credit or income check, which is one reason it's a harder sell to someone
who's only just arrived.

## The three national networks

| Operator | Prepaid brand | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| CelcomDigi | Prepaid 5G (Edisi Biru / Edisi Kuning) | Formed by the merger of Celcom and Digi |
| Maxis | Hotlink | Runs its own postpaid and Hotlink prepaid lines |
| U Mobile | Unlimited Funz Prepaid | Malaysia's third national network operator |

Two smaller players — **Yes** (YTL Communications) and **Unifi Mobile**
(Telekom Malaysia) — run their own 4G/LTE networks alongside the three above,
often bundled with home broadband. Whichever operator you choose, the
registration requirement below applies identically.

## Registration is mandatory — and it's free

The Malaysian Communications and Multimedia Commission (MCMC) requires every
prepaid SIM to be registered against a valid identity document before it can
be activated, under powers in the Communications and Multimedia Act 1998.
Registration costs nothing — anyone asking you to pay for it is not following
the rules.

What you need depends on who you are:

- **Malaysian citizens** — MyKad
- **Permanent residents** — MyPR card
- **Armed forces personnel** — MyTentera card
- **Foreign nationals** — original passport, plus work permit or student ID
  where applicable, and your visa if one was required for entry

Photocopies, photos and scanned images are not accepted — the dealer must
check the original document, and registration must go through an approved
automated system (OCR scanning, biometric verification or a MyKad reader).
Manual registration by SMS is no longer permitted anywhere.

A **Mandatory Standard** MCMC registered on 26 February 2026 tightened the
regime further. Citizens, permanent residents and temporary residents may
still hold up to five prepaid SIM cards per service provider, but non-citizens
are now capped at two per provider. Children under 12 cannot have a prepaid
SIM registered in their name at all; for those aged 12 to 17, a parent or
legal guardian must complete the registration and have their own consent
recorded.

## Common mistakes

- **Assuming a photocopy of your passport will do.** It won't — dealers are
  required to sight the original.
- **Buying "pre-registered" SIMs from an unlicensed street vendor.** These
  sidestep the registration rule entirely and are exactly what the tightened
  standard targets; the line can be suspended or terminated once irregular
  registration is detected.
- **Registering a child's SIM in your own name to get around the age rule.**
  It defeats the purpose of the rule and can flag your own account for review
  if you're already near your card limit.
- **Expecting postpaid without a local track record.** Newly arrived
  foreigners are usually steered to prepaid first; postpaid tends to follow
  once you have a longer residence and payment history in Malaysia.
