Most people meet Malaysia's income tax as a single line on a payslip — "PCB" — and a once-a-year scramble in April. Behind that line sits one of the most consequential systems in the country, and almost everything written about it starts in the wrong place: a rate table.

Rates are the last thing you need. First you need to know whether you are taxed at all, on what, as whom, and by when. This page is the map. It covers the whole system at a glance and then points you to the detailed guide for your exact question.

## One law, one authority

Malaysia's income tax runs on a single statute: the **Income Tax Act 1967** (Act 53). It is administered by the **Inland Revenue Board of Malaysia** — *Lembaga Hasil Dalam Negeri*, which you will see written as **LHDN** or, in its branded form, **HASiL**. (Indirect taxes like SST, customs and excise are a different authority, the Royal Malaysian Customs Department — not covered here.)

Everything that follows — personal tax, corporate tax, withholding tax, the reliefs, the penalties — is a branch of that one Act. When a rule feels arbitrary, it is usually a specific section of Act 53 doing its job.

## What is actually taxed: chargeable income

You are never taxed on your salary, and a company is never taxed on its turnover. Tax is charged on **chargeable income**: gross income, less allowable expenses, less deductions, less every relief you qualify for.

Income is grouped into classes under section 4 of the Act — employment, business, dividends, interest, rents, royalties, pensions and so on. Each is assessed for a **year of assessment (YA)**, which in Malaysia follows the calendar year, 1 January to 31 December. So "YA2025" means income for the 2025 calendar year, filed in 2026.

That one idea — tax on *chargeable* income, per *year of assessment* — is the spine of the whole system.

## Who pays: residents, non-residents and the 182-day test

Residency decides your **rate** and whether you get **reliefs** — it does not decide whether you are in scope. And residency in Malaysia is a **day count, not a nationality or visa question**.

The main rule, under **section 7 of the Income Tax Act 1967**, is the 182-day test: an individual present in Malaysia for **182 days or more in a calendar year** is generally a tax resident for that year. The days need not be consecutive, and the statute adds further tests that can make someone resident on fewer days — for example by linking a period to a 182-day stretch in the adjoining year, or by a 90-day test tied to residence in surrounding years.

The practical consequences are large:

- A **resident individual** is taxed on the progressive scale (0% to 30%) and can claim personal reliefs and rebates.
- A **non-resident individual** is taxed at a **flat 30%**, with no personal reliefs and no rebate.

This is why expatriates so often pay 30% in their arrival year and the resident scale thereafter: they simply have not yet accumulated 182 days. LHDN publishes a dedicated guidance document on the tax treatment of residents versus non-residents setting out the tests in detail.

## Personal tax or corporate tax?

Both flow from the same Act, but they behave very differently. The single most useful distinction to hold in your head:

| | Personal income tax | Corporate income tax |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who | Individuals (employed, self-employed, partners) | Companies and certain other entities |
| Rate shape | Progressive 0% to 30% (resident); flat 30% (non-resident) | Flat 24% standard; reduced rates for qualifying resident SMEs |
| Reliefs | Personal reliefs, rebates, deductions apply | No personal reliefs; business deductions and allowances instead |
| Main return | Form BE (no business income), B (business income), M (non-resident) | Form C |
| Pre-payment | Monthly PCB/MTD for employees | CP204 estimate, paid in monthly instalments |
| Deadline | 30 April / 30 June following the year | Within 7 months of financial year-end |

Most individuals only ever touch the left column; most business owners end up dealing with both, once in their own name and once for the company.

## How much: the rate structure

Headline numbers move with each Budget, so this page describes the **shape** and links you to the live rate tables rather than freezing a schedule that will date.

**Personal (resident).** A stepped, progressive scale: the first slice of chargeable income is taxed at 0%, and each higher band carries a higher marginal rate up to a top rate of **30%**. Because it is stepped, the top rate applies only to the ringgit above the threshold, never to the whole income. The exact bands — and the restructuring announced for YA2027 — live in the dedicated guide.

**Personal (non-resident).** A flat **30%** on chargeable income, with specific lower rates for a few categories such as approved knowledge workers and returning experts.

**Corporate.** The standard rate is **24%**. A resident company that qualifies as an **SME** pays reduced rates on a first tranche of chargeable income before 24% applies above it, subject to paid-up capital and gross income caps and a foreign-ownership restriction. The precise tiers and eligibility tests are in the corporate rate guide.

A separate **dividend layer** now applies to high individual dividend income, and various sector-specific regimes (petroleum, Labuan) sit outside the ordinary scales entirely.

> For the actual bands and figures, go straight to [Personal income tax rates](/en/taxation/personal-tax-rates/) and [Corporate tax rates](/en/taxation/corporate-tax-rates/). Describing them in prose would only go stale.

## Reliefs, rebates and deductions — the three levers

For individuals, three different mechanisms reduce the final bill, and they are routinely confused:

- **Deductions and reliefs** reduce your **income** before the rate is applied (EPF and life insurance, education and medical, lifestyle, childcare and so on). A relief of RM1,000 saves you tax at your marginal rate, not the full RM1,000.
- **Rebates** reduce the **tax itself**, after it is computed — for example the RM400 rebate for lower chargeable income, and the zakat and fitrah rebate.
- For businesses, the equivalent levers are **allowable expenses**, **capital allowances** and specific **double deductions** and incentives.

Getting the order right matters: reliefs decide which band you land in; rebates then knock money off the tax that band produces.

## Paying as you earn: PCB / MTD

Employees rarely write LHDN a large cheque in April, because they have been paying all year. **Monthly Tax Deduction (MTD)** — in Malay, *Potongan Cukai Bulanan*, or **PCB** — requires employers to deduct an estimated amount of tax from each salary payment and remit it to LHDN.

By design, PCB over the year should land close to the final liability. The annual return then **reconciles** the difference: you pay a small balance or claim a refund. For many employees whose only income is employment and whose reliefs are standard, PCB can even be treated as final, letting them skip filing under specific conditions.

The mechanics — the schedule, how reliefs feed into it, and when PCB counts as final tax — are in the dedicated PCB guide.

## Filing: self-assessment, MyTax and the forms

Malaysia runs a **self-assessment system (SAS)**. You compute your own chargeable income and tax, file the return, and pay any balance. Crucially, your submitted return is **deemed to be the notice of assessment** — LHDN does not send you one first. Companies moved to self-assessment in 2001 and individuals in 2004.

Filing is electronic, through the **MyTax** portal (the e-Filing system). The main returns:

| You are | Form | Due by |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Resident individual, no business income | BE | 30 April (following year) |
| Resident individual, with business income | B | 30 June (following year) |
| Non-resident individual | M | 30 April / 30 June |
| Company | C | Within 7 months of financial year-end |
| Employer annual return | E (and EA statements to staff) | 31 March |

Companies also file a **CP204** estimate of tax payable before the basis period begins and pay it in monthly instalments, with chances to revise the estimate mid-year. The company-side mechanics are covered in the Form C and CP204 guide.

## Foreign-source income (FSI)

Malaysia taxes on a **broadly territorial** basis: income sourced **in** Malaysia is the core charge. For years, foreign-source income received in Malaysia by residents was effectively untaxed — then that treatment changed, bringing remitted FSI into charge, with a **conditional exemption** carved out for resident individuals for a defined window (reported as running to **31 December 2036**, subject to conditions such as the income having been taxed abroad).

Because this area has shifted repeatedly and the end date has been extended more than once, treat any single figure with caution and confirm the current position before acting. The dedicated FSI guide tracks the live treatment.

## Special cases worth flagging

The ordinary rules bend for several common situations:

- **Rental income** is taxable, but the deductible-expenses rules (and when a let becomes a "business") have their own logic.
- **Expatriates and non-residents** face the flat 30%, residence-linking quirks, and double-tax-agreement relief.
- **Petroleum income** is taxed under a separate statute (the Petroleum (Income Tax) Act 1967) at its own rate, not under the ordinary corporate scale.
- **Labuan** entities and **REITs** run on distinct regimes.

Each of these has a specialist guide; the routing table below points you to them.

## LHDN's role and the cost of getting it wrong

LHDN registers taxpayers, runs MyTax, processes returns and refunds, and enforces the Act through **audits** and **investigations**. Under self-assessment the honesty burden sits with you, and the back-stop is penalties.

Penalties apply across the board: **late filing**, **late payment**, **under-estimation** of a company's CP204, **incorrect returns**, and — at the serious end — **wilful evasion**, which carries both financial penalties and potential prosecution. LHDN can generally raise an additional assessment within **five years** of the relevant year of assessment (longer for transfer pricing, and with no limit in cases of fraud or wilful default). The detail lives in the tax penalties guide.

## Common mistakes

- **Confusing residence with citizenship.** A Malaysian abroad can be non-resident; a foreigner here long enough is resident. It is a day count under section 7.
- **Taxing the whole income at the top rate.** The resident scale is stepped; the marginal rate hits only the ringgit above each threshold.
- **Mixing up reliefs and rebates.** Reliefs cut income before the rate; rebates cut the tax after it. They are not interchangeable.
- **Assuming PCB is always final.** It usually approximates the bill, but filing still reconciles it — and most people with reliefs, side income or refunds must file.
- **Missing the business-income deadline.** Business income pushes your individual deadline from 30 April to 30 June, but it is still a deadline, not a reprieve.
- **Treating foreign income as automatically tax-free.** The territorial rule has narrowed; remitted FSI and its conditional exemption need checking, not assuming.
