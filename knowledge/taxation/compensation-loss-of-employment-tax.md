faq:
  - q: "Is compensation for loss of employment taxable in Malaysia?"
    a: "Only in part, and often not at all. A payment that is genuinely compensation for the loss of your employment is a capital receipt brought to tax under section 13(1)(e) of the Income Tax Act 1967, but paragraph 15 of Schedule 6 then exempts it. If the Director General is satisfied the loss of employment is due to ill health, it is fully exempt. Otherwise RM10,000 is exempt for each completed year of service with the same employer or companies in the same group (for terminations on or after 1 July 2008 — it was RM6,000 before that), and only the balance is taxed. See LHDN Public Ruling No. 1/2012."
  - q: "What is the difference between compensation for loss of employment and gratuity or salary in arrears?"
    a: "Substance decides, not the label. Compensation for loss of employment is paid because the job — the source of income — has ended; it is a capital receipt eligible for the paragraph 15 exemption. Arrears of salary, bonuses and contractual gratuity are rewards for services rendered, so they are ordinary taxable employment income. Gratuity has its own separate exemptions (for example on retirement at 55 or after ten years of service), but it does not use the paragraph 15 loss-of-employment exemption. Payment in lieu of notice, which stands in for salary you would have earned during the notice period, is generally taxed as employment income."
  - q: "How is the RM10,000 per year exemption calculated?"
    a: "Multiply RM10,000 by the number of completed years of service with the same employer or with companies in the same group. Only completed years count — a part year is ignored. So an employee with 8 years and 7 months of service gets 8 x RM10,000 = RM80,000 exempt. If the compensation is RM150,000, then RM70,000 is added to that year's taxable income. This is the partial exemption in paragraph 15 of Schedule 6 of the Income Tax Act 1967."
  - q: "Are VSS or mutual separation scheme payments tax-exempt?"
    a: "Yes — a payment under a Voluntary Separation Scheme (VSS) or Mutual Separation Scheme (MSS) is treated as compensation for loss of employment and gets the paragraph 15 exemption (full for ill health, otherwise RM10,000 per completed year of service). But LHDN denies the exemption where the scheme is not a genuine loss of employment — for instance where the person is re-employed by the same or a related employer under an arrangement, or where the recipient is a director of a controlled company who is not a full-time service director. See Public Ruling No. 1/2012."
  - q: "What must the employer report when it pays out a termination package?"
    a: "The employer reports the payment, split into its exempt and taxable parts, in the employee's Form EA (C.P.8A) for the year, and the taxable portion is included in the employee's income for that year. Losing your job usually comes with a payout, and the first question is often the wrong one: "how much tax will I pay on it?" The right question is "how much of this is actually *compensation for losing my job* — and how much is just my normal pay in disguise?" Malaysian tax law draws a hard line between the two, and only one side of that line gets the generous exemption. Get the split wrong and you either overpay tax or get a nasty assessment later.

Here is how a termination payout is taxed in Malaysia, and how to work out the exempt part.

## Why the type of payment matters more than the amount

A payout on leaving a job is rarely one thing. It is usually a bundle: outstanding salary, a bonus, perhaps a contractual gratuity, pay in lieu of notice, and a lump sum for the fact that the job is ending. Tax treats these very differently, because Malaysian income tax is a tax on **income from a source** — and the job is the source.

- Payments that **reward the service you gave** are income *from* that source: arrears of salary, bonus, and contractual gratuity. These are ordinary **taxable employment income**.
- A payment for the **loss of the source itself** — the fact that the employment has come to an end — is a **capital receipt**, not a reward for work. It is still brought into charge (by **section 13(1)(e)** of the Income Tax Act 1967, which taxes any amount received "in respect of loss of employment"), but it then gets a special exemption that ordinary pay does not.

So the exercise is not "how big is the cheque?" but "how much of the cheque is genuinely compensation for loss of employment?" That portion is what qualifies for the exemption below; the rest is taxed like any other salary.

## What actually counts as "compensation for loss of employment"

LHDN sets out the treatment in **Public Ruling No. 1/2012, *Compensation for Loss of Employment***. Broadly, a payment qualifies if it is made by the employer to compensate the employee **because the employment is ending** — retrenchment or redundancy pay, severance, an ex-gratia "goodbye" lump sum, or a payout under a **Voluntary Separation Scheme (VSS)** or **Mutual Separation Scheme (MSS)**.

Payments that are **not** compensation for loss of employment — and therefore stay fully taxable as employment income — include:

- **arrears of salary** and unpaid wages;
- **bonus** and incentive payments earned for work done;
- **contractual gratuity** (a long-service reward — this has its *own* separate exemptions on retirement, but not the loss-of-employment one); and
- **payment in lieu of notice**, which substitutes for the salary you would have earned during the notice period, and so is generally taxed as employment income.

The label the employer puts on a line item does not settle it — LHDN looks at the substance of *why* the payment is made.

## The two exemptions in Schedule 6, Paragraph 15

Once you have isolated the genuine compensation-for-loss-of-employment amount, **paragraph 15 of Schedule 6** of the Income Tax Act 1967 (Exemptions From Tax) exempts it — in one of two ways.

| Situation | Exemption |
|---|---|
| The Director General is satisfied the loss of employment is **due to ill health** | **Full exemption** — the whole compensation is tax-free |
| Any other reason (retrenchment, redundancy, VSS/MSS, etc.) | **RM10,000 for each completed year of service** with the same employer or with companies in the same group |

Two points on the partial exemption:

- The **RM10,000** figure applies to terminations **on or after 1 July 2008**; for terminations before that date the exemption was RM6,000 per completed year of service.
- Only **completed** years count — a part year is dropped. Service is counted with the **same employer** *or* with **companies in the same group**, so moving between group companies does not reset the clock.

For the full ill-health exemption, the Director General must be satisfied the loss is genuinely due to ill health, which in practice means supporting medical certification.

## When the exemption is denied

Paragraph 15 is not a blanket giveaway. The exemption (including for VSS/MSS) does **not** apply where, per Public Ruling No. 1/2012:

- the recipient is a **director of a controlled company who is not a full-time service director** — controlling directors cannot shelter a payout to themselves this way; or
- the "separation" is **not a genuine loss of employment** — for example the scheme provides for the person to be **re-employed** by the same or a related employer, so the job has not really ended.

If either applies, the payment is taxed in full.

## Worked examples

**1. Small package, fully covered.** Aisha is retrenched after **12 completed years** with one employer and receives **RM120,000** as severance (no ill health). Her exemption is 12 x RM10,000 = **RM120,000**. The whole amount is exempt; **RM0** is taxable.

**2. Larger package, partial exemption.** Ben is retrenched after **8 completed years** (8 years, 7 months — the extra months are ignored) and receives **RM150,000** in loss-of-employment compensation. His exemption is 8 x RM10,000 = **RM80,000**. The remaining **RM70,000** is added to his employment income for the year and taxed at his marginal rate.

**3. Ill health — fully exempt.** Chandra's employment ends because of certified ill health, and she receives **RM200,000**. Because the Director General is satisfied the loss is due to ill health, the **entire RM200,000 is exempt**, regardless of her years of service.

**4. Mixed bundle — split it first.** Dinesh, with **6 completed years**, receives a package of **RM30,000 salary arrears + RM20,000 pay in lieu of notice + RM100,000 severance**. Only the **RM100,000** severance is compensation for loss of employment. Its exemption is 6 x RM10,000 = **RM60,000**, leaving **RM40,000** taxable. The RM30,000 arrears and RM20,000 notice pay are **fully taxable** employment income. So his taxable amount from the package is RM40,000 + RM50,000 = **RM90,000**.

## What the employer has to report

The employer, not the employee, does most of the mechanics:

- **Form EA (C.P.8A).** The employer records the payment in the employee's annual Form EA, showing the **exempt** and **taxable** portions separately; the taxable portion is included in the employee's income for that year of assessment.
- **Cessation notification (Form CP22A).** A private-sector employer must notify LHDN that the employee is about to cease employment — generally by filing **Form CP22A at least 30 days before** the cessation date.
- **Withholding and tax clearance.** The employer should **withhold monies** due to the departing employee pending a **tax clearance letter (Surat Penyelesaian Cukai)** from LHDN before releasing the final pay, so that any tax owed can be settled.

For the employee, the figures then flow into the annual return; keep the employer's breakdown of the package, because the exempt-versus-taxable split is exactly what LHDN will want to see.

## What's next

Before you accept or pay out a termination package, do the split *first*: identify the part that is genuinely compensation for loss of employment, apply the paragraph 15 exemption (full for certified ill health, otherwise RM10,000 per completed year of service), and treat everything else — arrears, bonus, gratuity, notice pay — as ordinary taxable income. Watch the two traps that void the exemption: controlling-director recipients and non-genuine schemes with re-employment built in.

Because the exemption turns on completed years of service and the same-group rule, keep clean records of the employment history and the reason for the termination. For how the underlying payout is calculated in the first place, see the related guide on termination and lay-off benefits. And because a large one-off sum can push you into a higher band for the year, confirm the final tax position — or an ill-health full exemption — with LHDN or a tax agent before signing off.
