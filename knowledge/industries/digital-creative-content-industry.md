When Les' Copaque's _Upin & Ipin Universe_ reached consoles worldwide on **17 July 2025**, it closed a loop that began with a short animated series about two mischievous twins. A Malaysian cartoon had become a globally published video game — and a neat illustration of what the country's digital creative content industry has quietly built over two decades.

This sector is not film, and it is not broadcast news. It is the creation of digital content and intellectual property: **video games, animation, visual effects (VFX) and esports**. For traditional cinema and for television, radio and news, see the [media & broadcasting industry](/en/industries/media-broadcasting-industry/). What follows is strictly the digital creative side.

## What the industry covers

Malaysia's digital content industry is substantial. According to the [Malaysia Digital Economy Corporation (MDEC)](https://mdec.my/media-release/news-press-release/384/malaysia-targets-stronger-global-stage-presence-with-fresh-funding-for-games,-animation-and-metaverse), it has generated roughly **RM87.25 billion** in cumulative revenue and **RM11.18 billion** in export sales since 2011, employing some **11,154** Malaysians. The narrower "digital creative" slice contributed around **RM5.3 billion** and more than **10,000 jobs** in 2023 alone, per [reporting on MDEC data](https://www.thevibes.com/articles/news/110105/digital-creative-industry-nets-rm5.3-billion-and-over-10000-jobs-in-2023). Exports matter here more than in most industries: Malaysian studios sell art, animation and services to clients abroad, so the sector earns foreign income rather than merely substituting imports.

## Games

Game development in Malaysia runs along two tracks. The first is **original IP** — studios building and owning their own titles. The flagship example is the _Upin & Ipin Universe_ console game, which Les' Copaque developed with [Streamline Studios](https://streamline-studios.com/post/announcing-upin-ipin-universe) and released across Switch, PlayStation and Windows; Streamline says more than **100 people** worked on it over roughly two years, with the core team based in Malaysia.

The second, larger track is **co-development and art outsourcing** for global studios. **Lemon Sky Studios**, based in Shah Alam, is the best-known name, having produced 3D environment art and assets for AAA titles such as _Elden Ring_ and _Final Fantasy VII Remake_. This work is less visible to the public but is where much of the export revenue is earned — Malaysian talent costs are competitive, English proficiency is high, and time zones align usefully with both Asian and Western clients.

The market itself is growing. In a January 2026 parliamentary reply, the government cited projections of Malaysia's video game market revenue at about **USD 712 million in 2024**, rising to **USD 824 million by 2028**. Rising smartphone and broadband penetration — see the [telecommunications industry](/en/industries/telecommunications-industry/) — underpins that demand.

## Animation & VFX

Animation is Malaysia's heritage strength. **Les' Copaque** turned _Upin & Ipin_ into a franchise with international broadcast reach, feature films and licensed merchandise, proving that locally owned IP could travel. Other studios — among them **Giggle Garage** and a cluster of boutique houses — produce series and short-form content for domestic and regional broadcasters and streaming platforms.

VFX and post-production form a related services layer. Malaysian facilities handle visual effects, compositing and finishing for advertising, series and features, often as part of the same outsourcing economy that games art taps. As with games, the business model blends original production with service work for overseas clients, and it draws on the same pool of 3D artists, riggers and technical directors.

## Esports

Esports is the newest pillar and the one with the clearest government backing. Malaysia recognises competitive gaming as a discipline, and esports has featured as a medal event at the SEA Games. The **Ministry of Youth and Sports (KBS)** leads here: **Budget 2020** allocated **RM20 million** to esports, tied to a five-year Esports Strategic Development Plan, and in 2020 KBS established the agency **[Esports Integrated (ESI)](https://www.thestar.com.my/tech/tech-news/2020/10/01/new-kbs-agency-to-drive-malaysias-national-esports-roadmap)** to drive a national roadmap, act as a catalyst much as the National Sports Council does for traditional sport, and develop athletes and a league structure. MDEC complements this on the industry side, connecting developers, tournament organisers, sponsors and content creators.

## Institutions & incentives

The central institution is **MDEC**. Its principal instrument is the **Malaysia Digital (MD) status** scheme, launched on **4 July 2022** to replace the Multimedia Super Corridor (MSC Malaysia, introduced in 1996). MD status is awarded to qualifying digital companies and, unlike the old MSC regime, is not tied to physical "cybercity" locations; recipients must typically employ at least two knowledge workers on a minimum RM5,000 average monthly salary, and an outcome-based **MD Tax Incentive** was introduced on **31 May 2024**.

On top of status, MDEC runs targeted grants. In **April 2025** it announced roughly **RM5.7 million** in new funding: a **Digital Games Testbed Programme** (RM3.5 million for at least five studios, up to RM700,000 each), an **Animation Shorts Challenge** (RM1.2 million for 12 participants and 12 new IPs), and a **Business in Metaverse Programme** (RM1 million for 200 businesses). These sit alongside earlier content-grant mechanisms and the equity role historically played by MyCreative Ventures, the government's creative-industry investment arm. Investment promotion and higher-value incentives are coordinated with MIDA, and talent flows from local universities, art colleges and specialist academies into studios that also serve the [global business services industry](/en/industries/global-business-services-industry/).

## Outlook

The headline ambition is to make Malaysia a **regional and global hub for digital content creation by 2030**. The foundations are real: proven IP, a mature art-outsourcing base, government funding and a recognised esports scene. The challenges are equally clear. Talent retention is the hardest — skilled artists and developers are mobile and courted by higher-paying studios in Singapore, Japan and the West. Scaling from service work to owning and monetising original IP is the other test, since outsourcing earns revenue but captures less long-term value than a hit franchise. If Malaysia can keep its talent and turn more service capacity into owned IP, the digital creative content sector could become one of the more durable exporters in the country's digital economy.
