Few people realise that when an American homeowner or utility installs a solar array, the panels may well have been made in Malaysia. For more than a decade the country has quietly been one of the world's largest producers of solar photovoltaic (PV) cells and modules — not to power its own grid, but to export. This is an industry of factories, not feed-in tariffs.

## A manufacturing story, not an energy story

It is worth separating two different "solar Malaysias". One is the domestic push to generate clean electricity through schemes such as Large Scale Solar and Net Energy Metering — covered under [solar energy in Malaysia](/en/energy/solar-energy-malaysia/). The other, the subject here, is the **export manufacturing** sector: plants that fabricate wafers, solar cells and finished modules and ship them abroad. The two overlap only loosely. Malaysia makes far more PV capacity than it installs, and most of its output has historically left the country — above all for the United States.

By 2022 Malaysia had become the world's third-largest PV module manufacturer, with roughly 2.8% of global output, behind only China and Vietnam. That is a striking position for a mid-sized economy, and it was built deliberately.

## Why Malaysia

Several ingredients came together. First, a mature **electrical & electronics (E&E) base**: the same industrial parks, utilities, logistics and engineering talent that made Malaysia a global [semiconductor industry](/en/industries/semiconductor-industry/) hub translate neatly to solar cell fabrication, which is a cousin of chipmaking. Both etch and process silicon wafers in cleanrooms, so suppliers, equipment vendors and trained technicians were already on hand.

Second, **investment incentives**. The Malaysian Investment Development Authority (MIDA), under the Ministry of Investment, Trade and Industry (MITI), promotes and approves manufacturing projects and administers the headline tax breaks — Pioneer Status (a period of corporate-income-tax exemption) and the Investment Tax Allowance on qualifying capital expenditure, sometimes topped up with bespoke, negotiated packages for large investors.

Third, **competitive, reliable electricity and land**, English-speaking engineers, and political stability. And fourth — the decisive factor for the export model — **access to the US market**. For years, panels assembled in South-east Asia could enter the United States without the anti-dumping duties levied on Chinese goods, making Malaysia an attractive production base for Chinese and Western firms alike.

## Who makes panels here, and where

The industry clusters in a handful of states. **Penang** (the E&E heartland) hosts Chinese majors including Jinko Solar and JA Solar. **Kulim, in Kedah**, is home to the US firm First Solar, whose thin-film plants are among the largest single investments. **Melaka** has been a cell-manufacturing centre (the SunPower/Maxeon lineage), and the **Klang Valley/Selangor** area hosts Hanwha Q Cells. Sarawak has also drawn investment, including by LONGi near Kuching.

The ownership mix matters. Analysts at Wood Mackenzie estimated that Chinese-linked firms accounted for close to **80%** of Malaysia's solar production capacity in 2024, with the remainder led by First Solar and Hanwha Q Cells. That concentration — and the fact that most output was bound for the US — is exactly what has now made the sector so vulnerable.

## The US trade shock

The export model rested on duty-free US access, and that foundation has cracked. In **June 2024** the United States ended the tariff exemption that had shielded South-east Asian solar products. Then, in a sweeping anti-dumping (AD) and countervailing duty (CVD) case against Cambodia, **Malaysia**, Thailand and Vietnam, the US Department of Commerce finalised punitive rates in **April 2025**, and the US International Trade Commission confirmed injury to the domestic industry, with orders reported to take effect in June 2025.

The numbers are severe, though they vary sharply by company and country. Reported final **countervailing** rates ranged from about 14.64% to over 3,400% (the extreme figures falling on Cambodian firms that withdrew from the case); in Malaysia, Hanwha Q Cells was assessed around 14.64% and Jinko-related firms around 38.38%. **Anti-dumping** rates ran from roughly 0% up to 271%. Stacked together, the combined burden on some Malaysian exporters has been cited in the region of 14% to 250%. Because these duties change as reviews proceed, the exact figures should always be checked against the US Federal Register.

The effect was immediate. By **January 2025**, reports described Jinko, Risen Energy and JA Solar scaling back or closing Malaysian plants — together a large share of national capacity — and LONGi shelving expansion. For factories built to serve America, tariffs of this size can wipe out the business case overnight.

## Outlook and diversification

The sector is not collapsing, but it is being reshaped. Firms less exposed to the US — and those, like First Solar, that also sell into the American market from US-based plants — are better placed. The clearest strategic response is **market diversification**: Malaysian PV exports to **India** rose sharply in 2024, partly offsetting the fall in US shipments, and other emerging markets are being courted.

Longer term, Malaysia's advantage remains its industrial ecosystem rather than any single customer. The same strengths that underpin its semiconductor and emerging [electric-vehicle battery industry](/en/industries/electric-vehicle-battery-industry/) — supply chains, skills and incentives — give it a durable base in advanced manufacturing, even as the solar map is redrawn by geopolitics. The challenge for policymakers is to keep the factories, jobs and know-how while the industry's end-markets shift beneath it.
