Almost everyone in Malaysia carries a mobile connection, and most homes have some form of broadband. That makes telecommunications one of the country's most important and most mature industries — the invisible plumbing beneath the digital economy, online banking, e-government and everyday life. Yet behind the familiar brand names sits a market that has changed dramatically in recent years: fewer operators, bigger mergers and a national experiment in how to build 5G.

This guide explains what the telecommunications sector is, who the main operators are, how the market is structured after a wave of consolidation, the split between mobile and fixed broadband, and how the whole thing is regulated. (For the technology and policy of 5G itself, see the dedicated [5G and JENDELA guide](/en/technology/5g-jendela-dnb-malaysia/); this page focuses on the operators and the market.)

## What the sector covers

Telecommunications in Malaysia spans mobile (cellular) services, fixed-line voice, fixed broadband (mostly fibre), wholesale network infrastructure, and the satellite and submarine-cable links that connect the country to the world. Under Malaysian law these all sit inside a single converged "communications and multimedia" industry that also reaches into broadcasting and online content — a deliberate choice made when the sector was reorganised in the late 1990s.

The industry is a cornerstone of the broader [digital economy](/en/industries/digital-economy-overview/), providing the connectivity that every other digital service depends on.

## A near-saturated market

Malaysia is a highly connected country. Mobile penetration is well above 100% on a SIM basis — many people carry more than one SIM — and both mobile broadband and fixed (fibre) broadband are now widely adopted, according to the MCMC's statistics publications. The precise rates shift every quarter and should be checked against the MCMC Pocket Book of Statistics (see the verification note), but the overall picture is clear: this is a mature market.

That maturity shapes the industry's economics. With few new subscribers left to sign up, operators compete on price, speed, bundling and service quality rather than on raw growth. Future expansion is expected to come from higher-value services — 5G, faster fibre, enterprise and cloud connectivity, and digital add-ons — rather than from a rising headcount of users.

## Operators and market structure

The defining trend of the past few years has been **consolidation**. The mobile market, once a four-player field, is now effectively a three-player one.

The pivotal event was the **Celcom–Digi merger**, completed on **30 November 2022**, which combined Axiata's Celcom and Telenor's Digi into **CelcomDigi** — Malaysia's largest mobile operator by subscribers. Axiata and Telenor each hold about 33% of the merged company. At the merger announcement the combined group was described in FY2021 pro forma terms as serving roughly 20 million customers on about RM13.0 billion of revenue, though those were estimates rather than actual post-merger results.

The main mobile operators today are:

- **CelcomDigi** — the largest by subscribers, jointly controlled by Axiata and Telenor.
- **Maxis** — the long-established premium operator and second-largest mobile player.
- **U Mobile** — the smaller but fast-moving challenger, now central to the 5G story (below).

Alongside these run a layer of **mobile virtual network operators (MVNOs)** and prepaid brands (such as those riding on the big networks) that add competition at the budget end.

On the fixed side, the market leader is **Telekom Malaysia (TM)**, the former state monopoly, whose **unifi** brand dominates fixed broadband and which also owns much of the national backbone and international connectivity. TM competes with **Maxis Fibre**, **TIME dotCom** (strong in high-rise and urban fibre) and **YTL Communications**, which runs the **Yes** brand. Several operators now sell **converged** packages — mobile and home broadband bundled together — which is a major front in the competition for household spending.

## Mobile versus fixed broadband

The two halves of the market behave differently.

**Mobile broadband** is nearly universal and intensely price-competitive, delivered over 4G almost everywhere and increasingly 5G. It is the primary way most Malaysians get online, especially outside the big cities.

**Fixed broadband** is overwhelmingly fibre today, having moved well beyond the old copper-based Streamyx era. TM's unifi is the clear leader, with Maxis and TIME as the main challengers; brokerage estimates have put unifi's share of the fixed-broadband market around two-thirds, but those figures are undated and should be verified. Fixed broadband penetration is high in urban areas but thinner in rural ones — closing that gap is a continuing policy priority pursued through the national connectivity programme (JENDELA).

## The 5G model, in brief

Malaysia's 5G rollout is distinctive and worth understanding as context for the operators. Rather than letting each telco build its own 5G network, the government first deployed 5G through a **single state-owned wholesaler, Digital Nasional Berhad (DNB)**, from which all operators bought capacity. After debate over the risks of a single-network monopoly, the government shifted to a **dual-network model**, and in **2024 the MCMC selected U Mobile to build a second, competing 5G network**. The build-out timeline is fast-moving. For the full story of DNB, JENDELA and the dual-network policy, see the [5G and JENDELA guide](/en/technology/5g-jendela-dnb-malaysia/).

## The regulator: MCMC and the Communications and Multimedia Act 1998

The sector's referee is the **[Malaysian Communications and Multimedia Commission (MCMC)](/en/government/mcmc-communications-multimedia-commission/)**, which operates under the **Communications and Multimedia Act 1998 (CMA)**. The CMA created a modern "convergence" framework that treats telecommunications, broadcasting and online content as one regulated industry rather than separate silos, and it replaced older laws such as the Telecommunications Act 1950.

The MCMC's powers are wide. It issues **licences** (the Act uses a tiered structure covering network facilities, network services, applications services and content), enforces **competition** and consumer-protection rules, sets **quality-of-service standards**, manages **spectrum**, and — importantly for prices — determines **wholesale access pricing**. Through the **Mandatory Standard on Access Pricing (MSAP)**, the MCMC caps the wholesale rates that network owners such as TM can charge rivals for access to their infrastructure; the 2018 and 2023 MSAP rounds were credited with pushing retail fibre prices down, though the exact rates and their pass-through to consumers should be checked against current MCMC determinations.

In 2026 the MCMC opened a public consultation on proposed amendments to the CMA, covering framework updates, regulatory fees and stronger enforcement powers — a sign the convergence law is being modernised for the streaming-and-platform era. Whether those amendments have been enacted should be confirmed before relying on them.

## Outlook

Malaysia's telecommunications industry has moved into a new phase: consolidated, saturated and shifting from subscriber growth to value. The big questions now are whether the dual-network 5G model delivers genuine competition and investment, whether fibre and mobile coverage can be extended fairly to rural and underserved communities, and how the MCMC modernises a 1998-era law for an age of streaming platforms and cross-border digital services. The brand names on your phone bill may change again — but the direction, toward fewer, larger, converged operators under an activist regulator, is well established.
