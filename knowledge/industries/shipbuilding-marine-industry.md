Malaysia sits on two of the world's busiest sea lanes and earns a large part of its living from the sea — yet its shipbuilding and ship repair (SBSR) industry is one of the quieter corners of the national economy. It is real, long-established and strategically important, but also fragmented, cyclical and dominated by relatively small yards. This guide explains what the industry builds, where, how big it is, and why one naval programme has come to overshadow the whole sector in public debate.

## What the industry is

"SBSR" covers everything from constructing new vessels to repairing, refitting and maintaining existing ones. In Malaysia the work splits into three broad streams: **commercial shipbuilding** (offshore support vessels, tugs, barges, workboats, ferries and increasingly leisure yachts), **ship repair and maintenance, repair and overhaul (MRO)**, and **naval shipbuilding** for the Royal Malaysian Navy and other government agencies.

Regulation and safety sit with the **Marine Department Malaysia (Jabatan Laut Malaysia)**, while investment promotion and incentives are led by the **[Malaysian Investment Development Authority (MIDA)](/en/government/mida/)**. Industry coordination runs partly through the **Association of Marine Industries of Malaysia (AMIM)** and sister bodies such as the Sarawak marine-industry association.

## How big is it?

The sector is built around a large number of small firms rather than a few giants. MIDA's industry page lists **99 registered shipyards** undertaking SBSR activities — **31 in Peninsular Malaysia and 68 in Sabah and Sarawak** — and states that Malaysian yards can build vessels of up to about **30,000 deadweight tonnes (DWT)**. A 2025 parliamentary reply from the trade ministry (MITI) put the count slightly higher, at **110 yards (38 peninsular, 72 in Sabah and Sarawak)**. The two counts differ, so both are flagged for verification; either way the shape is the same — mostly small-to-medium builders, with the larger facilities on the peninsula.

Investment into the sector is modest but growing. MIDA recorded **five approved SBSR projects worth about RM50.4 million in 2024**, all from Malaysian-owned companies. Over a longer window, MITI reported the sector drew **more than RM1 billion in shipbuilding and MRO investment between 2021 and mid-2025**. (A separate figure of about RM574.8 million for the first quarter of 2025 is attributed to aerospace in some reports and to SBSR in others; it is flagged for verification.) Employment figures are harder to pin down: industry sources around 2012 cited roughly 30,000 workers nationwide, but no reliable current total was confirmed, so those numbers should be treated as historical.

## The clusters

The industry's geography is distinctive, and splits cleanly between Borneo and the peninsula.

- **Sarawak** is the heartland by sheer number of yards. The state association has put the Sarawak count at around 60-68 yards, many clustered around **Sibu and Miri**, specialising in offshore support vessels, tugboats, anchor handlers, barges and passenger boats. Sarawak has also designated **Tanjung Manis** as a dedicated shipbuilding and ship-repair cluster.
- **Lumut, Perak** hosts the country's most significant naval facility and heavy-engineering marine base, anchored by the government-owned shipyard there.
- **Johor** hosts larger commercial and repair yards in the south, benefiting from proximity to Singapore's maritime economy.
- **Labuan and Sabah** host offshore-vessel builders and repairers serving the Borneo oil and gas fields.

Broadly, Borneo yards are many and small, while peninsular yards are fewer but tend to take on bigger vessels, offshore work and repair contracts.

## Commercial side: tied to oil and gas

The single most important fact about Malaysia's commercial shipbuilding is its dependence on the energy sector. A large share of what the yards build and repair — **offshore support vessels (OSVs), anchor-handling tug supply vessels, platform supply vessels and workboats** — exists to service offshore oil and gas platforms in Malaysian waters. MIDA explicitly names oil and gas, logistics and offshore support as the industry's key markets.

That linkage is a double-edged sword. When offshore spending is strong, order books fill; when the oil price falls, yards are left with overcapacity in OSVs built partly on speculation. This cyclicality is the defining commercial risk of the sector, and it is why the health of Malaysian shipbuilding tracks closely with the [oil and gas services and equipment industry](/en/industries/oil-gas-services-equipment-industry/). Beyond energy, yards have diversified into leisure yachts and security or patrol craft, with exports reaching markets as far as Australia, Europe, Nigeria and Brazil.

## Naval side: the LCS programme

The naval stream is dominated by one project. The **Littoral Combat Ship (LCS)** programme is a contract for a class of frigate-sized warships for the Royal Malaysian Navy, built at the Lumut yard — originally **Boustead Naval Shipyard**, taken over by the government in 2023 and renamed **Lumut Naval Shipyard (Lunas)**.

The programme is one of the most scrutinised defence projects in the country. Public accounts and audit reporting indicate the contract was reduced in scope (from six vessels to five), carried a reported cost in the region of RM11 billion, and has run **years behind its original delivery schedule**, with the first ship's handover repeatedly pushed back and completion lagging the planned percentage at various checkpoints. Because the specific dates, costs and completion figures are politically contested and change with each new audit or parliamentary hearing, every LCS figure in this article is flagged for verification and should be checked against the latest official Public Accounts Committee, Auditor-General and Ministry of Defence sources. The purpose here is not to adjudicate the dispute but to note that the programme's difficulties have shaped public perception of the whole domestic shipbuilding sector — fairly or not, since it is a single naval contract rather than a measure of the commercial yards.

## Challenges

Several structural issues recur across official and industry reviews:

- **Scale and capability.** Most local yards are small-to-medium and lack the capacity to build or repair large, technically complex vessels, pushing that work abroad.
- **Cyclicality and overcapacity.** The heavy tilt toward oil-and-gas vessels leaves yards exposed to energy downturns.
- **Regional competition.** Lower-cost yards in neighbouring countries such as Vietnam and Indonesia compete hard for the same commercial orders.
- **Project execution.** The LCS experience highlighted gaps in large-project management, design maturity and supply-chain coordination for complex builds.
- **Fragmentation.** A large number of small firms makes it harder to achieve the scale, financing and skills depth that higher-value work requires.

## Policy and incentives

The government's stated ambition is for Malaysia to become **Asia's premier SBSR hub**. MIDA's toolkit for the sector includes **Pioneer Status** (70% income-tax exemption on statutory income for five years) or an **Investment Tax Allowance** of 60% on qualifying capital expenditure, plus automation and reinvestment allowances — with these tax incentives and import-duty exemptions **extended to 31 December 2027**. The sector is also positioned as part of the **New Industrial Master Plan (NIMP) 2030**.

Earlier policy ran through the **Malaysian Shipbuilding/Ship Repair Industry Strategic Plan 2020** (launched in 2011), which targeted around RM6.35 billion in gross national income and roughly 55,000 jobs — targets widely regarded as unmet. A newer industry report (referenced at the LIMA 2025 exhibition) and the NIMP 2030 framework now carry the policy forward; the precise current targets should be confirmed against the official documents.

## Outlook

Malaysia's shipbuilding and ship repair industry enters the late 2020s with a clear identity and an unresolved question. Its identity is that of a diversified-but-small SBSR base — strong in offshore vessels and repair, geographically rooted in Sarawak and Lumut, and firmly tied to the energy cycle. The unresolved question is whether the "Asia's SBSR hub" ambition, backed by incentives and the NIMP 2030, can move the sector beyond small workboats and a troubled flagship naval programme into higher-value, larger and more complex builds against determined regional competition.
