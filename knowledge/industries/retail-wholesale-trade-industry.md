Almost every ringgit a Malaysian spends on physical goods — groceries, a phone, a car, a bottle of cooking oil — passes through the retail and wholesale trade. It is the plumbing of everyday consumption: the network of importers, wholesalers, distributors, shops, markets and online stores that moves products from where they are made to where they are bought. Officials call this network **distributive trade**, and it is one of the largest and most visible parts of the Malaysian economy.

This guide explains what the sector is, how big it is, how its traditional and modern halves fit together, who the major players are, how e-commerce is reshaping it, and the rules that govern foreign participation.

## What is distributive trade?

Distributive trade is the official term for the wholesale and retail sector. The Department of Statistics Malaysia (DOSM) measures it in three parts:

- **Wholesale trade** — selling goods in bulk to businesses: retailers, factories, contractors and other wholesalers, rather than to the public.
- **Retail trade** — selling goods directly to the final consumer, through shops, markets, stalls and online stores.
- **Motor vehicles** — the sale, maintenance and repair of cars, motorcycles and their parts, which DOSM reports as a distinct third category.

Wholesale and retail are two links in the same chain. A wholesaler or distributor buys in volume and breaks it down; a retailer sells the final unit to you. Many large modern operators now do both, buying centrally and selling through their own outlets.

## How big is it?

The sector is enormous. DOSM reported that distributive-trade sales reached a record of about **RM1.77 trillion in 2024**, up roughly 5.5% on the previous year. Within that total, **retail trade** was around RM764.9 billion (up about 6.1%), **wholesale trade** around RM782.1 billion (up about 4.7%), and **motor vehicles** around RM219.1 billion (up about 6.5%).

Measured as a share of the economy, wholesale and retail trade is consistently one of the largest services industries, estimated at roughly 17% of gross domestic product in recent years. It is also a major employer: DOSM's census and survey work has long put the number of people engaged in distributive trade in the millions, spread across hundreds of thousands of establishments, from one-person sundry shops to national chains. ## Modern versus traditional retail

Malaysia's retail landscape is really two overlapping worlds.

**Traditional retail** is the older base: independent sundry and provision shops (*kedai runcit*), wet markets, morning and night markets (*pasar pagi*, *pasar malam*), roadside stalls and small family businesses. These remain woven into daily life, especially outside the big cities, and they employ large numbers of people. Industry groups have warned that smaller independent and traditional operators face real pressure from rising costs and new compliance burdens such as e-invoicing.

**Modern retail** means organised, usually chain-operated formats: hypermarkets and supermarkets, convenience stores, department stores, specialty and fashion chains, pharmacies, and the shopping malls that house them. This segment has expanded steadily as incomes and urbanisation have risen, and it is where most new investment and professional management are concentrated.

Growth across these formats is uneven. Industry trackers such as Retail Group Malaysia recorded overall retail growth of only a few per cent in 2024 — a strong first quarter followed by a weak second quarter and a modest recovery — and have forecast growth of around 4% for 2025, closely tied to consumer confidence and the cost of living. Supermarkets and convenience stores were expected to grow slowly, while department stores anticipated a sharper rebound.

## Key players

The modern sector is anchored by a mix of domestic and foreign operators:

- **Hypermarkets and supermarkets:** AEON (Japanese-owned but long established locally), the home-grown Mydin, Lotus's (formerly Tesco), and Econsave, among others.
- **Convenience stores:** 99 Speed Mart, 7-Eleven, KK Super Mart and MyNews are the best-known chains. 99 Speed Mart in particular has grown rapidly, reporting roughly 2,778 outlets by the end of 2024, and its 2024 listing was among the largest Malaysian IPOs in years.
- **Department stores and malls:** Parkson and AEON operate department stores, while large mall developers and operators — from Suria KLCC to Pavilion, Mid Valley and 1 Utama — provide the physical space that much of modern retail depends on.
- **Wholesale and distribution:** A less visible but vast layer of importers, distributors and cash-and-carry operators supplies these retailers and the wider business economy.

## E-commerce and the online crossover

Online shopping has become a central channel rather than a niche. Platforms such as Shopee and Lazada dominate consumer marketplaces, and DOSM's statistics show total national e-commerce income running into the trillions of ringgit — though that headline figure spans manufacturing and services, not retail alone, and should not be read as pure retail turnover.

The more important shift is the blurring of online and offline. Traditional and modern retailers alike now add delivery, click-and-collect, loyalty apps and online storefronts, while online-first sellers open physical pick-up points and showrooms. For anyone selling goods this way, the practical starting points are covered in the guide to [starting an e-commerce business in Malaysia](/en/business/starting-an-ecommerce-business-malaysia/).

## Foreign participation and the WRT licence

Distributive trade is one of the sectors where Malaysia regulates foreign ownership. Foreign-owned companies that want to sell wholesale or retail generally fall under the Ministry of Domestic Trade and Cost of Living (**KPDN**) guidelines on foreign participation in distributive trade, and typically need a **wholesale/retail trade (WRT) licence**.

The rules set conditions that change from format to format. Larger formats such as hypermarkets carry higher minimum-capital requirements, and historically there have been conditions on Bumiputera equity participation and the hiring of staff that reflect the country's demographic make-up. Several smaller formats — such as small supermarkets, mini-markets, provision shops and 24-hour convenience stores — have generally been reserved for Malaysian ownership. Because these thresholds and lists are periodically amended, anyone planning to invest should confirm the current edition of the guideline with KPDN. The practical mechanics of the licence are covered in the [WRT licence guide](/en/business/wrt-licence-malaysia/).

## Outlook

The outlook is cautiously positive but sensitive to household budgets. Record 2024 sales, steady domestic consumption, recovering tourism and continued e-commerce growth all support the sector, and DOSM has expected momentum to carry into the following year. The main constraints are the cost of living, subsidy and tax changes such as an expanded sales and service tax, compliance costs like e-invoicing that weigh most on small operators, and global economic uncertainty.

The likely direction of travel is more consolidation and modernisation — larger chains, deeper online integration and more sophisticated logistics — alongside a traditional retail base that remains culturally and economically important, especially outside the major cities.
