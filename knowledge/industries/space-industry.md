Malaysia does not launch rockets of its own, yet it has flown an astronaut, built and operated microsatellites, and passed a law to license private space activity before most of its neighbours. Its space sector is small and quietly strategic — threaded through broadband policy, defence, disaster monitoring and national prestige. This guide maps the agency, the law, the satellites and the ambitions that make up Malaysia's space economy.

This is distinct from the [aerospace industry](/en/industries/aerospace-industry/), which covers aircraft, maintenance and aerostructures. Here the subject is strictly space: satellites, launch, space regulation and the downstream services that depend on orbit.

## Governance: MYSA and the Space Board Act 2022

The sector's coordinating body is the **Malaysian Space Agency** (MYSA, or Agensi Angkasa Malaysia). It was created in 2019 by merging two older bodies — the **National Space Agency (ANGKASA)**, established in 2002, and the **Malaysian Remote Sensing Agency**, whose lineage runs back to a remote-sensing centre founded in 1988. MYSA sits under the Ministry of Science, Technology and Innovation (MOSTI) and consolidates space policy, remote-sensing data and research under one roof.

For most of its history Malaysia's space activity ran without a dedicated legal framework. That changed with the **Malaysian Space Board Act 2022 (Act 834)**, gazetted in January 2022. The Act establishes the Malaysian Space Board and a licensing regime covering the core risks of modern spaceflight: building or manufacturing space objects, owning or operating facilities to integrate and test them, operating a launch facility on Malaysian soil, and registering objects placed in orbit. A **Space Regulatory Division** was set up inside MOSTI in mid-2022 to serve as the Board's executive secretariat and to draft the detailed rules. With the Malaysian Space Board Regulations 2024 in place, the Act was brought into full enforcement from **1 January 2025** — giving Malaysia a working authorisation system for commercial space operators.

### The National Space Policy 2030

Policy direction comes from the **National Space Policy 2030** (Dasar Angkasa Negara 2030, or DAN2030), approved in 2017. It frames space as a strategic contributor to national competitiveness and sovereignty, with goals spanning governance, local technology self-reliance and a stronger regional research standing. Economic targets have been stated at different scales: earlier figures pointed to roughly 0.3% of GDP (about RM3.2 billion) and several thousand jobs, while a later action plan has cited a more ambitious 1% of GDP, around RM10 billion. The gap between those numbers is itself a fair summary of the sector — large aspirations, a modest base.

## Satellites: from TiungSAT to MEASAT

Malaysia's hands-on space heritage began with small satellites built through technology transfer. **TiungSAT-1**, the country's first microsatellite, launched in 2000 and was developed with the United Kingdom's Surrey Satellite Technology, giving a generation of Malaysian engineers direct build experience. It was followed by **RazakSAT**, an earth-observation satellite placed in a near-equatorial orbit in 2009 aboard a SpaceX Falcon 1 — a notable mission, though the satellite failed after roughly a year, a sobering lesson in the difficulty of operating space assets.

The commercial backbone of the sector is **MEASAT**, Malaysia's main satellite operator. Through Measat Satellite Systems, it runs a fleet of communications satellites from the 91.5 degrees East orbital slot. Its most capable craft, **MEASAT-3d**, launched in June 2022 — the eighth MEASAT satellite — on an Ariane 5 from French Guiana. A high-throughput satellite estimated at around US$273 million, it carries multiple frequency bands and was aimed squarely at extending high-speed broadband into rural and East Malaysia under the national JENDELA connectivity plan, alongside direct-to-home television. MEASAT ties the space sector directly to the [telecommunications industry](/en/industries/telecommunications-industry/): much of Malaysia's satellite activity exists to deliver connectivity on the ground.

## The angkasawan programme

Malaysia's most visible space moment remains the **angkasawan programme**. In October 2007, **Sheikh Muszaphar Shukor** became the first Malaysian in space, travelling to the International Space Station aboard a Russian Soyuz spacecraft. The flight was funded through a defence offset arrangement tied to Malaysia's purchase of Sukhoi Su-30MKM fighter jets, under which Russia trained two Malaysian candidates and flew one. It was a landmark for national confidence and for science outreach, even as critics debated its scientific return.

A second flight has been discussed for years without a confirmed mission. Figures including Sheikh Muszaphar himself have spoken of sending a second astronaut — potentially a woman — around the end of the decade, and periodic diplomatic contacts have raised the possibility of renewed cooperation with Russia. As of late 2026, no funded second mission has been formally committed.

## Emerging private players and downstream applications

Beyond the established operators, a **new-space** layer is forming. The best-known startup, **Angkasa-X**, is pursuing low-earth-orbit (LEO) constellations to deliver internet connectivity across ASEAN on a satellite-as-a-service model; it launched a demonstrator satellite, A-SEANSAT-PG1, in 2023 and has spoken of a constellation numbering in the hundreds to blanket the region. The ambition is large and the path capital-intensive, but it signals a shift from government-led projects towards commercial ventures — exactly the activity the Space Board Act was written to license.

The sector's real economic weight, however, sits **downstream**. Satellite communications, global navigation satellite system (GNSS) positioning, and remote sensing and geospatial analysis feed agriculture, forestry monitoring, disaster response, urban planning and maritime surveillance. These applications draw on the same engineering and data skills as Malaysia's wider electronics and semiconductor base, and they are where most Malaysian firms and agencies actually add value today.

## Challenges and outlook

The constraints are structural. Malaysia has no indigenous launch capability and depends on foreign rockets and, often, foreign-built satellite platforms. The domestic market is small, specialist talent is scarce and easily drawn abroad, and funding for high-risk space ventures is limited. Ground infrastructure — tracking stations, data-reception facilities and testing capacity — needs sustained investment to match the new licensing framework.

The opportunities are equally real. A working legal regime, an established operator in MEASAT, growing demand for satellite broadband and earth-observation data, and a regional LEO race all give Malaysia a credible niche — less in launching rockets than in services, data and selective manufacturing. Whether the National Space Policy 2030 targets are met will depend on turning a well-drafted framework into funded, operating businesses.
