Malaysia has spent half a century building cars. Now it is trying to electrify them — and, just as importantly, to make the batteries and build the charging network that electric vehicles depend on. This is a different story from the one told in the guide to [Malaysia's automotive industry](/en/industries/automotive-industry/), which is largely about petrol and diesel cars. Here the subject is the EV and battery *ecosystem*: the vehicles, the incentives, the chargers and the factories that together decide how fast Malaysia switches to electric mobility.

## What the "EV ecosystem" means

An EV ecosystem is more than the cars on the road. It has four interlocking parts: the **vehicles** themselves (and whether they are imported whole or assembled locally), the **incentives and policy** that make them attractive to buy and build, the **charging infrastructure** that keeps them running, and the **manufacturing base** — assembly plants, battery cells and components — that captures economic value at home. Malaysia is moving on all four fronts at once, but at very different speeds.

## Adoption so far

EVs are still a small slice of the Malaysian market, but a fast-growing one. According to data from the Malaysian Automotive Association (MAA), full battery-electric vehicles (BEVs) accounted for only about **3.4% of new car sales among MAA members in the first half of 2025** — roughly 4.3% once non-member brands such as Tesla are added. Yet pure-EV sales in that half-year roughly **doubled year-on-year**, continuing a trend from 2024 when BEV registrations jumped sharply on the back of new, cheaper models from Chinese manufacturers.

Momentum built further towards the end of 2025, with a surge in buying ahead of the expiry of import-duty exemptions on fully imported EVs. (Exact unit totals and the final full-year share should be confirmed against MAA's year-end release — see the verification notes, as the headline "xEV" category sometimes blends hybrids with pure EVs.)

For context, the government's longer-term ambition is for electrified vehicles to reach a double-digit share of total industry volume by 2030. The precise target varies between official sources — some quote 15% by 2030 and 38% by 2040, others 20% by 2030 rising to 80% by 2050 — which is one reason the figure is flagged for verification against the National Automotive Policy 2020 mid-term review.

## Incentives and policy

Malaysia's EV push rides on two policy documents. The **National Automotive Policy 2020 (NAP 2020)**, launched in February 2020 as a ten-year roadmap, placed Next-Generation Vehicles (NxGV) — including EVs — at the centre of the country's industrial ambitions, offering incentives such as Pioneer Status and Investment Tax Allowance to firms in the EV supply chain. The **Low Carbon Mobility Blueprint 2021–2030**, led by the environment ministry and the Malaysian Green Technology and Climate Change Corporation (MGTC), sets the adoption and infrastructure targets.

The sharpest tool, though, has been tax exemption:

- **Fully imported (CBU) EVs** were exempt from import duty, excise duty and sales tax, a window that ran until **31 December 2025**.
- **Locally assembled (CKD) EVs** keep their incentives longer — to **31 December 2027** — a deliberate design meant to push manufacturers towards assembling in Malaysia rather than simply shipping cars in.
- **Road tax** on EVs was waived over roughly the same period, with a new, power-output-based structure due to follow.

These deadlines shift with each federal Budget, so any specific date should be checked against the latest Ministry of Finance announcements. Separately, under MITI's **BEV Global Leaders** initiative, approved brands can import and sell EVs outside the usual Approved Permit (AP) regime; **Tesla was the scheme's first applicant** in March 2023.

## Charging infrastructure

A car is only as useful as the places it can charge. Under the Low Carbon Mobility Blueprint, Malaysia aimed for **10,000 public charging points by 2025** — a target it missed. By the end of 2025 the network stood at roughly 5,600 points, rising to about **5,839 by the end of March 2026**, of which the majority (around 3,868) were slower **AC chargers** and about 1,971 were **DC fast chargers**. That works out to roughly one public charger for every sixteen battery-electric vehicles on the road.

The network is run by competing charge-point operators (CPOs) rather than a single utility — names such as ChargeSini, Gentari, JomCharge (EV Connection) and MGTC's own **chargEV** brand, alongside Tesla's proprietary Superchargers. Coverage is strongest in the Klang Valley and along the North–South Expressway, and thinnest in East Malaysia and rural areas. Operator-by-operator counts vary between sources and are flagged for verification.

## Manufacturing and battery investments

The most consequential shift is in where EVs and their parts are made. Investment has landed across the chain:

- **Proton e.MAS:** In September 2025, Proton inaugurated a dedicated EV assembly plant in **Tanjung Malim, Perak**, beginning CKD assembly of the **e.MAS 7** — a model derived from the Galaxy E5 of Proton's partner, China's Geely. Reported at around RM82 million in initial investment with capacity scaling from 20,000 towards 45,000 units a year, it is Malaysia's first dedicated local EV assembly line, and the e.MAS 7 was among the best-selling EVs of 2025.
- **Tesla:** After MITI approved its entry in 2023, Tesla established a local presence, a Supercharger network and had invested on the order of RM59 million by mid-2024 — a modest sum, but a signal of confidence.
- **Samsung SDI:** The clearest piece of genuine *battery* manufacturing is **Samsung SDI Energy Malaysia's** cylindrical EV battery-cell plant in **Seremban, Negeri Sembilan**, representing a cumulative investment of around **RM7 billion** and described by MIDA as the first EV battery-cell facility in the country.

Taken together, MIDA reports that Malaysia approved roughly **RM30 billion across 68 projects** in the EV and related ecosystem from 2018 to 2023, spanning vehicle assembly, components and charging equipment. For the wider energy context behind this shift, see the guides to [renewable energy in Malaysia](/en/energy/renewable-energy-in-malaysia/) and the [National Energy Transition Roadmap (NETR)](/en/energy/national-energy-transition-roadmap-netr/), and for the agency driving the investment, [MIDA](/en/government/mida/).

## Outlook and challenges

Malaysia's EV ecosystem is real but still early. The direction is clear — assembly moving onshore, battery-cell capacity taking root, charging multiplying year on year — yet the challenges are just as plain. Chargers remain below target and unevenly distributed. True battery value, from cell chemistry to raw materials, is still largely imported. And the whole edifice has leaned heavily on tax exemptions whose expiry, especially for imported cars, introduces real uncertainty about demand once prices normalise.

The central question for the next few years is whether locally assembled EVs and a maturing charging network can keep sales climbing after the richest import incentives lapse — turning a policy-driven spurt into a self-sustaining industry.
