Pepper is one of Malaysia's oldest and most geographically distinctive agricultural commodities: a vine imported from the Indian subcontinent and Sumatra that found its ideal home in the humid, well-drained hills of Sarawak, where it became the backbone of thousands of rural smallholdings. Though Malaysia is a modest player by global volume, it punches above its weight on reputation, selling a premium, origin-certified "Sarawak Pepper" into discerning markets. This article profiles the structure, geography, trade and prospects of the sector as a standalone industry.

## A smallholder crop anchored in Sarawak

Malaysia ranks fifth among the producer members of the International Pepper Community (IPC), behind Vietnam, Indonesia, India and Brazil. National output was estimated at around 30,000 tonnes in 2024, with the Ministry of Plantation and Commodities forecasting roughly 32,000 tonnes for 2025 — broadly in line with the 32,724 tonnes recorded in 2022.

What makes the industry unusual is its extreme geographic concentration. Sarawak accounts for more than 95% of national production — the state government puts its share above 98% — making pepper effectively a Sarawakian crop. It is also overwhelmingly a smallholder business. As of late 2025 there were about 39,000 registered pepper farmers cultivating some 8,300 hectares, with holdings typically measured in fractions of a hectare rather than plantation scale. Betong has overtaken Kuching as the leading supplying district, with Sri Aman and Serian also prominent.

This smallholder character links pepper closely to the broader [Agriculture sector overview](/en/agriculture/agriculture-sector-overview/), where it sits alongside other commodity crops as a source of rural livelihoods and a target for government support schemes.

## Black and white pepper: one vine, two products

Both black and white pepper come from the same plant, *Piper nigrum*. The difference lies in processing. **Black pepper** is made by harvesting the berries while still green and unripe, then drying them whole; the skin darkens and wrinkles to form the familiar peppercorn. **White pepper** is produced from fully ripened berries that are soaked (retted) to loosen and remove the outer skin before the inner seed is dried. The result is a milder, cleaner-tasting spice that typically commands a higher price.

Malaysia, and Sarawak in particular, is especially known for high-grade white pepper, a point of differentiation from competitors who lean more heavily toward black pepper. In 2024, exports comprised 4,517 tonnes of black pepper, 1,223 tonnes of white pepper and 13 tonnes of green pepper.

## Regulation and the Sarawak Pepper GI

The industry is overseen by the **Malaysian Pepper Board (Lembaga Lada Malaysia, MPB)** — formerly the Malaysian Pepper Marketing Board — a statutory body under the Ministry of Plantation and Commodities (MPIC). The MPB runs research and extension services, sets and enforces grading and quality standards, publishes daily reference prices, and promotes Malaysian pepper abroad.

A central pillar of its strategy is branding. Sarawak Pepper has held **geographical indication (GI) status since 2003** under the Geographical Indications Act 2000, certifying the product's origin and quality characteristics. The GI protects the Sarawak name in trade and underpins the premium pricing that Malaysian pepper enjoys. In early 2025, Malaysian black pepper was quoted by the IPC at around US$9,000 per tonne and white pepper at about US$11,600 per tonne — well above Vietnam (roughly US$6,650 black and US$9,550 white) and Indonesia (US$7,258 and US$10,019).

## Exports, prices and value

Pepper is an export-oriented crop. In 2024, Malaysia exported 5,753 tonnes of pepper, with earnings rising 25% to RM186.7 million from RM149.9 million in 2023. Japan is the single largest destination, taking roughly half of export volume, with other Asian and European markets also important. Measured across the whole value chain, including processed products, industry revenue was reported at about RM2.27 billion in 2025, up from RM2.24 billion in 2024.

Prices are notoriously cyclical. Global prices sat at US$6,000–7,000 per tonne for black pepper before a sharp spike in mid-2024 — driven by panic buying and stockpiling amid tightening supply — that pushed black pepper close to US$10,000 and white pepper to US$13,000 before correcting. The IPC estimated 2024 global production at just 465,000 tonnes against demand of about 529,000 tonnes, a structural deficit that has kept prices firm. Because so many Malaysian households depend on the crop, these swings translate directly into rural incomes, making price stability a recurring policy concern.

## Downstream value-adding

Historically the industry has exported raw, dried peppercorns, capturing relatively little of the final retail value. Efforts to move downstream focus on processing and product development — ground and packaged consumer pepper, pepper-based seasonings and sauces, essential oils and oleoresins, and even pepper-infused health, cosmetic and beverage products. These higher-margin goods feed into Malaysia's wider [food & beverage manufacturing](/en/industries/food-beverage-manufacturing-industry/) base and offer a route to insulate growers from raw-commodity price shocks.

## Challenges and outlook

Several structural pressures weigh on the sector. The farmer population is ageing, and younger generations are drawn away from labour-intensive pepper cultivation, raising questions about continuity. Competition is intense: Vietnam alone exported 250,600 tonnes in 2024, worth over US$1.3 billion, dwarfing Malaysian volumes, while Indonesia and India remain formidable. Low mechanisation, vulnerability to disease and climate stress, and dependence on volatile world prices all compress margins.

The policy response emphasises modernisation — mechanisation of harvesting and drying, replanting with better varieties, digital traceability, and a stronger push into certified, branded and processed products that exploit the Sarawak Pepper GI. If Malaysia can lean into quality and provenance rather than compete on bulk volume, the premium-niche strategy offers a credible path to resilience even in a market it cannot dominate by scale.
