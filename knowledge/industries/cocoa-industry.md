Malaysia is one of the world's great cocoa processors that has almost stopped growing cocoa. In a single generation the country went from a top-four bean producer, its hillsides in Sabah and Peninsular Malaysia heavy with pods, to a place that imports beans by the shipload and barely harvests a few hundred tonnes of its own. What stayed, and thrived, was the factory end: the grinding mills, refineries and chocolate lines that turn imported beans into cocoa butter, powder, liquor and finished confectionery for export. The result is a "cocoa paradox" - a world-class downstream industry sitting on an upstream that has all but disappeared.

## What the industry is

The cocoa sector today is defined by processing rather than farming. Grinders buy raw cocoa beans on the world market, roast and press them, and sell the semi-finished products - cocoa butter, cocoa powder and cocoa liquor (also called cocoa mass or paste) - to global confectionery, bakery and cosmetics manufacturers. A smaller but growing slice of the industry makes finished chocolate and compound coatings. This downstream focus makes Malaysia part of the global [food & beverage manufacturing](/en/industries/food-beverage-manufacturing-industry/) value chain far more than it is part of its own [Agriculture sector overview](/en/agriculture/agriculture-sector-overview/).

## How big it is

The scale of the gap between growing and grinding is striking. Bean cultivation peaked at about 247,000 tonnes in 1990, when planted area reached a record of roughly 414,000 hectares and Sabah alone supplied well over half the crop. Today national bean output has fallen to only a few hundred tonnes a year - the Malaysian Cocoa Board has cited figures under 500 tonnes - grown on a few thousand hectares, around 89% of it by smallholders.

Grinding moved the opposite way. Volumes rose from around 70,000 tonnes in 1990 to above 300,000 tonnes in the late 2000s and roughly 370,000 tonnes by 2023, with press reports placing Malaysia among the world's top four grinders and citing about 383,000 tonnes processed in 2024. Exports of cocoa and cocoa preparations were worth on the order of US$1.79 billion in 2023, and official ringgit-denominated series have put the value of cocoa and cocoa-based exports in the billions of ringgit.

## The grinding hub

The heart of the industry is a cluster of large processors, concentrated around Johor and Port Klang with deep-water port access for importing beans and exporting products. The standout is **Guan Chong Berhad**, headquartered in Pasir Gudang, Johor - one of the world's largest cocoa grinders and the biggest in Asia. Its combined grinding capacity across facilities in Malaysia (about 150,000 tonnes), Indonesia, Ivory Coast and operations in Europe and the United States exceeds 450,000 tonnes a year.

Guan Chong is not alone. **JB Cocoa**, the cocoa arm of JB Foods, operates grinding plants in Johor, and the Swiss group **Barry Callebaut**, the world's largest chocolate and cocoa manufacturer, runs processing operations in Malaysia. Together these companies make the country a significant node in the global cocoa trade, buying beans from West Africa, Indonesia, Ecuador and elsewhere, and shipping intermediate products to buyers worldwide. The business is cyclical and exposed to volatile bean prices: the record cocoa prices and supply squeeze of 2024-2025 pressured grinding margins across the sector.

## Upstream cultivation

What remains of bean farming is largely a Sabah story. The interior districts and the Tawau region, which drove the 1980s boom, still account for the majority of remaining planted area and production. The collapse from the 1990 peak was driven by low and volatile bean prices, labour shortages, pest and disease pressure, and the far higher returns available from oil palm, which replaced cocoa across much of the old estate land.

Smallholder yields vary enormously. The national productivity average has been cited at around 100 kg per hectare per year, yet some rehabilitated smallholdings have achieved over 1,000 kg, suggesting substantial room for improvement with better planting material and agronomy.

## Institutions and policy

The sector is regulated and promoted by the **Malaysian Cocoa Board (Lembaga Koko Malaysia, LKM)**, a statutory body headquartered in Kota Kinabalu, Sabah, operating under the **Ministry of Plantation and Commodities (MPIC)**. The Board licenses the trade, maintains quality and grading standards, conducts research and extension, publishes industry statistics, and runs replanting and farm-rehabilitation programmes aimed at reviving domestic supply. Recent initiatives include multi-year funds to rehabilitate smallholder farms and a target to expand planted area to around 10,000 hectares nationwide within five years.

## Outlook

Malaysia's ambition is to remain a leading cocoa and chocolate hub for Asia. The strategy rests on two legs: defending and upgrading the grinding and chocolate-manufacturing base, while modestly rebuilding premium, often fine-flavour, bean production for a growing bean-to-bar and specialty chocolate scene. The downstream industry's future depends on secure bean supply and stable margins in a tight global market, while the upstream revival will hinge on whether cocoa can compete with oil palm for land and labour. For now, the paradox holds: Malaysia will keep exporting far more cocoa than it grows.
