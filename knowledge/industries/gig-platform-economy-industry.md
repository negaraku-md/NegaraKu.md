Every day, millions of transactions in Malaysia are arranged not by a shop, an office or a taxi rank, but by an app: a car summoned to a kerb, a bag of food carried across town on a motorcycle, a logo designed for a client the freelancer will never meet. This is the gig and platform economy — work mediated by digital platforms — and in little more than a decade it has moved from novelty to one of the most visible features of Malaysian working life.

This guide explains what that sector actually is, how big it is, which companies dominate it, and how Malaysia is now trying to regulate it. It focuses on the **economic sector**. For the law itself — the rights, contracts and tribunal introduced in 2025 — see the companion guide to the [Gig Workers Act 2025](/en/employment/gig-workers-act-2025/).

## What is the gig and platform economy?

"Gig work" describes short, task-based or on-demand jobs rather than a traditional salaried post. The **platform economy** is the part of that world organised through digital platforms, which match workers to customers and take a cut of each transaction. In Malaysia it falls into three broad groups:

- **E-hailing (ride-hailing):** booking a car or motorcycle ride through an app, the segment Grab built its business on.
- **P-hailing (parcel-hailing):** on-demand delivery of food and goods, typically by riders on motorcycles — the GrabFood and Foodpanda world.
- **Online freelancing and services:** design, writing, coding, tutoring, home services and e-commerce selling arranged through platforms and marketplaces.

The common thread is that the worker is usually treated as self-employed — an "own-account worker" in statistical language — rather than an employee. That classification is the single most important fact about the sector, because it has historically left platform workers outside the protections that salaried staff take for granted.

## How big is it?

Size is where honesty is required, because the numbers are genuinely contested. Government statements made during the 2025 legislative debate referred to **more than 1.2 million** gig workers; a 2026 analysis by ISIS Malaysia put the figure closer to **1.6 million**. A Department of Statistics Malaysia (DOSM) survey from 2022 counted around **650,000** gig workers, of whom roughly 370,000 were active p-hailing riders. Broader measures that use "own-account workers" as a proxy reach into the **millions** and put the share of the workforce anywhere between about 18% and 26%.

These figures are not interchangeable. They differ because they draw the boundary of "gig work" differently — narrowly (platform riders and drivers only) or broadly (all self-employed own-account workers). The honest summary is that platform-based gig work probably involves well over a million Malaysians, that it is growing, and that no single official headcount should be treated as definitive (see the verification notes on this page).

The sector sits inside a far larger digital economy. DOSM reported that information-and-communications and e-commerce activities together contributed about **23.4% of GDP (around RM451.3 billion) in 2024**. The gig economy is the labour-market face of that shift — though it is only one part of it, and the 23.4% figure is a broad digital-economy measure, not a gig-specific one. For the wider picture, see the [digital economy overview](/en/industries/digital-economy-overview/).

## The main platforms

Malaysia's platform economy is concentrated among a small number of operators.

- **Grab** is the dominant force. The Singapore-headquartered "super-app" leads both ride-hailing and food delivery and bundles payments, deliveries and financial services into one platform. A consultancy report commissioned by Grab (EconWorks) estimated that its on-demand services contributed about **RM9.9 billion, or roughly 0.5% of GDP, in 2023** — a figure worth treating as an indicative, company-commissioned estimate rather than official data.
- **Foodpanda** is the principal food-delivery challenger, competing hard on restaurant selection and promotions, with analyst estimates placing its Malaysian food-delivery share around a fifth of the market.
- **ShopeeFood** and other entrants have taken a growing slice of p-hailing, while e-hailing has seen new operators enter and, at times, fall foul of licensing rules enforced by the Land Public Transport Agency (APAD).

Reported market shares vary between analysts and should be read as approximate: different firms measure gross merchandise value, order volume or revenue and reach different conclusions.

## Worker protections: a turning point

For most of the sector's history, the defining problem was that platform workers were neither employees nor conventionally self-employed in the eyes of the law — so they fell through the cracks of accident cover, retirement savings and dispute resolution. Three developments have begun to change that.

**The Gig Workers Act 2025 (Act 872)** is Malaysia's first dedicated law for platform workers. Passed by the Dewan Rakyat in August 2025 and the Dewan Negara in September 2025, and reported to have come into force in 2026, it formally recognises gig workers as a distinct category, requires platforms to provide **written service contracts** with minimum terms, mandates **social-security contributions**, and creates a **Gig Workers Tribunal** to handle disputes such as unjustified account deactivation. The detail belongs in the dedicated [Gig Workers Act 2025](/en/employment/gig-workers-act-2025/) guide.

**SOCSO coverage** is the practical backbone. Gig workers can contribute under the Self-Employment Social Security Act 2017, which provides protection against workplace accidents, disability and related risks; reporting around the new Act describes platform-linked contributions (widely cited as a 1.25% deduction per ride or delivery) becoming mandatory. **EPF retirement coverage**, by contrast, remains unresolved — several analyses note it was not settled in the first phase and is still being studied.

**A Gig Economy Commission** — the Malaysian Gig Economy Commission, or SEGiM (Suruhanjaya Ekonomi Gig Malaysia) — was approved by the Cabinet to oversee the sector, coordinate policy and monitor platform compliance, reportedly sitting under the Prime Minister's Department with the Malaysia Digital Economy Corporation as technical secretariat. Its exact gazetted status and mandate should be confirmed against official sources.

## The unresolved issues

The reforms do not close the debate; they open the next phase of it.

- **Income security.** Platform earnings fluctuate with demand, fuel costs and the algorithms that set fares and assign jobs. Riders and drivers routinely report that take-home pay after costs is lower than headline growth figures suggest.
- **Classification.** Recognising gig workers as a distinct, third category avoids forcing them into either "employee" or "contractor" — but it also leaves open how far employment-style protections (minimum earnings, paid leave) should extend.
- **Platform power.** A handful of operators set the terms, commissions and deactivation rules for hundreds of thousands of workers, raising questions about bargaining power and transparency that a tribunal alone may not resolve.
- **Measurement.** Policy is hard to calibrate when the basic headcount is disputed; better official statistics are a precondition for everything else.

## Outlook

Malaysia has moved faster than most of its neighbours in giving platform work a legal framework, and the combination of the Gig Workers Act, SOCSO coverage and a dedicated commission marks a genuine shift from laissez-faire to stewardship. The direction of travel is clear: platform work is being normalised as a recognised part of the labour market rather than an unregulated grey zone.

The open questions are about depth rather than direction — whether contributions translate into adequate protection, whether retirement savings follow accident cover, and whether workers gain real bargaining power against the platforms that mediate their work.
