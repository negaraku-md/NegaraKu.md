Long before Malaysia became known for computer chips and palm oil, it made cloth and clothes. The textiles, apparel and footwear industry is one of the country's oldest modern manufacturing sectors — the kind of labour-intensive, export-oriented business that helped kick-start industrialisation in the 1970s and 1980s. Today it is a smaller slice of a much larger economy, but it remains a real employer, a steady exporter, and a sector the government is actively trying to reinvent.

This guide explains what the industry covers, how big it is, who the main players and markets are, what support is available, and where it is heading.

## What the industry covers

Malaysia's official statistics and investment agency, the Malaysian Investment Development Authority (MIDA), group textiles, apparel and footwear together under what it calls the **"lifestyle" subsector**, alongside leather goods and jewellery. In practice the textile-and-apparel chain runs through several stages:

- **Upstream textiles** — polymerisation, spinning, weaving, knitting and wet processing (dyeing and finishing) to turn fibre into yarn and fabric.
- **Apparel** — cutting and sewing fabric into ready-made garments and clothing accessories, the most visible and most labour-intensive part of the chain.
- **Technical and industrial textiles** — higher-value fabrics engineered for performance rather than fashion, such as geotextiles, filtration media, automotive textiles and protective or medical fabrics (including personal protective equipment).
- **Footwear** — boots, shoes, sandals, slippers and clogs, closely linked to the leather segment.

Alongside the volume end sits a distinctive heritage strand — **batik and songket** — which carries cultural weight and a modest-fashion export angle, exemplified by home-grown brands in the Muslim-wear space.

## How big is it?

The industry is long-established but, by Malaysian standards, no longer large relative to the rest of manufacturing. According to figures attributed to the **Malaysia External Trade Development Corporation (MATRADE)**, apparel and clothing-accessories trade reached about **RM18.24 billion in 2025**, up 8.3% year on year, with **exports of roughly RM6.88 billion**. Shipments continued to climb in early 2026.

The more telling number is the trend in *share*. The textiles-and-footwear slice of Malaysia's total exports fell from around **2.9% in 2000 to about 1.1% in 2023**, according to a MIDF Research analysis — not because the industry collapsed, but because electronics and other sectors grew far faster while lower-cost garment producers abroad captured volume. (These figures are drawn from news and research summaries rather than a single official release; see the verification notes.)

Employment figures vary with definition. One reading, based on official statistics, puts the combined **textiles, apparel and leather** workforce at roughly **262,000 in 2023**; a narrower garment-and-textile count gives about **70,000 workers across 970-plus factories**, more than 400 of them making ready-made garments. The workforce is heavily reliant on migrant labour, which has at times drawn scrutiny over working conditions — a reputational issue the sector cannot ignore in export markets with strict labour-standards rules.

## Who the main players are

The industry is **fragmented**, mixing large, often foreign-linked manufacturers with many small and medium local firms. Names that recur in industry and market reporting include **Penfabric**, the long-running Penang-based fabric maker; **Esquel Group**, a major garment manufacturer; **D & Y Textile (M)**; **Asia Brand Berhad**; and **San Miguel Yamamura Woven Products**. Treat any such list as indicative rather than a ranking — it comes from commercial reporting, not an official registry.

The sector is organised through bodies such as the **Malaysian Textile Manufacturers Association (MTMA)** and the broader **Federation of Malaysian Fashion, Textiles and Apparels (FMFTA)**, launched in 2019 to coordinate the sector's modernisation. MTMA leaders have publicly described parts of the industry as having become "stagnant" because it remains labour-intensive, and have urged local firms to adopt automation, digitalisation and even partnerships with Chinese manufacturers to upgrade.

## Export markets

Because Malaysia's home market is small, the industry has always leaned on exports — and on a fairly concentrated set of destinations. The **United States** is the single largest market, followed by **Japan** and **Turkey**, with **China** and **Indonesia** also significant. That concentration is a double-edged sword: it gives established buyer relationships, but it also exposes manufacturers to tariff shifts and trade-policy swings in a handful of countries, which is why market diversification is a recurring theme at trade shows such as ATEX Malaysia.

## The competitive squeeze

The central challenge is straightforward. Volume garment-making competes largely on cost, and Malaysia's wages are higher than those of **Vietnam, Bangladesh and Cambodia**, which have taken a growing share of global apparel orders. Malaysia cannot win a race to the bottom on labour cost, and policymakers have concluded it should not try.

The strategic answer is to move *up* the value chain: toward **technical and industrial textiles** that compete on engineering and quality rather than price; toward **automation and digitalisation** that cut the labour content of each garment; and toward **sustainability and traceability**, which are becoming entry requirements for Western buyers. This is the same "move up the stack" logic that runs through much of Malaysian industrial policy.

## Incentives and policy support

The industry sits within Malaysia's broader manufacturing-investment framework, administered by MIDA — see the [manufacturing industry overview](/en/industries/manufacturing-industry-overview/) and the profile of [MIDA](/en/government/mida/) for how approvals and incentives work in general.

Two policy threads matter specifically here:

- **NIMP 2030.** The New Industrial Master Plan 2030, launched on 1 September 2023, names textiles, apparel and footwear among its priority subsectors and frames the goal as higher economic complexity, technology adoption and net-zero progress rather than low-cost production.
- **A new incentive framework.** From the first quarter of 2026, qualifying investment in the subsector falls under a **New Investment Incentive Framework** in which tax incentives are tiered and tied to outcomes — for example technology level, skills and local linkages — rather than granted as flat allowances. MIDA also administers manufacturing-licence approvals and continues to promote heritage segments such as batik.

MIDA reported approved investment of about **RM657 million across 28 projects** in the lifestyle subsector in 2025, generating some 1,222 jobs — a reminder that, while the sector is mature, it still attracts fresh capital.

## Outlook

The honest picture is of a mature industry at a crossroads. Commercial forecasts cited by MIDA project the Malaysian textile-manufacturing market growing from roughly **USD 3.49 billion in 2025 to about USD 4.47 billion by 2030** — steady, single-digit growth rather than a boom. Low-cost garment production will keep drifting to cheaper countries, and no incentive package can fully reverse that.

Where Malaysia can realistically win is in the higher-value, less labour-sensitive niches: engineered technical textiles, automated and sustainable manufacturing, and branded modest and heritage fashion. Whether the sector makes that transition — fast enough, and at scale — is the open question for its next decade.
