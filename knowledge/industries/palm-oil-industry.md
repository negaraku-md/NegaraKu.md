Palm oil is the world's most-traded vegetable oil, and Malaysia is its second-largest producer — behind Indonesia — turning a spiky red bunch of fruit grown from Johor to the hills of Sarawak into refined cooking oil, soap ingredients and biodiesel shipped to markets across Asia, Africa and Europe. The industry supports over 3 million Malaysians directly and indirectly, including nearly 500,000 smallholders, according to the Malaysian Palm Oil Council (MPOC). This is how that machine is built, from the tree to the tanker.

## How big is Malaysia's palm oil industry?

Big enough to move the national trade balance on its own. In 2025 Malaysia milled a record **20.28 million tonnes** of crude palm oil (CPO) — the highest in a decade and a 4.9% jump over the 19.34 million tonnes of 2024, according to the Malaysian Palm Oil Board (MPOB).

That oil grew on about **5.70 million hectares** of oil palm — an area a little larger than Croatia — spread unevenly across the country. Malaysia is the world's **second-largest producer and exporter** of palm oil, behind Indonesia, which produces several times as much.

| Indicator (2025) | Figure | Change vs 2024 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| CPO production | 20.28 million tonnes | +4.9% |
| Oil palm planted area | 5.70 million ha | +1.6% |
| Palm oil exports (volume) | 15.27 million tonnes | −9.6% |
| Total oil-palm-product exports (revenue) | RM112.43 billion | +2.8% |
| Average CPO price (local delivered) | RM4,292.50 / tonne | +2.7% |
| FFB yield (estates) | 17.77 tonnes / ha | +6.4% |
| Oil extraction rate (OER) | 19.74% | +0.4% |

*Source: MPOB, Overview of the Malaysian Oil Palm Industry in 2025.*

Notice that exports fell in volume while export revenue rose. That is the story of a firm-priced year: CPO averaged RM4,292.50 a tonne, and much stronger prices for palm kernel products and oleochemicals more than offset a smaller shipped tonnage. Palm oil ending stocks also swelled to **3.05 million tonnes** at end-2025 (up from 1.71 million a year earlier) — a signal worth watching, because heavy stocks usually weigh on the next season's price.

## Where does the oil actually come from — estates or smallholders?

Both, and the split matters. Malaysian oil palm land divides into three broad grower types:

- **Estates** — large private plantations run by listed groups such as Sime Darby Plantation, IOI and KLK, plus state agencies, managing thousands of hectares with their own mills.
- **Organised smallholders** — settlers on government land-development schemes, above all the Federal Land Development Authority (FELDA), plus FELCRA and RISDA, whose plots are managed collectively.
- **Independent smallholders** — farmers, usually working under 40 hectares, who own and run their own land, often as a family.

Geographically, the industry has drifted east. Sarawak is now the single largest oil-palm state at roughly **1.66 million hectares** (about 29% of the national total), followed by Sabah at **1.50 million** and the whole of Peninsular Malaysia at **2.54 million** (MPOB, 2025).

By sector, the 2022 government commodity statistics show estates and organised smallholders together working **85.6%** of planted area, with independent smallholders on **816,107 hectares (14.4%)**:

| Sector (2022) | Area (ha) | Share |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Estates & organised smallholders | 4,858,634 | 85.6% |
| Independent smallholders | 816,107 | 14.4% |
| **Total** | **5,674,742** | **100%** |

*Source: MPOB / Ministry of Plantation and Commodities, Statistics on Commodity 2023 (Table 1-2). Components are rounded to whole hectares and may not sum exactly to the official total.*

That table understates the human footprint. Counting organised and independent growers together, smallholders number close to **500,000 people**, and the industry as a whole supports **over 3 million Malaysians** directly and indirectly, according to the Malaysian Palm Oil Council (MPOC). Smallholders are therefore central to the sector's social footprint, not a marginal fringe.

## What happens between the tree and the tanker?

Palm oil is not sold as fruit — it is milled, refined and often chemically transformed before it leaves the country. The chain has clear, licensed stages.

1. **Field → Fresh Fruit Bunches (FFB).** A mature palm produces bunches year-round. Because the fruit starts degrading within hours of cutting, it must reach a mill fast — which is why mills sit close to plantations.
2. **Mill → Crude Palm Oil (CPO) + Palm Kernel.** The mill sterilises, threshes and presses the bunches. From the fruit's flesh comes CPO; from the seed comes the palm kernel. How much oil a mill squeezes out of each bunch is the **oil extraction rate (OER)** — nationally 19.74% in 2025.
3. **Kernel crusher → Palm Kernel Oil (PKO) + Palm Kernel Cake.** Crushers press the kernels into PKO (a lauric oil prized for soaps and confectionery) and leave a protein cake sold as animal feed.
4. **Refinery → RBD products.** Refineries turn crude oil into **Refined, Bleached and Deodorised (RBD)** palm oil, olein (the liquid cooking-oil fraction) and stearin (the solid fraction), plus by-products like palm fatty acid distillate (PFAD).
5. **Oleochemical & biodiesel plants → higher-value goods.** Oleochemical plants convert palm and kernel oil into fatty acids, glycerine, soap noodles and surfactants; biodiesel plants convert it into fuel.

As of 2022, Malaysia's downstream capacity looked like this:

| Facility (2022) | Number in operation | Annual capacity |
| --- | --- | --- |
| FFB mills | 450 | 119.36 million tonnes FFB |
| Palm kernel crushers | 41 | 7.35 million tonnes |
| Refineries | 52 | 26.90 million tonnes |
| Oleochemical plants | 22 | 2.69 million tonnes |

*Source: MPOB / Ministry of Plantation and Commodities, Statistics on Commodity 2023 (Table 1-13). Figures are for 2022.*

The strategic point is that Malaysia deliberately built the downstream. A tonne of crude oil is a commodity; a tonne of surfactant or specialty fat is a manufactured product with a fatter margin. That is why the country runs dozens of refineries and oleochemical plants rather than simply shipping crude.

## How much does the industry earn, and from what?

Total export earnings from oil-palm products reached **RM112.43 billion in 2025**, up 2.8% on 2024. The revenue is spread across the value chain:

| Export category (2025) | Revenue (RM billion) | Change vs 2024 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Palm oil | 71.63 | −1.8% |
| Oleochemicals | 19.39 | +11.3% |
| Palm kernel oil | 9.21 | +35.7% |
| Palm-based finished products | 4.87 | +12.8% |
| Other palm products | 4.18 | −19.3% |
| Biodiesel | 1.86 | +48.1% |
| Palm kernel cake | 1.30 | −12.9% |
| **Total** | **112.43** | **+2.8%** |

*Source: MPOB, Overview 2025. Components are rounded and may not sum exactly to the total.*

The mix tells the story. Revenue from crude and refined palm oil itself actually **dipped 1.8%**, so 2025's overall growth came entirely from the downstream and kernel products. Oleochemicals now earn RM19.39 billion — roughly a sixth of the total — and biodiesel export revenue **leapt 48.1% year on year**, riding regional biofuel mandates and firmer prices.

The customers are concentrated. India retained its position as Malaysia's largest palm oil market for the **twelfth consecutive year (since 2014)**, taking **2.66 million tonnes (17.4%)** in 2025. Notably, **Kenya emerged as the second-largest destination, surpassing both the EU and China, at 1.21 million tonnes (7.9%)**, followed by the EU (1.03 million, 6.8%) and China (0.90 million, 5.9%). The top seven markets — India, Kenya, the EU, China, Türkiye, the Philippines and Japan — together bought **7.86 million tonnes, or 51.5%** of all Malaysian palm oil exports (MPOB, 2025).

## Who governs the industry, and what is MSPO?

One agency sits at the centre: the **Malaysian Palm Oil Board (MPOB)**, a statutory body under the Ministry of Plantation and Commodities. MPOB licenses every link in the chain — you cannot legally operate a nursery, mill, refinery or dealer without an MPOB licence — and it runs the sector's research and publishes the statistics this article relies on.

Around MPOB sit specialised bodies: the **Malaysian Palm Oil Council (MPOC)** handles promotion and market access abroad, while trade associations represent planters, millers and refiners.

Palm oil is also one of Malaysia's most contested industries. Its expansion has been linked to deforestation and peatland loss, especially in Borneo, and to biodiversity impacts on species such as orangutans; the plantation sector has separately faced scrutiny over migrant- and forced-labour practices. These are the concerns behind measures such as the **EU Deforestation Regulation (EUDR)** and import restrictions in several markets — and they are disputed, with the Malaysian government and industry bodies arguing that palm oil is more land-efficient than rival oilseeds and that much criticism overstates current practice.

Malaysia's principal policy response is **Malaysian Sustainable Palm Oil (MSPO)** certification, which the government has made mandatory across the whole supply chain, from smallholders and estates through to mills and dealers. MSPO runs on the **MS 2530:2022** family of standards, owned by the Department of Standards Malaysia, with a separate chain-of-custody standard — **MS 2751:2022, Chain of Custody of Oil Palm Biomass** — for tracking certified material through the supply chain. Independent smallholders are the group hardest to certify and the segment MSPO was most designed to bring into the fold. For exporters, MSPO is increasingly a condition of market access in sustainability-sensitive markets, though NGOs and some importers question how fully certification addresses deforestation and labour concerns.

## How does Malaysia compare with Indonesia?

The two neighbours together supply the overwhelming majority of the world's palm oil, but they play different games.

| | Malaysia | Indonesia |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Global rank | 2nd largest producer & exporter | Largest producer & exporter |
| Relative scale | ~20 million tonnes CPO (2025) | Several times larger |
| Strategic tilt | Downstream: refineries, oleochemicals, specialty products | Upstream volume + fast-growing domestic biodiesel |
| Certification | MSPO (national, MS 2530:2022) | ISPO (national) |
| Domestic biofuel pull | Biodiesel blending, but export-oriented | Aggressive blending mandates (e.g. B-series) that absorb output |

*Malaysia rank and scale: MPOB and MPOC. Comparison is directional, not a like-for-like statistic.*

The practical takeaway: Indonesia sets the world's supply weather, and its biodiesel mandates — including a planned B50 blend from 2026 — can tighten global supply and lift prices, which is partly why Malaysian CPO firmed in 2025. Malaysia competes less on raw volume and more on refining, certification and higher-value products.

## A quick framework for reading the industry

If you are trying to make sense of a palm oil headline, run it through four questions:

1. **Which stage?** Cultivation (area, yield), milling (production, OER), or downstream (refined products, oleochemicals)? A "record production" story is upstream; a "margin squeeze" story is usually downstream.
2. **Volume or value?** As 2025 showed, exports can fall in tonnes yet rise in ringgit. Always check which one moved.
3. **Price driver.** CPO prices track soybean oil, Indonesian policy and biodiesel demand more than Malaysian output alone. A price move often starts outside the country.
4. **Stocks.** Rising ending stocks (3.05 million tonnes at end-2025) tend to pull future prices down; tight stocks push them up.

## Common mistakes and misconceptions

- **"Palm oil is just cooking oil."** Only part of it. Oleochemicals (RM19.39 billion in 2025) turn it into soap, detergent, cosmetics and industrial inputs — a bigger export earner than palm kernel oil.
- **"It's all giant corporate plantations."** Independent smallholders alone hold 14.4% of planted area, and smallholders overall number close to half a million people. The industry is more decentralised than its image suggests.
- **"More hectares means more oil."** Not necessarily. Yield and oil extraction rate matter more; 2025's production record came largely from better FFB yields and OER, not new land — planted area rose only 1.6%.
- **"Malaysia is the world's biggest producer."** It is second. Indonesia is substantially larger, and its policies often set the global price.
- **"Certification is optional branding."** MSPO is Malaysia's mandatory national standard, and market access increasingly depends on it. Equally, critics argue certification alone does not resolve every deforestation or labour concern, so it is neither pure marketing nor a full guarantee.
- **"Peninsular Malaysia is the heart of the industry."** By area, Borneo now leads: Sarawak is the largest single oil-palm state, and Sabah is not far behind.

## How the industry got here

Oil palm arrived in Malaya as an ornamental curiosity in **1875**. Commercial planting began in **1917**, when French planter Henri Fauconnier established the first estate at Tennamaram, in Batang Berjuntai, Selangor, and built the first mill. For decades it stayed a minor sibling to rubber.

The turning point was **1961**, when FELDA began settling landless families on oil palm smallholdings — converting a colonial plantation crop into a national poverty-reduction programme. Downstream ambition followed: **palm-based oleochemical production started around 1980**, and by **1989** oil palm had overtaken rubber as Malaysia's leading agricultural crop. The regulator in its current form, MPOB, was created in **2000** by merging earlier research and licensing bodies (a date the human reviewer should confirm against MPOB's own records).

## What's next

The industry's near-term challenges are visible in its own numbers: an ageing tree stock that needs replanting, a persistent labour squeeze in the plantations, and swelling stocks that pressure prices. Alongside these sit mounting scrutiny of the sector's environmental footprint — deforestation, peatland and biodiversity — and of its labour practices, reflected in measures such as the EU Deforestation Regulation. Malaysia's stated bet is to climb the value chain (more oleochemicals, more biodiesel, more certified oil) rather than clear more land, while contesting the criticisms it regards as overstated.
