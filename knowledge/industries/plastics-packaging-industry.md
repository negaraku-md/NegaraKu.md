Almost every physical product that leaves a Malaysian factory — a packet of instant noodles, a smartphone, a car bumper, a length of water pipe — depends on plastic that was shaped somewhere in the country. Plastics are one of Malaysia's quiet industrial strengths: less glamorous than semiconductors, but woven into nearly every other manufacturing sector and, increasingly, into the national debate about waste and sustainability.

This guide explains what the plastics and packaging industry is, how big it is, where its raw material comes from, what it makes, who the main players are, and how circular-economy policy and plastic-waste rules are reshaping it.

## What the industry is

The plastics industry is a **converting** industry. It buys polymer resin — supplied as pellets — and turns it, through processes such as extrusion, injection moulding, blow moulding and thermoforming, into finished or semi-finished goods. In Malaysia, packaging dominates, but the same factories also feed the electronics, automotive, construction and consumer-goods industries.

Malaysia's advantage is a combination that few neighbours match: a mature manufacturing base, a local supply of petrochemical resin, decades of engineering know-how, and competitive input, land, labour and energy costs. That has made it an export platform rather than merely a domestic supplier.

## How big is it?

Precise, up-to-date national figures are surprisingly hard to pin down, because different bodies draw the industry's boundary differently. The most-cited recent estimate comes from the US International Trade Administration, which put Malaysia's plastics-manufacturing **turnover at about US$13.1 billion in 2023**, of which roughly **US$3.4 billion was directly exported**. (These are secondary estimates and should be confirmed against Malaysian primary data — see the verification note on this page.)

By end-market, the same source gives an approximate split of **packaging ~45%, electrical & electronics ~28%, automotive ~12% and construction ~6%**. Within packaging, Malaysia has carved out a genuinely world-class niche: it is described as **Asia's largest producer of stretch film**, accounting for a significant share of global supply. Film and sheet are the largest single export category, sold mainly to the United States, the European Union, Singapore, Australia and Japan.

A note on employment: a single authoritative national headcount was not found for this draft. MIDA investment data for the plastics sector has reported dozens of approved projects and several thousand new jobs in a single year, but a confirmed total should be taken from MIDA, the Department of Statistics Malaysia (DOSM) and the Malaysian Plastics Manufacturers Association (MPMA).

## The link to petrochemicals

The industry does not begin with pellets — it begins with oil and gas. Plastics sit at the **downstream** end of Malaysia's [chemicals and petrochemicals industry](/en/industries/chemicals-petrochemicals-industry/), which cracks hydrocarbons into the building blocks of polymers.

Malaysia makes much of its own resin. **PETRONAS Chemicals Group** and **Lotte Chemical Titan** are the major producers of commodity polymers such as polyethylene (PE) and polypropylene (PP). PETRONAS's large Pengerang Integrated Complex (part of the RAPID project) in Johor added substantial downstream polymer capacity — including PE, PP and HDPE lines — deepening the country's ability to feed its own converters. This vertical integration, from refinery to resin to finished packaging, is a structural strength: it shortens supply chains and cushions converters against some of the volatility of imported raw materials. Even so, specialty grades are still imported, and resin prices track global oil markets closely.

## What the industry makes

The sector spans several distinct segments:

- **Packaging** — the largest by far. Flexible packaging (stretch film, shrink film, laminates, pouches, food wraps and industrial bags) is the flagship, alongside rigid packaging such as bottles, trays and containers. This segment serves food and beverage, logistics and e-commerce.
- **Electrical & electronics (E&E)** — precision moulded components, housings and insulation that feed Malaysia's huge E&E and semiconductor supply chains.
- **Automotive** — bumpers, trims, dashboards and under-the-bonnet parts, as carmakers replace metal and glass with lighter plastics.
- **Construction** — pipes, fittings, cladding and polymer-based building materials.
- **Consumer and household goods** — a long tail of moulded everyday products.

For the packaging segment specifically, demand has been forecast to grow at around a 5% compound annual rate over the medium term, driven by food and beverage consumption and e-commerce, though that figure is an analyst estimate rather than an official projection.

## Key players

Malaysia's plastics sector is notable for a cluster of publicly listed converters that have grown into regional and global suppliers:

- **Scientex** — one of the country's largest packaging manufacturers, strong in industrial and consumer flexible packaging (and also a major property developer).
- **Thong Guan Industries** — a leading stretch-film and flexible-packaging producer that has partnered with resin majors on thinner, higher-performance films and pushed into Europe and the US.
- **BP Plastics Holding** — a specialist in stretch and blown film.
- **Daibochi** — a flexible-packaging maker serving food, beverage and fast-moving consumer goods.
- **SLP Resources** — focused on higher-value and recyclable mono-material films.

Upstream, the resin side is more consolidated, dominated by PETRONAS Chemicals and Lotte Chemical Titan. (Company specifics change with corporate actions and should be checked against current filings.)

## Plastic waste, recycling and the circular economy

No part of the industry has changed faster than its relationship with waste. After **China banned most plastic-waste imports in 2018**, Malaysia became one of the world's leading destinations for imported scrap plastic. Imports surged, illegal recycling operations proliferated — especially around Klang — and the government responded with a widely reported crackdown that closed scores of illegal facilities and shipped contaminated containers back to their countries of origin.

That episode pushed sustainability to the centre of policy. The **Malaysia Plastics Sustainability Roadmap 2021–2030**, led by the environment ministry, sets time-bound national targets: phasing out problematic single-use plastics, lifting the recycling rate for post-consumer plastic packaging, making plastic packaging fully recyclable, raising recycled content, and collecting the large majority of plastic waste for recycling by 2030. It also introduces an **Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR)** scheme, under which producers help fund the collection and recycling of the packaging they put on the market.

Import controls have tightened in parallel. Under rules reported to take effect in 2025, importers of plastic waste (HS code 39.15) must obtain a **Certificate of Approval from SIRIM**, backed by pre-shipment inspection and factory audits — an attempt to keep genuine recycling feedstock flowing to legitimate processors while shutting out dirty, unrecyclable scrap. Policymakers frame these measures as part of the New Industrial Master Plan 2030 and a circular-economy agenda for manufacturing.

## Incentives

Investment is steered by [MIDA](/en/government/mida/). Plastics manufacturing and, in particular, plastic-waste recycling can qualify for Malaysia's main manufacturing incentives under the Promotion of Investments Act 1986 — notably **Pioneer Status** (an income-tax exemption) or an **Investment Tax Allowance** on qualifying capital expenditure. Recycling and higher-value, sustainable activities are increasingly favoured, aligning the tax framework with the circular-economy direction of policy. Exact rates and conditions change and should be confirmed with MIDA directly.

## Outlook

Malaysia's plastics industry enters the second half of the 2020s from a position of real strength: a world-leading stretch-film franchise, deep integration with local petrochemicals, and a diversified customer base across the region's manufacturing economy. Its challenges are equally clear — exposure to oil-linked resin prices, rising labour and energy costs, and intense pressure to decarbonise and "design out" waste.

The through-line is a shift from volume to value and from linear to circular: lighter and recyclable mono-material films, recycled content, and processing capacity for genuine recycling feedstock rather than imported scrap. How well the industry manages that transition — without losing its cost competitiveness — is the defining question for its next decade.
