Somewhere in Cyberjaya or Kuala Lumpur right now, an accountant is closing the monthly books for a company headquartered in London, a help-desk agent is resolving a ticket for a customer in Sydney, and a data analyst is building a dashboard for a factory in Germany. None of those employers manufactures anything in Malaysia. What they run here is something less visible but increasingly valuable: their global back office.

That is the Global Business Services (GBS) industry — one of Malaysia's quiet economic success stories, and consistently one of the most competitive in the world.

## What "Global Business Services" actually means

GBS is an umbrella term for the business of running a multinational's internal operations from a central location, usually offshore. It covers three overlapping models:

- **Shared services centres** — in-house units that consolidate a company's own back-office functions (finance and accounting, human resources, IT, procurement) into one hub serving many countries.
- **Business process outsourcing (BPO)** — the same kind of work, but contracted out to a third-party provider rather than kept in-house. Call centres and contact centres are the best-known example.
- **Global capability centres (GCCs)** — captive offshore centres that go beyond routine processing into higher-value knowledge work: data analytics, software engineering, cybersecurity, product design and research.

The industry has steadily moved up this ladder — from low-cost transaction processing toward analytics, automation and AI-enabled services. That shift is the central theme of Malaysia's current strategy.

## How big is it?

Precise numbers are slippery because different bodies define the sector differently, but the direction is unambiguous. The **GBS Malaysia Strategy 2022–2027 mid-term report** — produced by GBS Malaysia (a chapter of the national tech association PIKOM, formerly Outsourcing Malaysia) with support from MDEC — counts roughly **750 GBS companies** operating in the country and projects sector revenue of about **US$7 billion (RM28 billion) by 2025** *(figures to verify; see the note on this page)*.

Growth has been rapid. Reported figures include GBS investment of around **RM9.87 billion in 2024** — up from about RM730 million in 2021 — and **more than 36,000 high-value jobs** created since the strategy launched *(to verify)*. Industry bodies cite a trained workforce of **more than 250,000 people** across customer experience, digital technology, finance, accounting and HR roles. An earlier projection from research firm IDC valued the industry at about US$4.95 billion in 2020, growing to US$6.7 billion by 2025.

## Why multinationals choose Malaysia

Malaysia's standing is best captured by **Kearney's Global Services Location Index (GSLI)**, the industry's most-watched ranking. In the 2023 edition — covering 78 countries across 52 metrics (financial attractiveness, people skills and availability, business environment and digital resonance) — Malaysia placed **third in the world, behind only India and China**, a position it has held with remarkable consistency.

The reasons behind that ranking are the industry's core selling points:

- **Cost.** Operating and salary costs are well below those in Singapore, Australia or Western markets, while quality remains high.
- **A multilingual talent pool.** This is Malaysia's distinctive edge. Beyond widely spoken English, the workforce covers Malay, Mandarin, Cantonese, Tamil and other languages — letting a single Malaysian hub serve customers across much of Asia and the English-speaking world.
- **Infrastructure and connectivity.** Decades of investment since the 1990s Multimedia Super Corridor built the data centres, fibre and office stock the sector needs.
- **Time zone and stability.** Malaysia's hours overlap usefully with both Asia-Pacific and, at the margins, Europe, within a politically stable, business-friendly environment.

## Where the hubs are

The industry is geographically concentrated:

- **Greater Kuala Lumpur** is the centre of gravity, with the **Tun Razak Exchange (TRX)** emerging as a magnet for finance-related players.
- **Cyberjaya**, the original Multimedia Super Corridor cybercentre, remains a focal point for IT-heavy operations and was where many pioneers first landed.
- **Selangor** rounds out the Klang Valley cluster; together, Selangor and Kuala Lumpur reportedly accounted for around **87% of new GBS companies** established between 2021 and mid-2025 *(to verify)*.
- **Penang** (especially Bayan Lepas) is the other established hub, blending GBS with its deep electronics base.

Industry leaders have urged the sector to spread beyond the Klang Valley, with **Johor, Perak, Sabah and Sarawak** all evaluating specialised centres rather than copying existing hubs.

## Anchor companies and the GCC trend

Malaysia's GBS story is told through its anchor tenants. **HSBC** runs a long-established Global Service Centre — incorporated in 2002, operational in Cyberjaya from 2003, and since 2022 housed in its own tower at Tun Razak Exchange, supporting customers across 39 markets. **AMD** operates GBS facilities in both Penang and Cyberjaya alongside its engineering presence. Over the years, multinationals including Shell, BP, DHL, Dell, Prudential and others have based shared-services or regional support operations in the country.

The most important recent shift is the rise of the **global capability centre**. Rather than offshoring only routine transactions, companies now locate analytics, engineering and R&D teams in Malaysia — work that is harder to automate and pays more. Attracting and retaining these higher-value GCCs is the explicit goal of current policy, because they anchor skilled jobs and are far stickier than a cost-driven call centre.

## Incentives: the Malaysia Digital programme

The main policy anchor is **Malaysia Digital (MD)** — the rebranded successor to MSC Malaysia — administered by the **Malaysia Digital Economy Corporation (MDEC)**. Companies awarded MD status in approved digital and services activities can qualify for tax incentives and other facilitation. As of 31 March 2024, more than 5,300 companies held MD status (over 73% of them local), a figure reported above 6,000 by 2025 *(to verify)*.

In 2024, MDEC introduced two **outcome-based tax incentives** — the **New Investment Incentive** and the **Expansion Incentive** — targeting technology enablers such as AI, big-data analytics, the Internet of Things, cloud and cybersecurity, with applications open through 31 December 2027. Because incentive terms change frequently, any investor should confirm current eligibility and rates directly with MDEC or a tax adviser rather than relying on general summaries.

## Outlook

Malaysia's GBS sector sits at a confident but competitive moment. It has graduated from a low-cost back-office destination into a top-three global services location, and its ambitions now centre on moving further up the value chain — more GCCs, more analytics and AI-enabled work, and more geographic spread beyond the Klang Valley.

The challenges are equally clear: a persistent talent crunch as demand for digital skills outpaces supply, wage inflation eroding the cost advantage, and sharpening competition from India, the Philippines, Vietnam and others. The sector's future depends less on winning the next call centre than on becoming the place where multinationals base the brains of their regional operations.
