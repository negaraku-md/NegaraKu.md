Malaysia sits astride some of the oldest rainforest on Earth, and for much of its modern history the trade in tropical hardwood was one of the pillars of the national economy. That upstream world — the forests themselves, the licences to log them, and the first processing of raw logs into timber — is a distinct industry from the furniture factories and wood-products exporters further down the chain. This guide covers the **forestry and timber resource sector**: where the wood comes from, who controls it, how it is exported, and the sustainability and market pressures now reshaping it.

## What the industry is

The forestry and timber industry is the **upstream, resource end** of Malaysia's wood economy. It spans the management of natural forests and forest plantations, the harvesting of logs, and the production of primary wood products — chiefly sawn timber, plywood and other wood-based panels, along with veneer, mouldings and wood chips.

This is deliberately narrower than "the wood industry" as a whole. The high-value **furniture and finished wood-products** sector — which turns Malaysian and imported timber into exportable goods — is the downstream manufacturing end and is usually counted separately. It is also quite separate from the cultural craft of wood carving. What ties the upstream sector together is that its primary asset is the forest itself, a natural resource that in Malaysia is owned and managed overwhelmingly by the states.

## Forest cover and exports

Malaysia remains heavily forested. Government statements have put total forest cover at well over half the country's land area — figures in the range of 55 to 56 per cent are commonly cited, though the exact number depends on how "forest" is defined and should be checked against current Forestry Department data. Peninsular Malaysia alone reported around 42.95 per cent forest cover (about 5.69 million hectares) in a 2023 parliamentary reply, with a stated aim of reaching 50 per cent by 2040. Much of the forest managed for timber is legally designated as **Permanent Reserved Forest (PRF)** — land gazetted to stay under forest and managed on a sustained-yield basis, split between production forest and protection forest.

On the trade side, timber is a major but no longer dominant export. The **Ministry of Plantation and Commodities** reported timber product exports of about **RM22.9 billion in 2024**, up roughly 4.9 per cent on 2023. That made timber the country's **third-largest commodity export**, far behind palm oil (around RM114 billion) and rubber (around RM33 billion). Wooden furniture and plywood are consistently among the largest earners within that basket, though reliable full-year 2024 product-level figures should be confirmed against official trade tables before being quoted (see the verification notes).

## Why Sabah and Sarawak hold the cards

One feature sets Malaysia's forestry sector apart from most of its other industries: **land and forests are state matters, not federal ones**, under the Federal Constitution. Each state gazettes its own forests, issues its own logging licences, sets timber royalties and grants plantation leases. The federal government's role is largely advisory — research, training, coordination and national policy, channelled through bodies such as the National Land Council.

In practice this means the two Borneo states, **Sabah and Sarawak**, control most of the resource, because that is where the largest areas of productive natural forest lie. They also operate under their own distinct legislation — Sabah broadly under its Forest Enactment and state forest policy, and Sarawak under its Forests Ordinance — rather than the Peninsular framework. The upshot is that forest policy, revenue and enforcement in Malaysia are highly decentralised, and headline national figures can mask very different realities between the peninsula and Borneo.

## Sustainable forest management and certification

For decades Malaysia has sought to show that its timber is produced on a sustainable, sustained-yield basis rather than by mining the forest. The main instrument is the **Malaysian Timber Certification Scheme (MTCS)**, run by the **Malaysian Timber Certification Council (MTCC)**. MTCS certifies both forest management at the level of individual forest management units and the **chain of custody** that tracks certified timber from forest to product.

Crucially, MTCS is **endorsed by PEFC** (the Programme for the Endorsement of Forest Certification), the world's largest forest-certification system. That endorsement lets Malaysian certified timber be recognised as sustainably sourced in major importing markets. Reports put the MTCS-certified forest area at close to **5.9 million hectares**, having grown substantially over the past decade as more units in Sabah, Sarawak and peninsular states were certified. Some operators additionally pursue **FSC (Forest Stewardship Council)** certification, which carries weight with particular buyers. Certification is not a formality: it requires audited management plans, biodiversity and social safeguards, and credible traceability.

## Deforestation and the EUDR pressure

Tropical-forest loss is a genuinely contested subject, and Malaysia's record has drawn international scrutiny over the years, particularly around conversion of natural forest to plantations and the legality of some clearing. The picture is mixed and politically sensitive; the figures and their interpretation vary widely between government, industry and campaign sources, and are best approached with care and attribution rather than blanket claims.

What is concrete is the market pressure now arriving through regulation. The **EU Deforestation Regulation (EUDR)** will require that timber (and other commodities such as palm oil and rubber) sold into the EU be **deforestation-free and produced legally**, backed by geolocation data and due-diligence statements. After a delay to its IT and benchmarking systems, enforcement is now reported to apply from around the end of 2026 for large operators, with small enterprises following in 2027. The EU's country-benchmarking system will classify producing nations as low, standard or high risk; Malaysia's classification was still undecided at the time these sources were consulted. For exporters, EUDR raises the bar from "certified sustainable" to "demonstrably traceable and legal," and MTCS certification helps but does not by itself guarantee compliance.

## The link downstream, and the outlook

The upstream sector exists to feed a downstream one. Logs and sawn timber become plywood, panels, mouldings and, ultimately, the furniture and finished wood products that earn Malaysia its highest wood-sector margins. Policy increasingly emphasises moving value **downstream** — processing more timber domestically rather than exporting raw logs — and reforestation and forest-plantation programmes to secure future supply as natural-forest harvests are constrained.

The outlook is one of managed transition. Malaysia is balancing a long-standing export industry and the state revenues it generates against tightening sustainability expectations at home and abroad, a shift toward plantation and certified timber, and the compliance demands of markets like the EU.
