Most of the fish on a Malaysian dinner plate still comes from the sea — but a fast-growing share now comes from a pond, a cage or a long-line raft. That is aquaculture: the deliberate farming of aquatic life, as opposed to catching what swims in the wild. As Malaysia's wild catch plateaus and demand for protein climbs, aquaculture has become the country's main lever for keeping fish on the table. This article goes deep on the farmed side of the business; for the wider picture, including the fishing fleet, fishermen's welfare and enforcement, see the [fisheries industry overview](/en/agriculture/malaysian-fisheries-industry/).

## How big is Malaysia's aquaculture industry?

Aquaculture is no longer a sideline. The Department of Fisheries Malaysia (DOF) reported roughly **492,680 tonnes** of aquaculture production worth about **RM3.9 billion** in 2023, with output rising to around **510,000 tonnes** in 2024. Set against total edible-fish production of 1.79 million tonnes in 2023, farmed output now accounts for close to **30%** of all fishery production — and that share is designed to grow.

The strategic logic is simple. Capture fisheries are limited by what the sea can sustainably yield, and Malaysia's catch has broadly flattened. Aquaculture, by contrast, can be scaled with investment. That is why the government's target, under the National Agrofood Policy, is to shift the balance of edible-fish output toward farming — aiming over time at an **aquaculture-to-capture ratio of roughly 60:40**, a near-reversal of the historical mix.

## What does Malaysia farm, and where?

The industry divides naturally by water type.

### Brackish-water aquaculture

Coastal, estuarine and marine systems produce the highest-value species:

- **Shrimp and prawns** — the single biggest earner. Whiteleg shrimp (*Litopenaeus vannamei*, locally *udang putih* or *udang vannamei*) dominates pond farming, alongside tiger prawns (*udang harimau*). National shrimp output was about **152,790 tonnes in 2022**.
- **Marine finfish** — seabass (*siakap*) and grouper (*kerapu*), farmed largely in floating cages, command premium prices in domestic and export markets.
- **Cockles** (*kerang*) — farmed on mudflats, especially along the west coast of the peninsula, a traditional and high-volume shellfish.

### Freshwater aquaculture

Inland ponds, tanks and cages focus on affordable, fast-growing table fish:

- **Tilapia** — including GIFT and red (*tilapia merah*) strains — a workhorse protein farmed nationwide.
- **Catfish** (*keli*) and **patin** (silver catfish) — cheap, hardy and widely consumed.
- Other species such as *lampam* and *kelah* fill niche and premium segments.

### Seaweed — Sabah's speciality

Seaweed farming is overwhelmingly a **Sabah** story. The state produced roughly **225,048 tonnes valued at about RM100 million in 2023**, the large majority of national output, concentrated around **Semporna** and nearby east-coast districts such as Tawau, Kunak and Lahad Datu. Farmers grow *Kappaphycus* and *Eucheuma* species — sources of carrageenan, a thickener used across the food industry — and the **long-line method** is used by the overwhelming majority of them. Seaweed is a rare aquaculture activity with low capital barriers, making it an important coastal livelihood.

## Who runs the sector?

Two agencies anchor the industry, both under the **Ministry of Agriculture and Food Security (MAFS / KPKM)**:

- The **Department of Fisheries Malaysia (DOF / Jabatan Perikanan Malaysia)** is the regulator. It licenses farms, runs extension and hatchery services, manages biosecurity, and operates **MyGAP** — the Malaysian Good Agricultural Practice certification for responsible aquaculture.
- The **Fisheries Development Authority (LKIM / Lembaga Kemajuan Ikan Malaysia)** handles development, marketing, infrastructure and the welfare of fishing and farming communities.

The flagship policy instrument is the **National Agrofood Policy 2021–2030 (NAP 2.0)**, which treats fisheries and aquaculture as one of its four agrofood sub-sectors. On the ground, its main delivery vehicle is the **Aquaculture Industrial Zone (ZIA / Zon Industri Akuakultur)** programme — designated areas with shared infrastructure for fish, shrimp and shellfish farming. Backed by RM18 million under the 12th Malaysia Plan, **54 ZIA sites** have been identified; in 2024 their farms produced **9,044 tonnes worth RM684 million** across 12,730 hectares. For the broader agricultural context, see the [Agriculture sector overview](/en/agriculture/agriculture-sector-overview/).

## Export versus food security

Malaysian aquaculture serves two masters. Shrimp, grouper, seabass and seaweed (and seaweed-derived carrageenan) are **export** products that earn foreign exchange and feed processing by the [food & beverage manufacturing](/en/industries/food-beverage-manufacturing-industry/) sector. At the same time, tilapia, catfish and cockles are **domestic food-security** staples that keep affordable protein in local markets.

The food-security case is the stronger policy driver. Fish is Malaysians' main animal protein, and the national **fish self-sufficiency ratio** stood at about **90.7% in 2025**. Nudging that figure higher — and insulating it from imported-fish price shocks — is precisely why policy favours expanding farmed output.

## Disease, feed and sustainability challenges

Growth is not guaranteed. The sector faces several structural pressures:

- **Disease and biosecurity.** Intensive shrimp farming is vulnerable to outbreaks such as white spot and early mortality syndrome, which can wipe out a pond in days. Robust hatchery health, disease-free broodstock and biosecurity are make-or-break.
- **Feed costs.** Much fish feed depends on imported fishmeal and soy, so farm margins are hostage to global commodity prices and the ringgit's exchange rate.
- **Environmental sustainability.** Pond expansion has been linked to mangrove loss, while effluent, antibiotic use and escapes raise water-quality and biodiversity concerns. Certification schemes such as MyGAP are the main response, but uptake is uneven.
- **Fragmentation.** A long tail of small, under-capitalised farmers struggles to meet the biosecurity and traceability standards that export markets demand — the problem the ZIA clusters are meant to solve.

## Outlook

The direction of travel is clear: Malaysia intends to farm a larger share of its fish, consolidate farmers into managed zones, and climb toward higher-value, better-certified products for both export and domestic tables. Whether it hits the 60:40 aquaculture-to-capture ambition depends less on ponds and cages than on controlling disease, taming feed costs and making sustainability credible to buyers. The raw potential — a long coastline, year-round warmth and strong regional demand — is not in doubt; execution is the variable to watch.
