Every factory runs on machines — and those machines have to be built by someone. The machinery & equipment (M&E) industry is the part of manufacturing that makes the production lines, process machinery, metalworking tools, power systems and automation that every *other* industry depends on. It is the least visible big industry in the economy and, for exactly that reason, one of the most strategically important.

In Malaysia, M&E is treated as a priority high-technology sector by the Malaysian Investment Development Authority (MIDA). It is the quiet engine behind the country's headline export stories: the test-and-assembly equipment that keeps the semiconductor industry running, the mills and presses behind palm oil, and the lines that move the food industry. This guide explains what the industry is, how big it is, its main segments, the sectors it enables, its key players, and the policy pushing it up the value chain.

## What the industry is

M&E is best understood as an **enabler** industry. Its products are not sold to consumers; they are sold to other manufacturers as the capital equipment those manufacturers need to make their own goods. The government's long-standing framework groups the sector into four broad families: **power-generating machinery**, **metalworking machinery** (machine tools), **specialised process machinery** built for a particular industry, and **general industrial machinery and parts**. To these, Malaysia's current strategy adds a fast-growing fifth strand — **industrial automation and robotics**.

A defining feature of the Malaysian industry is its structure: it is overwhelmingly made up of small and medium enterprises. MIDA states that over 90% of M&E companies are SMEs (an older MIDA figure put it at over 85%), many of them precision-engineering and tooling specialists that feed larger manufacturers and multinationals. These figures should be confirmed against MIDA; see the verification note on this page.

## How big is it?

M&E is a significant export earner in its own right. According to MIDA's machinery & equipment sector page, **total M&E exports rose from about RM41.6 billion in 2019 to about RM78.2 billion in 2025**, and **specialised machinery exports more than doubled, from about RM16.1 billion to roughly RM42.1 billion** over the same period — a sign that Malaysia is shifting toward higher-value, more specialised equipment rather than commodity machinery. The main export markets are ASEAN, the United States and China.

Investment interest has tracked that growth. MIDA reports that approved M&E investments reached **RM11 billion across 138 projects in 2025**, of which about 76.4% (RM8.4 billion) was foreign, with the projects expected to create around 10,436 jobs. Broader trade data cited in a Germany Trade & Invest (GTAI) report — drawing on Malaysian statistics — put production growth at about 4% in 2024 and machinery exports (SITC 71-74) at roughly US$15.1 billion, with an estimated 2,000 larger companies across the machinery supply chain. All of these figures carry definitional differences between sources and are flagged for verification.

## The main segments

**Specialised process machinery** is Malaysia's standout strength. The country has built deep capability in **automated test, assembly and handling equipment for the semiconductor and electronics industry**, alongside **palm-oil milling and processing machinery** and **food and beverage processing lines**. MIDA also points to deepwater production equipment for oil and gas, precision moulding and power generation.

**Metalworking machinery and machine tools** form the physical foundation beneath all of this. MATRADE lists Malaysian-made machine tools and parts including laser-cutting machines, machining centres, electro-discharge machines (EDM), and milling and forging machines. Precision machining, tooling and high-tolerance metal fabrication are what make the more complex automated systems possible in the first place.

**Industrial automation and robotics** is the growth frontier. As wages rise and customers demand consistency, Malaysian firms are moving from building individual machines to integrating full automated systems — robotic cells, vision-based inspection and smart-factory subsystems — the capabilities most directly rewarded under the country's Industry 4.0 agenda.

## The enabler role

The clearest way to see M&E's importance is through the industries it serves. The [electrical & electronics](/en/industries/electrical-electronics-industry/) and [semiconductor](/en/industries/semiconductor-industry/) sectors — Malaysia's largest exporters — depend on precisely the automated test and assembly equipment that M&E firms supply; a November 2025 industry release described the sector as the "silent strength" powering semiconductor manufacturing. Palm oil relies on specialised milling and extraction machinery, and food processing on automated packaging and handling lines. A strong domestic M&E base lets Malaysia build, service and upgrade this equipment locally rather than importing it, keeping more value — and more engineering know-how — inside the country. That is why M&E sits inside the broader [manufacturing sector](/en/industries/manufacturing-industry-overview/) as connective tissue rather than a standalone silo.

## Key players

Malaysia's most prominent M&E names are concentrated in semiconductor and electronics equipment. MIDA highlights home-grown, listed companies such as **ViTrox Corporation** (automated vision inspection), **Greatech Technology** (automation solutions), **Pentamaster Corporation** (factory automation and test equipment) and **UWC Berhad** (precision engineering and contract manufacturing). Around them sits a dense base of SME tooling, mould-making and precision-machining suppliers, many clustered in the northern corridor near the Penang electronics ecosystem.

## Incentives and Industry4WRD

Because M&E is a MIDA priority high-technology industry, qualifying projects are eligible for investment incentives, and the sector is a central beneficiary of the **Industry4WRD** national policy on Industry 4.0, launched by MITI in October 2018. Industry4WRD pushes manufacturers to adopt automation, data and smart-factory practices, supported by tools such as the Industry4WRD Readiness Assessment and funding including the **Industry4WRD Intervention Fund** (a matching grant, reported at 70:30 and capped at RM500,000) and the **Domestic Investment Strategic Fund** (reported at 60:40) for firms that complete the assessment. These parameters should be confirmed against MIDA's current incentive guidance. The over-arching direction now comes from the **New Industrial Master Plan (NIMP) 2030** and, for the semiconductor-equipment strand, the **National Semiconductor Strategy**, both of which push Malaysia toward higher-value, higher-technology machinery — including front-end semiconductor equipment.

## Outlook

Malaysia's M&E industry is quietly repositioning itself from a maker of general machinery toward a maker of specialised, high-technology equipment — especially for semiconductors, where global supply-chain diversification is drawing fresh investment. The strengths are real: a deep SME engineering base, proven specialised-machinery niches, and listed automation champions. The challenge is scale and sophistication — moving more SMEs up the technology ladder, deepening front-end semiconductor-equipment capability, and competing with established machine-tool nations. If the NIMP 2030 and Industry4WRD agenda succeed, the industry that builds Malaysia's factories could become one of its most valuable exports in its own right.
