Every time a Malaysian fills a prescription for an antibiotic, a painkiller or a blood-pressure tablet, there is a good chance the medicine was made in Malaysia — not invented here, but formulated, pressed, filled and packaged in a local factory working to international manufacturing standards. That is the heart of Malaysia's pharmaceutical industry: a solid, generics-led manufacturing base that keeps the national medicine cabinet stocked, paired with two more ambitious bets — halal pharmaceuticals and vaccine production — that the country hopes will carry it up the value chain.

This guide explains what the industry actually does, how big it is, where its strengths and limits lie, who the main players are, and how it is regulated.

## What the industry is — and is not

It helps to split medicines into two broad groups. **Generics** are off-patent copies of established drugs: cheaper, high-volume, and the workhorses of any public health system. **Innovative or patented medicines** are the newest branded drugs and complex biologics, protected by patents and sold at a premium. Malaysia's manufacturing strength sits firmly in the first group.

Local firms are strong in generic tablets, capsules, syrups, creams and injectables, plus health supplements. What they largely do not do is discover new molecules or manufacture most cutting-edge patented drugs at scale — those are still imported. So Malaysia is a capable producer of everyday medicines but, taken as a whole, remains a **net importer** of pharmaceuticals, because the high-value patented and specialty drugs it buys from abroad outweigh what it exports.

## How big is it?

The clearest number is about supply, not sales: industry bodies say local manufacturers meet a large share of domestic *generic* demand. The Malaysian Organisation of Pharmaceutical Industries (MOPI) states that around **80% of generic medicines are produced locally** and that its members supply roughly **half of all the medicines** Malaysians consume; other overviews put local self-sufficiency in generics at **70-80%** [verify]. That self-sufficiency is a genuine strategic asset — it was visibly tested, and largely held, during pandemic-era supply shocks.

On trade, media reporting of MATRADE figures puts **pharmaceutical exports at about RM3.04 billion in 2024** (up around 4%), within a combined medical-device-and-pharmaceutical trade of roughly **RM61.31 billion** — though medical devices, not medicines, make up the bulk of that larger figure [verify]. The domestic market itself is reported as expected to reach about **US$3.5 billion by 2028** [verify]. These numbers come from secondary reporting and should be confirmed against MATRADE, the Department of Statistics Malaysia (DOSM) and MIDA before being quoted as official.

A structural caveat sits behind all of this: Malaysia imports far more pharmaceutical value than it exports — one second-hand figure suggests imports ran several times the value of exports in recent years [verify] — precisely because patented and specialty drugs are bought in, not made locally.

## Generics and halal pharmaceuticals — the twin strengths

Generics are the foundation. A deep base of manufacturers — MOPI represents around 60 NPRA-licensed companies, and broader counts of licensed manufacturers run higher [verify] — turns out the antibiotics, analgesics, cardiovascular drugs and supplements that fill public hospital and community-pharmacy shelves, often at a fraction of the branded price.

Layered on top is Malaysia's most distinctive play: **halal pharmaceuticals**. Building on its position as a global halal-certification authority, Malaysia developed **MS 2424**, described as the world's first standard for the manufacture of halal pharmaceutical products (introduced in 2012 and later revised to add guidance on halal vaccines) [verify]. The logic is simple and commercial: for consumers in Malaysia and across Muslim-majority export markets, a medicine certified free of non-halal ingredients and cross-contamination is a differentiator that few other manufacturing nations can match. This ties the pharmaceutical sector directly to the wider [halal economy](/en/industries/halal-industry-economy/).

## The key players

A handful of names dominate the local industry:

- **Pharmaniaga** — the largest integrated player, spanning manufacturing, logistics and distribution, and historically the concession-holder supplying medicines to government hospitals and clinics. It also moved into vaccine fill-finish.
- **Duopharma Biotech** — listed on Bursa Malaysia and grown out of the Chemical Company of Malaysia (CCM) group; notable as an early Malaysian producer of a locally made biosimilar (erythropoietin).
- **CCM (Chemical Company of Malaysia)** — the long-established chemicals-and-pharmaceuticals group from which Duopharma emerged.
- **Kotra Pharma** — maker of generics, nutraceuticals and supplements (its Appeton and Axcel brands) for domestic and export markets.
- **Hovid** — an Ipoh-based manufacturer of ethical drugs, supplements and herbal and enhanced-absorption formulations.

Multinationals including Pfizer, Novartis and GSK are active in Malaysia too, but focus on marketing innovative and patented medicines rather than large-scale local manufacturing.

## Biologics and vaccines — the higher-value frontier

If generics are the base, **biologics and vaccines** are where Malaysia is trying to climb. Biologics — drugs made in living cells, including biosimilars (off-patent copies of biologic drugs) — command higher margins and demand far more technical capability than conventional pills.

Two developments stand out. First, **Duopharma Biotech** produced one of the country's first locally made biosimilars, signalling that Malaysian firms can operate at the biologic end of the business. Second, **Pharmaniaga** built **fill-and-finish** capacity — the final, sterile step of putting a vaccine into vials or pre-filled syringes — and used it during the COVID-19 pandemic to bottle Sinovac's vaccine domestically. The company has since invested in pre-filled-syringe lines and positioned itself around the goal of halal vaccine manufacturing. MIDA actively promotes Malaysia as a destination for vaccine manufacturing investment. Fill-finish is still a step short of manufacturing a vaccine's active substance from scratch, but it is a meaningful move up the value chain and a foundation for more.

## The regulator and the incentives

Every medicine sold in Malaysia must be **registered** before it reaches the market. That system is run by the **National Pharmaceutical Regulatory Agency (NPRA)**, under the Ministry of Health, which supports the **Drug Control Authority (DCA)**. Registration applications are assessed for quality, safety and efficacy and submitted electronically through NPRA's Quest3+ system, using the ASEAN Common Technical Dossier format.

Manufacturers must hold **Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP)** certification, and here Malaysia's credentials are strong: NPRA is a member of the **Pharmaceutical Inspection Co-operation Scheme (PIC/S)**, so local plants work to PIC/S GMP and NPRA can recognise GMP certificates from other member regulators — a quiet but important enabler of exports. The legal backbone is a cluster of statutes, chiefly the **Sale of Drugs Act 1952**, the **Control of Drugs and Cosmetics Regulations 1984**, and the **Poisons Act 1952**, which classifies controlled substances and so determines whether a product is a prescription-only medicine or an over-the-counter one.

On the investment side, pharmaceuticals is a promoted activity. Companies can seek incentives through the [Malaysian Investment Development Authority (MIDA)](/en/government/mida/), including Pioneer Status and Investment Tax Allowance, and a dedicated concessional tax rate for pharmaceutical (and vaccine) manufacturers was offered for a defined application window [verify]. The sector also features in national industrial planning as a target for higher-value investment.

## Outlook

Malaysia enters the next phase from a position of genuine strength in generics and a credible, if still emerging, story in halal pharmaceuticals and vaccines. The strategic task is clear: convert a reliable, cost-competitive generics base into something higher-value — more biosimilars, real vaccine manufacturing rather than only fill-finish, and exports that lean on the halal advantage — while reducing the heavy dependence on imported patented drugs. Whether Malaysia can fund the capital-intensive leap into biologics, against strong regional competition, is the central question for the industry's next decade.
