If you are reading this on a phone or laptop, there is a real chance one of its chips spent part of its life in Malaysia — not being invented or printed, but being turned from a bare slice of silicon into a finished, packaged component ready to solder onto a circuit board. That quiet, unglamorous step is where Malaysia has built one of the most consequential positions in the entire electronics world.

Malaysia is often described as a "chip nation," which is both true and slightly misleading. The country is a genuine heavyweight in semiconductors — but overwhelmingly in the **back end** of the industry, the assembly, testing and packaging (ATP) of chips whose silicon wafers are fabricated elsewhere. Understanding that distinction is the key to understanding both Malaysia's strength and its ambition.

This guide explains what Malaysia actually does in the chip supply chain, where it does it, how big it is, and what the National Semiconductor Strategy is trying to change.

## What does Malaysia actually do in the chip supply chain?

A finished chip passes through roughly three stages: **design** (laying out the circuit), **fabrication** (printing it onto silicon wafers in a "fab"), and the **back end** (cutting the wafers into individual dies, then assembling, testing and packaging them into the components you can actually use). The front end — design and fabrication — is the most capital-intensive and highest-margin. The back end is lower-margin but essential, high-volume, and skill-intensive.

Malaysia's historic specialisation is the back end. According to the Malaysian Investment Development Authority (MIDA), the country handles an estimated **13% of the world's back-end semiconductor assembly, testing and packaging**, while accounting for about **7% of global semiconductor trade** by value. In other words, Malaysia is far more central to *packaging* the world's chips than to *making the wafers* — a position it has held and deepened for half a century.

That back-end base is complemented by contract manufacturing and a deep local supplier ecosystem in precision engineering, automation and materials, much of it clustered in the north of the peninsula.

## How big is it?

Semiconductors are the single most valuable export category Malaysia produces. In 2024, exports of semiconductor devices — integrated circuits, transistors and valves — were worth about **RM388 billion**, according to MATRADE's trade statistics, making up the majority of the country's roughly RM601 billion in electrical & electronics (E&E) exports.

A note on figures: different sources quote different semiconductor export numbers because they draw the boundary differently — some count only semiconductor devices, others include a wider basket of "semiconductor products." Media reports of around RM437.5 billion reflect a broader definition. Either way, the scale is enormous: semiconductors alone rival the entire export economies of some neighbouring countries.

One caveat worth keeping in mind: because Malaysia imports wafers, packages them and re-exports the finished chips, gross export values include substantial imported content and therefore overstate the domestic value actually added. The *value* Malaysia captures is in the back-end work, not the full sticker price of the chip.

## Where are Malaysia's semiconductors made?

The industry has a clear birthplace and a widening map.

- **Penang (Bayan Lepas and Batu Kawan):** The heartland. Intel opened its first offshore assembly plant in Penang in 1972, and the cluster that grew around it — AMD, Broadcom, Bosch, OSRAM and a large contract-manufacturing base — still anchors the national industry.
- **Kulim, Kedah:** The Kulim Hi-Tech Park extended the northern corridor with advanced electronics manufacturing, including genuine LED wafer fabrication.
- **Klang Valley, Melaka and Negeri Sembilan:** A central cluster closer to Kuala Lumpur, home to names such as Renesas and ON Semiconductor.
- **Johor:** Increasingly important, as spillover from neighbouring Singapore drives new investment in the south.

This concentration — Penang plus Kulim especially — is why the northern corridor is sometimes nicknamed Malaysia's "Silicon Valley," though the label flatters the comparison: Malaysia's strength has been manufacturing and back-end services rather than chip design. For the broader picture, see the guide to the [Electrical & Electronics (E&E) industry](/en/industries/electrical-electronics-industry/), of which semiconductors are the largest part, and the state where it began, [Penang](/en/states/penang/).

## The back-end strength — and its ceiling

Being the world's packaging workshop has been a remarkable success, but it has a built-in limit. Assembly, testing and packaging is essential and high-volume, yet it sits in the middle of the value chain, earning thinner margins than chip design and wafer fabrication, where the largest profits concentrate.

That creates a strategic problem familiar to Malaysian policymakers: the country is deeply embedded in the global chip supply chain, but largely at its lower-margin end, and exposed to the famously cyclical swings of semiconductor demand. Moving "up the stack" has been a stated goal for years.

## The National Semiconductor Strategy

The government's formal answer is the **National Semiconductor Strategy (NSS)**, launched in 2024. Its aim is to move Malaysia beyond back-end work into higher-value activities: integrated-circuit **design**, **advanced packaging**, semiconductor **manufacturing equipment**, and **research and development**.

The strategy's headline ambition, as reported from government and industry briefings, is to roughly **double Malaysia's share of the global semiconductor market** — from about 7% toward around 14% by the end of the decade — backed by large investment and workforce-training targets. (The precise figures vary between sources and should be confirmed against the official MITI/MIDA strategy document; see the verification note on this page.)

Whether every target is met, the direction is clear: Malaysia wants to stop being only the place chips are tested and packaged and become a place where they are also designed and fabricated. Momentum has been supportive — E&E and semiconductor exports set records in 2024 and continued climbing into 2026.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Malaysia designs the chips in your phone."** Mostly not — the historic role is back-end assembly, testing and packaging; design is the goal of the NSS, not yet the established strength.
- **"Malaysia has lots of wafer fabs."** Far fewer than its back-end footprint suggests; large-scale front-end fabrication is an ambition the NSS is trying to grow.
- **"Semiconductors and E&E are the same thing."** Semiconductors are the biggest slice of E&E, but E&E also includes consumer electronics, industrial electronics and electrical products.
- **"The export number is pure Malaysian value."** Gross semiconductor exports include imported wafer content; the value Malaysia captures is in the back-end work.

## What's next

Malaysia's semiconductor industry is at a genuine inflection point. Half a century after Intel's first Penang plant, the country has turned back-end chip services into a record-setting export engine — and has openly concluded that staying at the lower-margin end is not enough for the decades ahead. Whether the National Semiconductor Strategy succeeds in pulling Malaysia into design and fabrication, against intense regional competition and a volatile global chip cycle, is the central question for the industry's next chapter.
