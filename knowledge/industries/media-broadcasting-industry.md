When Malaysians talk about "the media," they usually mean one of a few familiar names: Astro's satellite dish bolted to the side of the house, the TV3 news bulletin, a morning radio show on the drive to work, or — increasingly — whatever is playing on a phone during the commute. Those everyday touchpoints belong to a single, sprawling industry: media and broadcasting. It is one of the more visible service sectors in the country, and also one of the most disrupted.

This guide looks at the sector as a whole — television, radio, publishing, advertising and the creative-content businesses that supply them. For cinema and feature films specifically, see the separate guide to the [Malaysian film industry](/en/arts-culture/malaysian-film-industry/); this article covers the broader broadcasting and advertising landscape around it.

## What the sector covers

Media and broadcasting is really a bundle of related businesses:

- **Broadcast television** — free-to-air (FTA) channels funded by advertising, and subscription pay-TV funded by monthly fees.
- **Radio** — a crowded field of commercial and public stations, still one of the most widely consumed media in the country.
- **Publishing** — newspapers and magazines, now overwhelmingly digital, plus their online news portals.
- **Advertising** — the agencies and ad spend ("adex") that pay for most commercial media.
- **Creative content** — the production of programmes, animation, music and games, increasingly aimed at export markets.

What ties them together is a shared economic engine — advertising and subscriptions — and a shared regulator. And it is that economic engine that has been transformed over the past decade.

## How big is it, and where is the money going?

The clearest window into the sector's health is advertising spend. Malaysia's total ad market was reported at around **RM9.0 billion (roughly US$1.9 billion) in 2024**, up about 8% on the previous year, according to MAGNA (IPG Mediabrands) data cited in the trade press. The headline figure is healthy — but the composition has changed beyond recognition.

**Digital now dominates.** Digital channels took roughly **three-quarters of all ad spend in 2024** — up from under a third a decade earlier — led by social media and search. Television's share has fallen to single digits, and print to around 6%. Forecasters expect digital to climb toward 85% of the market by the end of the decade. (These are industry estimates on differing definitions; see the verification note on this page.)

That single trend explains most of the pressure on traditional broadcasters and publishers: the audiences are still large, but the advertising money that once followed them has migrated to global platforms. The wider context is Malaysia's broader [digital economy](/en/industries/digital-economy-overview/), of which digital advertising and content are a growing slice.

Beyond advertising, the government has also begun measuring the **creative-content** economy directly: the digital creative industry was reported to have generated about **RM5.3 billion in revenue, with roughly RM800 million in exports, in 2023**, according to figures attributed to the Digital Ministry.

## The players: broadcast, pay-TV, radio and publishing

Three large organisations anchor Malaysian broadcasting:

- **Astro** is the dominant **satellite pay-TV** operator, launched in 1996. It bundles dozens of channels — local and international, sport, movies and vernacular-language programming — and has moved aggressively into broadband and streaming. Its traditional subscription model, however, is under pressure as households "cut the cord."
- **Media Prima** is the leading **free-to-air television** group, home to TV3 — long the country's most-watched commercial channel — along with other FTA channels, a major radio network, and newspaper and digital-publishing arms. It is the closest thing Malaysia has to a diversified, advertising-funded media conglomerate.
- **Radio Televisyen Malaysia (RTM)** is the **public broadcaster**, running the TV1, TV2 and TV Okey television channels and a large family of national and regional radio stations. As a government broadcaster it carries a public-service and national-language mandate rather than a purely commercial one.

**Radio** remains surprisingly resilient. A dense market of commercial stations — spanning Malay, English, Chinese and Tamil audiences — competes with RTM's public networks, and radio continues to reach large daily audiences even as its share of ad budgets shrinks. **Publishing**, by contrast, has been hit hardest: the major newspaper groups have shifted their centre of gravity online, and print circulation and advertising have fallen sharply.

## Streaming: the disruption from above

The defining change in the sector is the rise of **streaming (OTT, "over-the-top") video**. Global services — **Netflix**, **Disney+ Hotstar**, **Viu** and **iQIYI** — compete directly for the viewing time that once belonged to pay-TV and free-to-air. Disney+ Hotstar's arrival in particular reshaped the market, and telcos such as CelcomDigi now bundle streaming subscriptions with mobile data.

Rather than resist, incumbents have tried to adapt. Astro has positioned itself partly as an **aggregator**, bundling third-party apps (Netflix, Disney+ Hotstar, iQIYI and others) alongside its own channels, and has built up its home-grown streaming service, **sooka**. The strategic question for the whole sector is whether the established broadcasters can capture enough of the streaming economy to offset the slow decline of their legacy subscription and advertising revenues.

## Content and animation: Malaysia's export story

Behind the broadcasters sits a **creative-content** industry that produces the programmes, music and animation they carry — and which, increasingly, sells abroad.

Malaysian **animation** is the standout success. The children's series **Upin & Ipin**, produced by **Les' Copaque Production**, grew from a short film into an internationally recognised franchise with a large regional following (notably in Indonesia) and a substantial online audience, and has expanded into games and licensed merchandise. It has become the government's favourite example of home-grown intellectual property (IP) with export potential.

To nurture more of this, policymakers lean on dedicated funding and agencies. **MyCreative Ventures** channels grants and matching funds into music, film and other creative businesses, and the Communications Ministry has topped up a **Creative Content Fund** for film and music and floated the idea of a dedicated animation division within FINAS. The ambition is consistent: turn Malaysian creative output into exportable IP rather than one-off productions.

## Regulation: the CMA 1998, MCMC and FINAS

Media and broadcasting in Malaysia sit under a converged regulatory framework built around the **Communications and Multimedia Act 1998 (CMA)**. The Act brought telecommunications, broadcasting and online content under one roof, administered by the **[Malaysian Communications and Multimedia Commission (MCMC)](/en/government/mcmc-communications-multimedia-commission/)**.

In practice this means:

- **Licensing.** Private television and radio operators hold a **Content Application Service Provider (CASP)** licence from MCMC and operate within its guidelines; the regulator has been developing a broader National Broadcasting Policy to govern public, private and international broadcasters.
- **Content standards.** An industry **Content Code**, administered through a designated Content Forum, sets standards for broadcast, radio and online content, with a complaints mechanism and the power to impose penalties.
- **Film and screen content.** The **National Film Development Corporation (FINAS)**, under the Communications Ministry, oversees the film and screen-content side — licensing, incentives and schemes such as the Compulsory Screening Scheme for local films in cinemas.

Content regulation is a live and sometimes contested area — periodic debates over local-content requirements, licensing enforcement and online content show that the rules are still evolving alongside the technology.

## Outlook

Malaysia's media and broadcasting industry is caught between a large, loyal domestic audience and a business model that is being rebuilt in real time. The near-term story is one of adjustment: traditional broadcasters defending their revenues as advertising and viewing migrate to digital platforms they do not own. The more hopeful story is on the **content** side, where Malaysian animation and creative IP have shown they can travel beyond the domestic market.

Whether the sector thrives will depend on how successfully its established players convert legacy audiences into digital subscribers and advertisers — and on whether the creative-content push produces more exportable hits like Upin & Ipin.
