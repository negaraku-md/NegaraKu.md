Malaysia's modern economy was, quite literally, dug out of the ground. Long before palm oil, electronics or semiconductors, it was **tin** that made the peninsula matter to the world — financing railways, growing towns such as Ipoh and Kuala Lumpur, and drawing in generations of migrant miners. Mining is the country's oldest industrial story, and although it no longer dominates the economy, it is quietly being rewritten around a very modern prize: rare earth elements.

This guide explains what Malaysia mines today, how the industry is owned and regulated, and why rare earths have become its most talked-about — and most politically sensitive — frontier.

## From tin capital to diversified sector

For much of the 20th century, Malaya and then Malaysia was the world's leading tin producer. Heritage and historical studies describe it as the premier global supplier until the late 1970s, with an estimated **40% of world tin output** at its peak and the Kinta Valley in Perak as the single largest mining district. (That share and period come from academic literature rather than a single official statistic and should be verified.)

The dominance did not last. Tin prices and controls grew unstable, and in **1985 the International Tin Council collapsed**, taking the price with it. Mines closed in waves, miners lost their jobs, and the dredges that had defined the Perak landscape fell silent. Malaysia still mines and smelts tin — the USGS estimates roughly **5,460 tonnes of mined tin (metal content) in 2024** and remains a significant smelter of imported concentrate — but at a fraction of its former scale, now ranked only around tenth worldwide for mined tin. The old mining pools now dot the landscape as lakes, some redeveloped into townships and tourism sites.

What replaced single-commodity dominance is a **diversified minerals sector**, with no one mineral carrying the industry the way tin once did.

## What Malaysia mines today

Beyond tin, Malaysia's commercial mineral output spans several commodities (2024 figures below are USGS estimates pending confirmation against JMG statistics):

- **Bauxite** — the ore of aluminium, concentrated overwhelmingly in **Pahang** around Kuantan. Output has been volatile: a mining boom in 2015–2016 caused serious pollution, and the government imposed a **moratorium on bauxite mining in Pahang in January 2016** that was repeatedly extended. USGS estimates around 387,000 tonnes were produced in 2024, well below the boom-year peaks.
- **Gold** — mined chiefly in **Pahang and Kelantan**, along the so-called "central gold belt," with an estimated 1,700 kg of gold content in 2024.
- **Iron ore** — the largest commodity by tonnage, estimated at about **5.25 million tonnes** in 2024.
- **Silica sand** — used in glass and increasingly eyed as a feedstock for solar-grade and semiconductor-grade silicon; named a strategic mineral but not separately quantified here.
- **Kaolin** ("china clay") — around 267,000 tonnes in 2024, used in ceramics, paper and coatings.

The government has formally designated **five strategic minerals** — tin ore, bauxite, silica sand, kaolin and non-radioactive rare earth elements — as priorities for domestic value-adding rather than raw export.

## Rare earth elements: the new frontier

Rare earth elements (REEs) — a group of 17 metals vital to magnets, electric-vehicle motors, wind turbines and defence electronics — have become the centrepiece of Malaysia's mineral ambitions. The appeal is strategic: global supply is dominated by China, and buyers are hunting for alternative sources.

Malaysia's own REE story has two separate strands that are easy to confuse.

**Domestic non-radioactive rare earths (NR-REE).** Malaysia has ionic-clay rare earth deposits, particularly in **Perak and Kedah**, marketed as "non-radioactive" to distinguish them from thorium-bearing ores. The government has pushed to develop this resource under standard operating procedures distributed to the states in December 2023, covering pre-mining, active mining and rehabilitation, and has floated in-situ leaching as a lower-impact extraction method. Production remains small but is rising.

**The Lynas plant.** Separately, the **Lynas Advanced Materials Plant in Gebeng, near Kuantan (Pahang)** — one of the largest rare-earth separation plants outside China — has operated since 2012. Crucially, it does **not** process Malaysian ore: its feedstock is concentrate shipped from the **Mt Weld mine in Australia**. Lynas has faced long-running local concern over low-level radioactive residue from its cracking-and-leaching process, which it disputes. Its presence makes Malaysia a major rare-earth *processor* even though domestic *mining* is still nascent.

To stop its own resource leaving the country unprocessed, the government introduced a **moratorium on the export of raw rare earths**, which the USGS dates to **1 January 2024** and which officials reaffirmed in 2025. The aim is to force refining and downstream manufacturing onshore. Because this policy area is politically charged and has shifted repeatedly, its exact scope — and whether it touches Lynas's imported-ore operations — should be checked against the latest government statement rather than taken as settled.

## Who owns the minerals — states versus federal

Malaysia's mining governance has an unusual split rooted in the **Federal Constitution**: **land and minerals belong to the individual states**, not the federal government. Each state therefore grants mining leases and licences through its **State Land and Mines Office** and its own mineral enactment (for example, Kelantan's Enactment of Minerals). A would-be miner negotiates land and lease terms with the state.

The federal layer sits on top for standards and supervision. The **Mineral Development Act 1994** is the main federal statute; it sets out how mining schemes are approved, operational and safety standards, and the offences and penalties for non-compliance. Enforcing it is the job of the **Minerals and Geoscience Department Malaysia (JMG)** — formed in 1999 by merging the old Geological Survey and Mines departments — which also maps the country's geology and keeps the national mineral statistics. JMG today sits under the **Ministry of Natural Resources and Environmental Sustainability (NRES)**.

This state-versus-federal division is central to understanding Malaysian mining: environmental controversies such as the Pahang bauxite episode play out between a state that issues leases and a federal government that sets rules and, at times, steps in with moratoriums.

## Policy and outlook

Mining is a small slice of GDP — the minerals-only sub-sector sits well under 1%, with a government target to lift the mining-and-quarrying contribution from around 0.7% to 1% by 2030 (headline "mining and quarrying" GDP figures of several per cent generally include oil and gas, so the definition matters). Yet preliminary government mapping has valued the nation's mineral resources at roughly **RM4.11 trillion**, and policy has turned toward extracting more value from them.

The direction is set by the **National Mineral Industry Transformation Plan 2021–2030** and the mineral sectoral plan under the **New Industrial Master Plan 2030** (launched September 2023), both of which emphasise moving up the value chain — processing, not just extracting — and doing so more sustainably. A successor to the long-standing National Mineral Policy has also been under discussion.

The through-line is clear. Having learned from tin — where Malaysia exported a raw commodity and then watched the market vanish — the country now wants its minerals, above all rare earths, to be refined and manufactured into higher-value products at home. Whether the rare earth bet pays off will depend on prices, environmental management and a policy environment that is still very much in flux.
