When people picture Malaysia's oil and gas wealth, they usually think of the crude and gas beneath the seabed off Terengganu, Sarawak and Sabah — the reserves that made PETRONAS one of Asia's largest energy companies. But hydrocarbons do not lift themselves out of the ground. Behind every producing field sits a small army of contractors: the yards that weld steel platforms, the rigs that drill the wells, the vessels that ferry crews and cargo offshore, the engineers who design the facilities, and the technicians who keep it all running. That ecosystem is the **oil & gas services and equipment (OGSE) industry**, and it is a major Malaysian industry in its own right.

This guide explains what OGSE is, how big it is, the segments and companies that make it up, the PETRONAS gateway that governs who gets work, where the activity clusters, and how the sector is pivoting as the world's energy mix changes.

## What OGSE is — and what it is not

The oil & gas value chain splits broadly into the **resource** and the **services** that support it. The resource side belongs to PETRONAS (the national oil company and resource custodian) and its production-sharing contractors, who hold the licences to explore, develop and produce petroleum and who book the revenue from selling it. OGSE firms do not own the oil or gas. Instead, they are hired to deliver the physical work and equipment that production requires, and they are paid in fees, day rates and contract values.

That distinction matters. The economics are different (service margins versus resource rents), the risks are different (contract cycles and oil-price swings versus reserves and reservoir risk), and the policy levers are different. This article focuses on the services-and-equipment ecosystem; for the resource, reserves and PETRONAS's role as operator, see the companion guide to the [oil & gas industry](/en/industries/oil-gas-industry/).

## How big is it?

The clearest official measure comes from the Department of Statistics Malaysia (DOSM), whose **OGSE Census** recorded **2,894 establishments** generating about **RM79.2 billion in gross output**, **RM39.4 billion in value added** and roughly **123,288 persons engaged** for reference year 2023. OGSE-specific output (as opposed to establishments' total output) was about RM59.3 billion. By establishment count the sector is dominated by services firms (around 57%), followed by construction, manufacturing, and mining and quarrying. *(These figures should be confirmed against the DOSM release; see the verification notes.)*

Company counts vary with the definition used. The Malaysia Petroleum Resources Corporation (MPRC), which tracks the sector through its annual **OGSE100** study, counted around **2,286 companies** for FY2022 with combined revenue of about **RM72.7 billion**. More recent ministerial statements have cited larger figures still, but these have not all been confirmed against a primary publication. Either way, OGSE is a multi-tens-of-billions-of-ringgit industry employing well over a hundred thousand people — a serious slice of the national economy, estimated by one 2025 industry account at roughly 5–8% of GDP.

## The main segments

OGSE is not one business but a cluster of specialised ones:

- **Fabrication and construction** — building the steel: offshore platforms, jackets, topsides, modules and onshore facilities. This is capital-heavy yard work, led by firms such as Malaysia Marine and Heavy Engineering (MMHE).
- **Drilling services** — operating drilling rigs (jack-ups and others) and providing well services. Velesto Energy is the best-known Malaysian rig operator.
- **Marine and offshore support vessels (OSVs)** — the workboats that supply platforms, handle anchors and support offshore operations, together with the floating production units (FPSOs/FSOs) that process and store hydrocarbons at sea.
- **Engineering, procurement and construction (EPC)** — designing and delivering whole facilities, often integrated with installation, hook-up and commissioning (HUC).
- **Maintenance, repair and overhaul (MRO)** — the recurring, lower-volatility work of keeping ageing assets running safely.
- **Decommissioning** — the newer, growing business of safely plugging wells and removing end-of-life platforms, now a dedicated working-group topic within the industry.

## Key players

Malaysia has produced several OGSE companies of genuine regional scale, most of them listed on Bursa Malaysia. In MPRC's OGSE100 rankings the top tier has typically featured **MISC** (shipping and floating production, part of the PETRONAS group), **Sapura Energy** and **Dialog Group** (integrated services), **Yinson** (floating production and, increasingly, renewables), **Bumi Armada** (floating production and offshore support), **MMHE** (fabrication) and **Velesto Energy** (drilling). Positions move year to year with contract wins and asset valuations — several asset-heavy players took heavy impairments during the oil-price downturn — so any ranking should be read against its financial year.

## The PETRONAS licensing gateway

For most OGSE firms, the single most important piece of bureaucracy is **PETRONAS's Licensing & Registration (L&R)** system, which effectively controls access to the bulk of Malaysian oil and gas spending. The logic is straightforward:

- A **licence** allows a vendor to supply both the **upstream** and **downstream** sectors — the production-sharing contractors, PETRONAS subsidiaries, offshore fabrication contractors and MISC.
- A **registration** allows supply to the **downstream** sector only — PETRONAS group companies such as PETRONAS Chemicals, PETRONAS Gas and PETRONAS Dagangan.

Every vendor must hold at least one **SWEC** (a standardised work and equipment category) code describing exactly what it is approved to supply, and certain strategic SWECs carry minimum technical requirements that must be met before applying. Vendors generally must be locally incorporated and meet minimum paid-up-capital thresholds *(reported as around RM100,000 for a licence and RM10,000 for registration — confirm against the current PETRONAS guidelines)*. For foreign firms, a local joint venture or branch and application through PETRONAS's online platform is the usual route. The administrator of resource policy more broadly is [PETRONAS](/en/companies/petronas/) itself.

## Where the activity clusters

OGSE work concentrates near the offshore basins and the ports that serve them:

- **Kemaman, Terengganu** — the Kemaman Supply Base is the largest petroleum supply base in Peninsular Malaysia, serving the Peninsular offshore fields, with fabrication yards nearby.
- **Pasir Gudang, Johor** — home to MMHE's deepwater fabrication yards, among the few in Southeast Asia able to build for international clients.
- **Labuan and the Asian Supply Base** — the logistics hub for offshore Sabah (and, with Bintulu, the Sarawak gas and LNG complex).
- **Lumut, Perak** — a marine, shipbuilding and heavy-engineering cluster on the west coast.

## The energy-transition pivot

The sector's biggest strategic challenge is that its core market — new oil and gas development — is maturing even as demand for lower-carbon energy rises. The government's response is the **National OGSE Industry Blueprint 2021–2030**, launched in April 2021 and reviewed mid-term in 2024. It organises the industry around competitiveness, resilience, sustainability and energy transition, and its stated 2030 ambitions include doubling export revenue, sustaining a roughly RM50 billion GDP contribution, and drawing a meaningful share of industry income from transition work such as carbon capture, utilisation and storage (CCUS), hydrogen and renewables. *(Specific targets should be confirmed against the blueprint and its mid-term review.)*

The pivot plays to real strengths: offshore engineering, marine operations and project management transfer directly to offshore wind, CCUS injection and decommissioning. Some Malaysian firms — Yinson's move into renewables is the clearest example — are already repositioning. The industry body, the Malaysian Oil & Gas Services Council (MOGSC), runs working groups on exactly these themes.

## Outlook

The near-term picture is cyclical but firmer than during the downturn, supported by renewed PETRONAS activity and a pipeline of maintenance, redevelopment and new-well work. The longer-term question is whether Malaysia's OGSE firms can convert decades of offshore expertise into durable positions in transition energy and export markets before domestic oil and gas work tapers. That is the explicit bet behind the blueprint — and the reason OGSE, long treated as a supporting act to the resource, is increasingly discussed as an industry with its own future to shape.
