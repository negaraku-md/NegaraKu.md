Plastics, fertilisers, detergents, synthetic rubber, cosmetics, paints and the resins inside your phone casing all start as chemicals — and a surprising share of them, or their building blocks, are made in Malaysia. Behind the country's better-known oil-and-gas and palm-oil stories sits a large, quietly strategic chemicals and petrochemicals industry that turns raw hydrocarbons and vegetable oils into the materials modern life depends on.

This guide explains what the sector is, how it is structured from upstream to downstream, who the major players are, where the big complexes sit, and how the government is trying to steer it toward higher value.

## What the sector is

"Chemicals and petrochemicals" is really two overlapping industries. **Petrochemicals** are derived from fossil feedstock — natural gas and crude-oil fractions such as naphtha — which are "cracked" into basic building blocks called olefins (chiefly ethylene and propylene) and then polymerised into plastics and resins. **Oleochemicals** are the natural-oil equivalent: fatty acids, fatty alcohols and glycerine made from oils and fats, which in Malaysia means mainly palm and palm-kernel oil. Around and above both sit **specialty and fine chemicals** — higher-value products such as care chemicals, electronic chemicals, agrochemicals and performance materials.

Malaysia's advantage is that it has the feedstock for all three: domestic natural gas and crude from PETRONAS's upstream operations, a world-scale refining base, and the world's second-largest palm-oil output. That combination is why the country has been able to build an integrated chemicals industry rather than just import-and-blend operations.

## How big it is

Pinning down the sector's exact size is harder than it sounds, because official statistics usually bundle chemicals with petroleum, rubber and plastics. The Department of Statistics Malaysia's Economic Census 2023 put the combined "petroleum, chemical, rubber and plastic products" manufacturing sub-sector at **RM579.6 billion in gross output in 2022**, up sharply from RM299.1 billion in 2015 — but that figure is far wider than chemicals alone, so it should be read as context rather than a chemicals-specific number (see the verification note on this page).

As a guide to relative weight, the government's own framing is clearer: the **Chemical Industry Roadmap 2030 (CIR2030)** aims to lift the chemical sector's gross value added to **more than 4.5% of GDP by 2030, up from about 3.4%**, an uplift the Minister of Investment, Trade and Industry estimated at roughly **RM40 billion**. Either way, chemicals and petrochemicals are a top-tier contributor to Malaysian manufacturing and exports, and a core part of the wider [manufacturing industry](/en/industries/manufacturing-industry-overview/).

## The value chain: upstream to downstream

The **upstream** end begins with feedstock. Natural gas and refinery streams feed steam crackers that produce ethylene, propylene and other olefins; these are the parents of almost everything downstream.

The **midstream and downstream** segments convert those olefins into polymers — polyethylene, polypropylene and other plastics — plus intermediates such as glycols, ammonia, urea (fertiliser) and methanol. This is where most of Malaysia's installed petrochemical capacity sits, and where PETRONAS Chemicals and Lotte Chemical Titan dominate.

Running in parallel is the **oleochemical** stream, which processes palm and palm-kernel oil into fatty acids, fatty alcohols, methyl esters and glycerine for soaps, surfactants, cosmetics, food additives and biodiesel. Malaysia has long been one of the world's major palm-based oleochemical producers, although current capacity rankings should be checked against recent data rather than older claims of outright global leadership.

The industry's strategic ambition, set out in CIR2030, is to shift weight toward the highest-value end — base chemicals and intermediates, plastics and polymers, and specialty chemicals such as care, nutrition, electronic and construction chemicals.

## Key players and GLCs

The anchor is **PETRONAS Chemicals Group (PCG)**, the listed petrochemical arm of national oil company [PETRONAS](/en/industries/petronas-industry-profile/). PCG is the dominant integrated producer of olefins, polymers and fertilisers, and its scale comes from privileged access to domestic gas feedstock.

**Lotte Chemical Titan**, majority-owned by South Korea's Lotte Chemical, is the other heavyweight. Established in 1991 as Malaysia's first standalone polyolefins producer, it has been described as the country's largest integrated producer of olefins and polyolefins, with capacity across two Johor sites (the precise figure varies between sources). A wide field of multinationals — in industrial gases, coatings, agrochemicals and specialty products — and a large base of palm-based oleochemical producers round out the sector.

## Where it is: the clusters

Malaysia's chemicals industry is organised around a handful of integrated complexes, each built next to feedstock and deep-water ports:

- **Pengerang, Johor** — the **Pengerang Integrated Complex (PIC)**, home to the **RAPID** (Refinery and Petrochemical Integrated Development) project. RAPID pairs a refinery designed to process about 300,000 barrels of crude a day with a steam cracker and downstream petrochemical units, targeting roughly 7.7 million tonnes a year of petrochemical and speciality output on a very large coastal site. It was built as a PETRONAS-Saudi Aramco joint venture; in 2026, Aramco agreed to transfer its stake in the Pengerang refining and petrochemical companies (PRefChem) back to PETRONAS, ending the 50:50 partnership struck in 2017.
- **Kerteh, Terengganu** — the Kerteh Integrated Petrochemical Complex, one of the country's original petrochemical hubs, tied to east-coast gas.
- **Gebeng, Pahang** — the Gebeng Industrial Park, part of the East Coast Economic Region, hosting petrochemicals and specialty chemicals.
- **Pasir Gudang, Johor** — an older industrial and petrochemical port zone.

These interconnected zones — Pengerang, Kerteh and Gebeng especially — give Malaysia a genuine cluster advantage: feedstock, infrastructure and downstream users in close proximity.

## Regulator and incentives

Investment and licensing run through the [Malaysian Investment Development Authority (MIDA)](/en/government/mida/), whose **Chemical and Advanced Material Division** evaluates manufacturing-licence applications and administers incentives such as Pioneer Status and the Investment Tax Allowance; petroleum and petrochemical activities also fall under the **Petroleum Development Act 1974**. MIDA has described Malaysia as hosting over 100 petrochemical companies making a diverse range of products.

The policy direction comes from two documents. The **New Industrial Master Plan 2030 (NIMP 2030)** is the national industrial-transformation blueprint, built on four missions (raising economic complexity, digitalisation, the net-zero push and economic security), with chemicals treated as a high-impact, high-growth segment addressed through a set of action plans. The **Chemical Industry Roadmap 2030** is its sector-specific companion, targeting the GDP-share and value uplift noted above and prioritising specialty chemicals.

## Outlook

The near-term picture is mixed. Global petrochemical margins have been squeezed by over-supply and volatile feedstock costs, pressuring commodity-polymer producers. Yet investment continues to flow in — for example Sarawak Petchem's RM7 billion methanol complex in Bintulu, which MIDA says makes Malaysia one of Asia-Pacific's leading methanol producers — and the strategic logic is intact: Malaysia has the feedstock, the complexes and the policy intent to move up from commodity chemicals toward specialty and advanced materials.

Whether CIR2030's targets are met will depend on execution — attracting specialty-chemical investment, decarbonising energy-intensive plants, and competing with larger regional rivals. The direction, though, is clear: Malaysia wants its chemicals industry to be known not only for bulk plastics and palm oleochemicals, but for the higher-value chemistry that sits above them.
