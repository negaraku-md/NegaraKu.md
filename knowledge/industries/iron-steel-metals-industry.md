Behind almost every building, bridge and highway in Malaysia is a quieter industry that makes the metal holding it all up. Iron and steel rarely make headlines the way semiconductors or palm oil do, yet they are foundational: the reinforcing bar inside a concrete column, the wire in a cable, the sheet in a car door. Malaysia makes a great deal of this itself — and, in a few metals, is genuinely world-class. But the steel half of the story is also one of the country's most troubled manufacturing sectors, defined by a stubborn mismatch between how much it can produce and how much the market actually needs.

This guide explains what the industry covers, how big it is, the crucial split between "long" and "flat" products, the non-ferrous metals that sit alongside steel, who the main players are, and why overcapacity and cheap imports have pushed the government into a moratorium and a wall of anti-dumping duties.

## What the industry covers

"Iron, steel and metals" is really two stories. The larger and more problematic one is **ferrous** — iron and steel. The smaller but healthier one is **non-ferrous** — chiefly aluminium and tin, plus smaller volumes of other metals.

Steelmaking itself splits by route and by product. Some Malaysian mills run **blast furnaces** that turn imported iron ore into new ("primary") steel; many others run **electric arc furnaces** that melt scrap. The output then divides into two families that behave almost like different industries, which is the single most important distinction to grasp.

## Long products versus flat products

**Long products** — reinforcing bar (rebar), wire rod and structural sections — are the backbone of construction. This is where Malaysia is strongest: the bulk of national finished-steel output is long products, by some secondary estimates around 83% (a figure worth verifying). Because they are tied to building activity, long products rise and fall with the construction cycle.

**Flat products** — hot- and cold-rolled sheet, plate and coil — feed cars, appliances, pipes, packaging and electronics. Malaysia's flat-products base is thinner, with fewer producers, so the country still imports a meaningful share of the flat steel and specialist grades (heavy plate, certain sections) it consumes. That import dependence is exactly where cheap foreign steel bites hardest, and it shapes the anti-dumping story below.

## How big is it, and what's wrong

By tonnage, Malaysia is a mid-sized steel producer whose output has grown quickly. Secondary analysis of World Steel Association data suggests crude steel production reached roughly **8.8 million tonnes in 2024**, up from about 7.5 million tonnes in 2023 — a steep climb over the past decade (these figures are from trade analysis and should be confirmed against primary data).

The problem is not output but **utilisation**. The Malaysian Iron and Steel Industry Federation (MISIF) has pointed to average capacity utilisation of only about **39% in 2023** — meaning mills, on average, ran at well under half their potential, far below healthy global levels. When too many plants chase too little demand, prices fall, margins vanish and weaker producers bleed cash. That single statistic explains most of the policy decisions that follow.

## The key players

Malaysia's established steelmakers are mostly long-products producers:

- **Ann Joo** — a long-time integrated producer with upstream (blast furnace) and rolling operations; it has been restructuring and seeking to divest its upstream steel unit.
- **Southern Steel** — a major long-products maker, part-owned by interests linked to the Hong Leong group and the Esteel/Green Esteel camp.
- **Lion Industries** (Lion Group) and **Malaysia Steel Works / Masteel** — two more of the traditional long-steel names.
- **Eastern Steel** — operates an integrated plant in Kemaman, Terengganu.
- **Alliance Steel** — a large **Chinese-invested** integrated mill in Kuantan, Pahang, built as part of the Malaysia–China Kuantan Industrial Park, with blast-furnace capacity far above the older local mills. Its arrival sharpened the overcapacity debate, and local producers have at times accused it of selling into segments beyond its licensed scope.

In recent years these companies have explored mergers and asset sales as they try to consolidate and compete with newer, larger, cheaper capacity. Treat the exact line-up and ownership as a moving target.

## Overcapacity and dumping

Malaysia's steel difficulties are a local version of a global one. Worldwide, there is far more steel capacity than demand, much of it in China, and surplus steel is exported at low prices. For a country that both over-built its own capacity and imports flat products, that is a double squeeze.

The main defensive tool is **anti-dumping duties** — extra import taxes imposed after investigators find that foreign steel is being sold below fair value and injuring local producers. Malaysia has used them repeatedly on flat steel: in 2025, for example, it set definitive duties on certain **flat-rolled iron and non-alloy steel products** from China, India, Japan and South Korea, and in the same period extended existing duties on **cold-rolled coil** from China and Japan after a sunset review. Rates and the list of covered products and countries change with each case, so current measures should always be checked against MITI and the official gazette.

## Links to construction and automotive

The industry's fortunes are tied to its customers. **Construction** is by far the biggest, which is why long-products demand tracks building activity, infrastructure spending and the property cycle; see the [construction industry](/en/industries/construction-industry/) guide for that demand side. A newer driver is the wave of **data-centre and semiconductor-plant construction**, which MIDA has flagged as a fresh source of steel demand. **Automotive** is the classic flat-steel customer — car bodies and components need quality sheet — linking steel to the [automotive industry](/en/industries/automotive-industry/) and the national marques.

## Non-ferrous: the bright spot

Away from steel, Malaysia is far more competitive. In **aluminium**, Sarawak-based **Press Metal** runs large smelters at Samalaju and Mukah powered by cheap Sarawak hydroelectricity, with smelting capacity of roughly 1.08 million tonnes a year, making it Southeast Asia's largest integrated aluminium producer. In **tin**, a legacy of the country's historic tin-mining boom, **Malaysia Smelting Corporation (MSC)** is one of the world's leading refined-tin producers, smelting at its Pulau Indah plant in Port Klang. These non-ferrous businesses are export-oriented and, unlike much of steel, globally cost-competitive.

## Policy and outlook

The government's response to steel overcapacity has two prongs. First, a **moratorium**: from August 2023, MITI froze new and expanded iron and steel manufacturing licences, aiming to let existing mills lift utilisation toward about 80% before allowing fresh capacity; the freeze was **extended in February 2025**, covering the upstream long- and flat-steel segments and limiting large expansions. Second, a push on **governance and decarbonisation** — tighter licensing oversight and the prospect of carbon pricing for emissions-intensive mills, in line with the New Industrial Master Plan 2030. The [Malaysian Investment Development Authority (MIDA)](/en/government/mida/) handles investment policy, while licensing and the moratorium sit with [MITI](/en/government/miti/).

The outlook is mixed. Steel's near-term fate depends on construction demand (including the data-centre boom), on how firmly imports are kept in check, and on whether consolidation finally rationalises surplus capacity. Decarbonisation and a possible carbon tax add both cost and opportunity. Non-ferrous metals, by contrast, look structurally healthier. The through-line is clear: Malaysia can make the metal the region needs, but its steel sector must first solve a problem of its own making — too much capacity for too little demand.
