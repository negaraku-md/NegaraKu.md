Almost everyone in Malaysia touches this industry several times a day — a cup of Milo at breakfast, a packet of instant noodles at lunch, a bottle of mineral water in the afternoon, a bar of chocolate made from cocoa ground in Johor. Food & beverage (F&B) manufacturing is one of the country's quietest big industries: less glamorous than semiconductors, but deeply woven into daily life, national food security and a growing export story built around one powerful idea — halal.

This guide explains what the industry makes, how large it is, its main segments, why halal certification is its competitive edge, who the major players are, and how it is regulated and supported.

## What is the food & beverage manufacturing industry?

F&B manufacturing means turning raw agricultural and marine inputs — grains, cocoa, palm oil, sugar, fruit, fish and dairy — into processed, packaged products ready for shops, restaurants and export. It sits downstream of farming and fishing (see the [agriculture sector overview](/en/industries/agriculture-sector-overview/)) and upstream of retail and food service.

It is largely a **domestic-oriented** industry, meaning much of its output is consumed inside Malaysia, but a rising share is exported. That dual nature makes it strategically important: it underpins food security while also earning foreign exchange.

## How big is it?

Precise sub-sector numbers are surprisingly hard to pin down, so the figures below should be read as indicative and are flagged for verification.

Malaysia's total manufacturing sales came to roughly **RM1.9 trillion in 2024**, and the Department of Statistics Malaysia (DOSM) repeatedly named **food, beverages and tobacco** as one of the leading drivers of growth that year, with the sub-group posting strong double-digit monthly gains ([DOSM](https://www.dosm.gov.my/portal-main/release-content/monthly-manufacturing-statistics-malaysia-december-2024); [New Straits Times](https://www.nst.com.my/business/economy/2025/02/1171784/manufacturing-sales-hit-rm19-trillion-46pct-2024)). Secondary data puts food-products manufacturing sales at around RM292 billion in 2023, though that figure is from an aggregator rather than an official release and should be confirmed.

What is clear is the direction of travel: food processing has been one of the most reliable sources of manufacturing growth in recent years, powered by domestic demand, a recovering tourism and food-service trade, and expanding exports.

## The main segments

The industry is not one thing but several, each with its own economics:

- **Processed and packaged food** — snacks, instant noodles, biscuits, sauces, seasonings and ready meals. This is the most visible segment and home to many of Malaysia's best-known consumer brands.
- **Cocoa and confectionery** — one of Malaysia's most striking specialisms. Despite growing very little cocoa today, Malaysia is a major **cocoa grinder**, importing beans and processing them into cocoa butter, powder, liquor and industrial chocolate. Grinding reportedly reached about 90,849 tonnes in the second quarter of 2026 — roughly 40% of Asia's total — after around 370,000 tonnes for full-year 2023 ([Malay Mail](https://www.malaymail.com/news/money/2026/08/13/malaysias-cocoa-industry-sees-sweet-recovery-as-grinding-jumps-29pc/231224)). Guan Chong Berhad and Barry Callebaut both run large grinding operations in Johor.
- **Edible oils and fats** — closely tied to Malaysia's palm oil industry, producing cooking oils, specialty fats, margarine and food ingredients for both local use and export.
- **Beverages** — soft drinks, bottled and mineral water, coffee, tea and energy or herbal drinks. Brands here include F&N's 100Plus, Spritzer's bottled water and Power Root's herbal energy drinks.
- **Aquaculture and seafood processing** — turning Malaysia's marine and farmed catch into frozen, canned and value-added seafood products, with significant export potential.

## The halal advantage and export markets

If one thing sets Malaysian F&B apart internationally, it is **halal**. Malaysia's halal certification, issued by JAKIM, is widely regarded as a global gold standard and is recognised by importing authorities in many countries. That recognition turns "made in Malaysia" into a trust mark for Muslim consumers worldwide.

The scale is meaningful. Malaysia's total halal product exports reached about **RM61.79 billion in 2024**, up roughly 15% from RM53.72 billion the year before, according to the trade ministry ([The Star](https://www.thestar.com.my/business/business-news/2025/05/01/malaysias-halal-product-exports-up-15-to-rm6179bil-in-2024); [MIDA](https://www.mida.gov.my/mida-news/malaysia-records-rm45-04b-in-halal-product-exports-from-january-to-september-2024-miti/)). Processed food is a core part of that basket, and government has cited global-halal-market projections of around US$5 trillion by 2030 as the prize.

Export momentum has been strong in new regions. MATRADE has reported processed-food exports to Europe of about RM3.48 billion in 2024 — a roughly 70% jump — led by cocoa products, non-dairy creamer, confectionery and biscuits, and exports to the Middle East and North Africa of about RM2.32 billion, with the UAE the single largest market there ([MATRADE](https://www.matrade.gov.my/en/about-matrade/press-release/malaysian-f-b-innovation-to-take-the-spotlight-at-anuga-2025-in-germany)). Agencies such as the Halal Development Corporation (HDC) and MATRADE actively push Malaysian F&B at trade fairs like Gulfood and Anuga. For the wider picture, see the [halal industry and economy](/en/industries/halal-industry-economy/) guide and how [halal certification](/en/business/halal-certification-system/) works.

## Who are the key players?

The industry blends multinationals with strong home-grown brands:

- **Nestlé (Malaysia) Berhad** — the market heavyweight, making Milo, Nescafé, Maggi and KitKat locally, and one of the largest halal-certified food producers in the world.
- **Fraser & Neave Holdings (F&N)** — soft drinks (including 100Plus), dairy and the local Coca-Cola bottling heritage, with one of South-East Asia's larger beverage plants in Shah Alam.
- **Mamee-Double Decker** — instant noodles and snacks (Mamee Monster, Mister Potato).
- **Munchy's** — a leading biscuit and wafer maker.
- **Power Root** — herbal and energy drinks with a notable Middle East export presence.
- **Spritzer** — the country's best-known bottled and mineral water producer.
- **Guan Chong Berhad** — a globally significant cocoa grinder based in Johor.

## Regulation and incentives

Two separate regulatory tracks govern the industry. **Food safety** falls under the **Food Act 1983** (Act 281) and its subsidiary rules — the Food Regulations 1985 and the Food Hygiene Regulations 2009 — administered by the **Ministry of Health**, covering hygiene, labelling, additives and premises registration ([FAOLEX](https://www.ecolex.org/details/legislation/food-act-1983-act-281-lex-faoc027309/)). Separately, **halal integrity** is controlled by the **Department of Islamic Development Malaysia (JAKIM)**, which issues the official halal certificate; without it, a product cannot credibly claim to be halal.

On the investment side, the Malaysian Investment Development Authority (MIDA) offers manufacturing incentives — historically **Pioneer Status** (a partial income-tax exemption) and the **Investment Tax Allowance** — with specific provisions for **halal food** projects ([MIDA](https://www.mida.gov.my/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/Chapter-2-Incentives-for-New-Investments.pdf)). These are being updated under a New Investment Incentive Framework, so current rates should be checked before relying on specific percentages.

## Outlook

The direction for Malaysian F&B manufacturing is clear even where the numbers need confirming: a solid domestic base, a widening export footprint and a genuine global differentiator in halal. The main constraints are familiar — reliance on imported raw materials (cocoa beans, wheat, dairy and much feedstock), exposure to commodity-price swings, and a persistent bottleneck in the speed of halal certification that policymakers have openly acknowledged. If Malaysia can streamline certification, climb into higher-value and healthier product lines, and keep opening export markets, F&B manufacturing is well placed to remain one of the country's steadiest industrial success stories.
