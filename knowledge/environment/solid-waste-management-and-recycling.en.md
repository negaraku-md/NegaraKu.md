Throw away rubbish in Johor and it becomes a federal government matter; throw away the same rubbish in Penang and it is still a local council matter. That is the core oddity of how Malaysia manages its solid waste — one grand federal law, but one that only takes effect where states agree to adopt it.

## Who is responsible for your rubbish?

The backbone of solid waste management in Peninsular Malaysia is the **Solid Waste and Public Cleansing Management Act 2007**, better known as **Act 672**. This act empowers the federal government to take over solid waste management and public cleansing from local authorities, with the aim of standardising the law across the country.

Two bodies do the work on the ground. The **National Solid Waste Management Department (JPSPN)** formulates policy, while **SWCorp** — the Solid Waste and Public Cleansing Management Corporation — acts as the implementing and enforcement body. SWCorp manages various waste streams: household, commercial, industrial, institutional, construction and public.

Private concessionaires such as Alam Flora and SWM Environment, meanwhile, handle daily rubbish collection on the ground, under SWCorp's supervision.

## Why does the act not apply everywhere?

This is the part most often misunderstood. Although Act 672 is a federal law, it is **not automatic**. Every state must formally agree to adopt it before the federal government can take over waste management there.

As of the Minister of Housing and Local Government's report in March 2024, **seven states** had adopted Act 672, together with the Federal Territories of Kuala Lumpur and Putrajaya. Five states had not yet: **Kelantan, Terengganu, Perak, Selangor and Penang**. In these states, waste management remains under their respective local authorities.

(Note: the Minister did not list the seven states that had adopted the act in that report. It should be noted that "seven adopted" plus "five not yet" adds up to twelve — more than the 11 states in the Peninsula — so this count may also include the Federal Territories or needs to be re-verified against official sources.)

The situation is changing. By early 2025, the Cabinet had agreed in principle to accept **Selangor and Penang** as the latest states set to adopt Act 672, with detailed negotiations under way. Penang even appointed a firm to assess its waste system ahead of formal adoption.

It must be stressed: **Sabah and Sarawak are entirely outside the geographic scope** of Act 672. Both Borneo states retain their own legal frameworks for solid waste.

## What changes when a state adopts Act 672?

When a state adopts the act, waste collection and disposal become a federal responsibility through SWCorp and its appointed concessionaires. One of the most tangible changes for households is **waste separation at source** — the obligation to separate recyclables (such as plastic, paper and metal) from residual waste before collection.

In states that have adopted the act, separation at source is enforced, and SWCorp enforcers can issue compounds for non-compliance. In states that have not yet adopted the act, waste separation is usually encouraged but not enforced uniformly under the same federal framework.

## How much rubbish does Malaysia produce?

The figures are large and keep rising. Malaysia generates around **39,000 tonnes of solid waste every day** — equivalent to roughly 3,000 rubbish lorries.

The composition of household waste — as shared by the Minister of Housing and Local Government at the launch of the Circular Economy Master Plan and reported by The Edge Malaysia — shows why recycling is hard and why food waste has become a policy focus:

| Type of household waste | Percentage |
|---|---|
| Food waste | 44.5% |
| Plastic | 13.2% |
| Disposable diapers | 12.1% |
| Paper | 8.5% |

Food waste dominating almost half the stream means many recyclable materials are contaminated before they can be recovered — a key reason recycling rates struggle to climb.

## Is Malaysia meeting its recycling target?

Not yet. Under the 12th Malaysia Plan, the country targets a **40% recycling rate by 2025**. Progress is slow but real:

- **2023:** 35.38%
- **2024:** 37.9%

Malaysia is approaching the target, but still lags far behind developed nations — Germany records around 65% and South Korea 59%, according to a comparison released by MIDA.

## Why is Malaysia still too dependent on landfills?

Because they are cheap and easy. Malaysia operates **135 landfills**, but only **22** of them are properly managed sanitary landfills; the remaining **114** are open dumpsites. Landfilling remains the country's dominant disposal method.

It is this dependence that is driving policy change. To divert rubbish away from landfills, Malaysia is exploring waste-to-energy. At present the country operates one WTE plant in Port Dickson, and according to the Minister of Housing and Local Government (reported by The Edge Malaysia), Malaysia could potentially support up to **18 WTE plants** that are financially viable and environmentally sustainable — although those plants have not yet been built.

## Towards a circular economy

In 2024, at the Circular Economy Conference 2024, the Ministry of Housing and Local Government launched the **Circular Economy Master Plan for Solid Waste in Malaysia (2025–2035)** — a ten-year roadmap to shift Malaysia away from a "throw and forget" model to a system in which materials are recovered and reused. According to the Minister (reported by The Edge Malaysia), this circular economy approach also helps the country achieve its target of net-zero carbon emissions by 2050.

Investment momentum also supports this direction. As of June 2025, the integrated waste management and recycling sector had attracted investments totalling **RM22.7 billion**, according to MIDA.

## What's next

For households and businesses, the most practical step is to check whether your state has adopted Act 672. If it has, waste separation at source is a legal obligation, not a choice — separate plastic, paper and metal from food waste, and check your local concessionaire's recyclables collection schedule. If your state has not adopted the act, local councils still provide recycling programmes, though less uniformly.

Things to watch: the formal adoption by Selangor and Penang, which will significantly expand SWCorp's coverage; whether the 2025 recycling rate finally reaches the 40% target; and the implementation of the Circular Economy Master Plan 2025–2035, which will determine whether Malaysia can break its dependence on landfills.
