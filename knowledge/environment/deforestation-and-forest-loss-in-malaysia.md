Ask three credible sources how much of Malaysia is still forest and you can get three answers that are ten percentage points apart — all defensible, all measuring something slightly different. That gap is not a rounding error; it is the whole argument about Malaysian deforestation in miniature.

Malaysia remains one of the most forested large countries in Southeast Asia. But "forested" is doing a lot of quiet work in that sentence. Whether a logged production forest, a rubber estate or a fast-growing acacia plantation counts as "forest" changes the headline number, the trend line, and who gets to claim success.

## How much forest does Malaysia actually have?

The World Bank, drawing on Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) data, reported Malaysia's forest area at **57.72% of land area in 2023**. FAO's earlier Global Forest Resources Assessment 2015 was higher still, at **22.2 million hectares, or 67.6%** of the land surface. Industry figures sit in between: the Timber Trade Portal cites the Malaysian Timber Council's total forest figure of **18.27 million hectares (55.3%)** for 2017.

Independent, satellite-based work tells a tenser story. RimbaWatch's *State of the Malaysian Rainforest* took a 2017 baseline of **18,332,583 hectares (55.52%)** and projected that if all the areas earmarked for clearing are lost, natural forest cover would fall to **15,636,737 hectares — 47.35%** of the country.

| Source | Year | Reported forest cover | What it counts |
|---|---|---|---|
| FAO (GFRA 2015) | 2015 | 22.2 M ha (67.6%) | Broadest — includes planted and logged forest |
| World Bank (FAO basis) | 2023 | 57.72% | FAO forest definition |
| Malaysian Timber Council (via Timber Trade Portal) | 2017 | 18.27 M ha (55.3%) | Natural + planted production forest |
| RimbaWatch (satellite baseline) | 2017 | 18.33 M ha (55.52%) | Natural forest |
| RimbaWatch (projection if earmarked areas cleared) | future | 15.64 M ha (47.35%) | Natural forest |

The same country, in roughly the same era, is reported as both comfortably above 55% and — under the pessimistic projection — below the symbolic 50% mark.

## Why do the numbers disagree so much?

The disagreement is definitional, not dishonest. Official and FAO-aligned statistics generally treat **planted forest, rubber and logged production forest** as "forest." A hillside that has been selectively logged, or an estate of harvested timber trees, still shows up in the tally. Satellite-based natural-forest datasets strip those out and count only intact, closed-canopy natural forest.

This matters for a second reason: it separates two very different metrics.

- **Tree cover loss** counts *any* removal of canopy — including the routine harvesting of plantations, fire and storm damage. Some of it regrows.
- **Primary forest loss** counts only the clearing of mature natural forest that has been "undisturbed in recent history." It is the closest proxy for permanent, irreplaceable loss of biodiversity and stored carbon.

For context, the Global Forest Review estimates the humid tropics as a whole lost **87 million hectares of primary forest since 2001 — about 8.5%** of the 2001 extent. Against that backdrop, Malaysia's own primary-forest trajectory is the figure worth watching, not the flattering aggregate cover percentage.

## What is driving the forest loss?

The popular shorthand blames palm oil for everything. The recent data complicates that. In RimbaWatch's accounting of deforestation between **2017 and 2021 (349,244 hectares lost)**, the leading driver was **timber plantations at 41.6%**, with **oil palm second at 15.5%**.

In other words, industrial *tree crops* — fast-rotation timber species grown inside forest reserves — have overtaken palm as the single biggest recorded driver in that window. Looking forward, RimbaWatch found that timber plantations inside forest reserves accounted for **1,794,887 hectares — about 76%** of all future clearing threats it identified.

Malaysia has lost **nearly one-fifth of its primary forest since 2001**, and **close to one-third since the 1970s**, according to the Global Forest Review. The cumulative picture is one of decades of steady conversion, not a single recent shock.

## Is deforestation actually slowing down?

Here the news is genuinely better. The Global Forest Review reported that Malaysia's **primary forest loss fell 13% in 2024** compared with 2023, and that the country **dropped out of the global top ten** for tropical primary forest loss for the first time — attributed to government action and corporate no-deforestation commitments.

That improvement held into 2025, a year when global tropical primary forest loss fell **36% to 4.3 million hectares** after a record-breaking 2024. WRI noted that "Indonesia and Malaysia maintained relatively low rates of primary forest loss" even as the global figure stayed **46% higher than a decade earlier**. Malaysia is, for now, moving against the global trend in the right direction.

## What is still at stake?

The caveat is scale. As of the 2023 RimbaWatch assessment, roughly **2.3 million hectares (precisely 2,346,601 ha)** of forest were still earmarked for potential future deforestation — enough to pull national natural-forest cover down toward that 47% projection and undershoot Malaysia's long-standing pledge to keep **at least 50% forest cover**.

So the honest summary is a split screen. The annual *rate* of primary forest loss is improving and Malaysia is outperforming the tropics as a whole. But a large stock of reserve land remains formally designated for clearing, much of it for timber plantations, which means the recent gains are policy-dependent and reversible rather than locked in.

## What's next

- **Watch the definition before the number.** When you see a Malaysian forest-cover figure, check whether it counts planted and logged forest (FAO/official) or only natural forest (satellite). The two are not interchangeable.
- **Track primary forest loss, not cover percentage,** for year-to-year trend — it is the metric that captures permanent ecological loss.
- **Follow the reserve reclassifications.** The gap between "improving rate" and "risk ahead" turns on whether the ~2.3 million hectares of earmarked forest are cleared, replanted or protected.
- **Cross-check state-level data.** National averages hide sharp regional differences; Sarawak, Sabah and Pahang have carried much of the recent loss, and their state forestry decisions drive the national number.
