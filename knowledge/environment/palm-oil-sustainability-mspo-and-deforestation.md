Malaysia grows roughly a fifth of the world's palm oil, and the industry has long drawn criticism from European and NGO campaigners over its links to deforestation. In response, the country now runs a mandatory, state-backed certification scheme, and on 5 September 2025 the European Union acknowledged that scheme in a joint statement. This is how MSPO works, what it does and does not prove, and where the hard problems still sit.

## What is MSPO, and why is it mandatory?

MSPO — Malaysian Sustainable Palm Oil — is the national sustainability standard for Malaysia's oil palm industry. Unlike voluntary market labels, it is government-run, audited by independent third-party certification bodies, and it spans the entire value chain: smallholders, growers, mills, dealers and processors.

The scheme was introduced in the mid-2010s and made compulsory by policy in phases. Since 2020, oil palm estates of 40.47 hectares (100 acres) and above have been legally required to hold MSPO certification, and the Malaysian Palm Oil Board (MPOB) can withdraw the licence of operators who fail to comply. By February 2020, 4.42 million hectares — about 74.8% of Malaysia's planted area at the time — were already certified, along with 390 of 452 palm oil mills.

Enforcement has been extended toward the downstream end of the chain. Industry compliance guidance reports that MSPO certification under the updated MS2530:2022 standard is being tied to MPOB licence renewal, with licence-holders such as dealers unable to renew without a valid certificate. The specific circular reference and effective date for this downstream requirement should be confirmed against an official MPOB source (see verification notes). The broader direction is consistent with the established policy since 2020: certification functions as a licensing condition rather than a voluntary marketing claim.

## How does MSPO differ from RSPO?

The two schemes are often confused because both certify "sustainable palm oil," but they differ in ownership, scope and legal force.

| Feature | MSPO | RSPO |
|---|---|---|
| Owner | Malaysian government (national standard) | Roundtable on Sustainable Palm Oil (industry-led) |
| Nature | Mandatory in Malaysia | Voluntary |
| Founded / launched | Mid-2010s, mandatory from 2020 | 2004 |
| Geographic scope | Malaysia only | Global (producing and consuming countries) |
| Legal enforcement | Yes — tied to MPOB licensing | No legal enforcement power |
| Deforestation cut-off | 31 December 2019 | Varies by criteria; scheme-defined |

In practice, RSPO is a voluntary global scheme that growers join to reach premium buyers, while MSPO is the baseline every Malaysian grower must meet to operate legally. Because it is compulsory, MSPO reaches parts of the industry — especially small independent farmers — that voluntary schemes have historically struggled to cover.

## What did the EU actually acknowledge?

On 5 September 2025, the EU and Malaysia issued a joint statement in which the EU acknowledged MSPO certification as "a credible sustainability scheme with a high standard digital traceability system that can facilitate operators' compliance with EUDR when sourcing deforestation-free and legal agricommodities from Malaysia." The statement followed the visit of European Commissioner for Environment, Water Resilience and a Competitive Circular Economy, Jessika Roswall, to Malaysia on 5 August 2025.

This matters because the EUDR is one of the largest market-access hurdles facing palm oil. Adopted on 31 May 2023 and in force since 29 June 2023, the EUDR bars seven commodities — palm oil, cattle, cocoa, coffee, soy, timber and rubber — plus their derivatives from the EU market unless they are proven deforestation-free and legally produced. After postponements, the obligations apply from 30 December 2026 for large operators and 30 June 2027 for micro and small enterprises.

Two nuances are worth stressing:

- **Acknowledgement is not automatic compliance.** The EU recognised that MSPO's traceability and no-deforestation rules can *facilitate* operators' EUDR compliance, but the legal due-diligence duty still rests with the importer. A valid MSPO certificate is supporting evidence, not an automatic pass through customs.
- **MSPO's cut-off is earlier.** MSPO bars deforestation for certification after 31 December 2019, a year earlier than the EUDR's 31 December 2020 cut-off. On the deforestation date itself, the Malaysian standard is the earlier of the two.

Plantation and Commodities Minister Johari Abdul Ghani said the acknowledgement "demonstrates that Malaysia can meet global market expectations without leaving anyone behind."

## Where are the smallholder and traceability gaps?

The reference to "leaving anyone behind" points at the central issue: independent smallholders. They farm small plots, often lack formal documentation, and are the hardest link to certify and trace.

The numbers show both progress and a remaining gap. As of late 2024, 76.9% of the 210,891 licensed independent smallholders were MSPO-certified, with MPOB targeting more than 90% by the end of 2025. The government has stated that financial assistance is available to help smallholders obtain certification; the specific budget amounts previously cited here have been removed pending an authoritative source (see verification notes). The Minister has described "more than half a million smallholders" as part of the MSPO framework, a figure that appears to count a broader population than the 210,891 licensed independents and should be reconciled.

The open challenges are less about headline coverage and more about depth:

- **The last mile of certification.** Reaching the final tranche of scattered, undocumented smallholders is slower and costlier per hectare than certifying large estates.
- **Traceability granularity.** EUDR demands geolocation of the plot of production. Aggregating many small farms into an auditable, plot-level trail is a data challenge, even with MSPO's digital traceability system.
- **Cost and credibility abroad.** Some critics in consumer markets have argued that MSPO's criteria are less stringent than RSPO's. The EU acknowledgement addresses part of that criticism, but scrutiny continues — recognition can be revisited if traceability or enforcement falls short.

## What's next?

Three things are worth watching. First, whether Malaysia reaches and holds its 90%-plus smallholder certification target, since that figure is a common benchmark for inclusivity. Second, how the downstream licence-renewal requirement is enforced in practice — whether MPOB actually withholds licences from non-compliant dealers. Third, the EUDR go-live: with large-operator obligations landing on 30 December 2026, the coming period will test whether MSPO's traceability holds up when EU importers begin demanding plot-level, deforestation-free proof at scale.

For growers and exporters, the practical takeaway is that MSPO certification is now the baseline to operate in Malaysia and a significant factor in EU market access — but it needs to be paired with EUDR-grade traceability records to carry weight once the regulation applies.
