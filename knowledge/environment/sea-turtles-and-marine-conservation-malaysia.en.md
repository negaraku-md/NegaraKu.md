In the late 1960s, around 2,000 female leatherback turtles came ashore every year on a single stretch of beach at Rantau Abang, Terengganu, leaving nearly 10,000 nests of eggs in the sand. Today, there are none. That story of decline is one of the region's most striking wildlife conservation failures — and it has shaped how Malaysia protects sea turtles to this day.

## How many turtle species are there in Malaysia, and how are they doing?

Four species of sea turtle nest actively in Malaysian waters: the green turtle, the hawksbill turtle, the olive ridley turtle and the leatherback turtle. Their fortunes differ sharply.

The green turtle is the most abundant and the most stable. According to SWOT, around 15,000–20,000 nests are recorded each year in Sabah and 5,000–10,000 nests in Terengganu, with a population considered stable or potentially increasing. The hawksbill is a mixed picture — falling to fewer than 200 nests a year in Sabah but rising to around 1,000 nests in Melaka in 2022. The olive ridley is now extremely rare, with fewer than 5 nests a year.

The leatherback sits at the bleakest end of the spectrum: extinct.

| Species | Main nesting site | Estimated nests per year | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Green turtle | Sabah; Terengganu; Sarawak | 15,000–20,000 (Sabah); 5,000–10,000 (Terengganu); 3,000–5,000 (Sarawak) | Stable / potentially increasing |
| Hawksbill turtle | Sabah; Melaka | <200 (Sabah); ~1,000 (Melaka, 2022) | Declining in Sabah, increasing in Melaka |
| Olive ridley turtle | Terengganu | <5 | Extremely rare |
| Leatherback turtle | Rantau Abang, Terengganu (historical) | 0 | Considered extinct |

*Source: SWOT — The State of the World's Sea Turtles.*

## How could the Rantau Abang leatherback go extinct?

Its collapse happened within a single generation. According to SWOT, the Rantau Abang population fell from around 2,000 females (10,000 nests) in the late 1960s, to only 40 females a year by the late 1980s, to about 5 individuals in the early 2000s. By 2010, the population was considered functionally extinct.

Two pressures overlapped. The first was egg harvesting: by the late 1950s, almost all eggs were collected for sale and consumption. The hatchery programme that began in the 1960s protected only about 1% of eggs — far too few to recover the population. Worse still, high incubation temperatures produced almost entirely female hatchlings, so that by the late 1980s many hatchery eggs were infertile due to a shortage of males.

The second pressure was fisheries bycatch. Throughout the 1970s and 1980s, trawls, drift nets, gill nets and bottom longlines caught at least several hundred turtles a year. By the late 1980s, at least 8 percent of the 40 females nesting each year were found dead, washed ashore after being entangled in fishing gear.

Protective measures — a ban on commercial egg sales and 100% hatchery incubation in 1988, a ban on large-mesh gill nets in 1989, and offshore turtle sanctuaries around 1990–1991 — were eventually introduced. But they came two decades too late.

## Why is turtle protection fragmented across states?

This is the structural cause behind the problem. Under the Federal Constitution, turtles fall under state jurisdiction, not federal. Each state must enact its own legislation to protect turtles and their eggs — and according to the Turtle Conservation Society of Malaysia, only six states (Kedah, Kelantan, Terengganu, Johor, Melaka and Negeri Sembilan) have dedicated turtle legislation. The result is a patchwork of laws that is not uniform.

Terengganu was the pioneer. The Terengganu Turtle Enactment 1951 was one of the earliest legal protections. According to the Turtle Conservation Society of Malaysia, the 1987 amendment introduced a range of protections — including a ban on the sale of leatherback eggs and penalties. Yet, ironically, that protection arrived when the leatherback population was already all but gone.

| Level | Legal instrument | Coverage |
|---|---|---|
| Federal | Fisheries Act 1985 | Protects turtles in Malaysian waters; basis for marine parks & sanctuaries |
| Federal | Wildlife Conservation Act 2010 | Protects freshwater turtles/terrapins (not sea turtles directly) |
| State (Terengganu) | Terengganu Turtle Enactment 1951 (amended 1987; 2021) | Regulates egg collection & trade; the 1987 amendment banned the sale of leatherback eggs |
| State (Kedah, Kelantan, Johor, Melaka, N. Sembilan) | Respective turtle enactments | Regulate egg licensing, ownership & trade |
| State (Sabah, Sarawak) | Separate conservation measures | Protection under state wildlife frameworks (not among the six dedicated turtle enactments) |

*Source: Turtle Conservation Society of Malaysia; Frontiers in Marine Science.*

## Is it still legal to sell turtle eggs?

For a long time, the answer in Terengganu was "yes" — and that was the core of the controversy. A study in Frontiers in Marine Science estimated that 422,000 turtle eggs were sold in local markets in 2007 alone. According to a Department of Fisheries officer quoted in the study, only about 10% came from licensed beaches; the rest were smuggled from neighbouring countries. The Department of Fisheries' official target is to ensure that at least 80% of eggs laid are incubated each year.

The major change came with the Terengganu Turtle Enactment 1951 (2021 Amendment). According to Macaranga, the amendment banned trade in the eggs of all turtle species, with effect from 1 June 2022, with fines of up to RM250,000. The new model allows the state government to tender out nesting beaches for egg collection, but collectors must sell the eggs to the Department of Fisheries for incubation, not to the market. One important note: the enactment bans the trade, not the taking of eggs for personal use.

## How do marine parks protect turtle habitat?

If state enactments protect eggs on the beach, the federal marine park system protects the waters where turtles feed and swim. Peninsular and Federal Territory marine parks are gazetted under the Fisheries Act 1985 (amended 1993).

According to the Department of Fisheries Malaysia, 49 islands are gazetted as marine parks. Each park covers a protection zone of two nautical miles from the island's lowest low-water mark — with the exception of Pulau Kapas in Terengganu and Pulau Kuraman, Rusukan Besar and Rusukan Kecil in Labuan, which are protected at one nautical mile. Among the main clusters:

- **Johor:** 20 islands, including Pulau Aur, Pulau Rawa, Pulau Tinggi and Pulau Pemanggil
- **Terengganu:** four island groups — Redang, Perhentian, Kapas and Tenggol
- **Pahang:** the Pulau Tioman group (Tioman and eight other islands)
- **Kedah:** the Pulau Payar group (4 islands: Payar, Lembu, Kaca, Segantang)
- **Melaka:** three islands (Undan, Dodol, Nangka)

Within these zones, activities such as eating turtle eggs, disturbing nesting turtles, collecting hatchlings, buying turtle-based handicrafts, and littering on the beach or in the water are prohibited. According to the Department of Fisheries Malaysia, six turtle centres and one exhibition room also operate across the Peninsula.

## What comes next

The Rantau Abang story shows that the right law can arrive too late. For the three species that still nest, the stakes now are enforcement and consistency — closing the gap between enactments "on paper" and the reality on the beach, and standardising protection across state boundaries.

To go deeper, explore the full text of the Fisheries Act 1985 and its amendments, follow the implementation of Terengganu's egg trade ban after 2022, and refer to the latest nesting data from the Department of Fisheries and local conservation organisations. If you visit a marine park island, follow the park rules — they are the last line of defence for the habitat that remains.
