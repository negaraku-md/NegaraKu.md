Turn on the tap on a Monday morning and find it dry — not because the faucet is broken, but because a factory upstream has discharged foul-smelling waste into the tributary that supplies raw water to your town. This is a recurring reality in the Klang Valley, and it explains why "river quality" is not merely an ecological issue but a matter of water supply security for millions of homes.

Rivers supply the bulk of the country's raw water. When a river is polluted, treatment plants cannot always filter it out — sometimes the only safe option is to shut the plant down and stop the supply. This article explains how Malaysia measures river quality, why raw water sources become contaminated, and how river reserves try to prevent it.

## How does Malaysia measure river water quality?

The main tool is the **Water Quality Index (WQI)** used by the Department of Environment (DOE). The WQI summarises several water quality parameters into a single score so that the status of a river can be compared over time and between locations.

The parameters that make up this index include dissolved oxygen (DO), biochemical oxygen demand (BOD), chemical oxygen demand (COD), ammonia nitrogen (NH3-N), suspended solids (SS) and pH. For river basin monitoring, the three main pollution indicators listed in the open data are BOD5, ammonia nitrogen and suspended solids. Based on this combined score, the DOE classifies rivers into three status categories.

| WQI status class | Brief meaning | Implication for water supply |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Clean | Good water quality | Suitable as a raw water source with conventional treatment |
| Slightly Polluted | Moderate quality | Requires more treatment; at risk if the pollution load increases |
| Polluted | Poor quality | Unsuitable without advanced treatment; high risk to supply |

The DOE monitors water quality through more than 1,300 monitoring stations (a mix of manual and automatic), covering nearly 700 rivers and around 150 river basins across the country. It is this monitoring network that allows authorities to detect sudden changes — for example a spike in ammonia — before pollutants reach the water intake.

## How clean are Malaysia's rivers really?

The overall picture is better than commonly assumed, but remains fragile. Based on 2024 data shared by the government, of **672 major rivers** monitored:

- **475 rivers (71%)** were classified as clean
- **170 rivers (25%)** slightly polluted
- **27 rivers (4%)** polluted

These figures conceal one important concern: the number of polluted rivers has not fallen to zero, and those 27 polluted major waterways are already enough to trigger calls for tighter wastewater discharge standards. Proposed new standards cover 11 parameters including pH, BOD, COD, suspended solids, oil and grease, nitrogen, mercury and colour — with the aim that river water is not only safe for recreation but also serves as a source for consumer use.

It must be stressed: an average "clean" river quality does not protect consumers from a single acute pollution event. One illegal spill in the wrong location can shut down a treatment plant within hours.

## Why do illegal discharges shut down water treatment plants?

The most frequent cause of supply disruptions in the Klang Valley is not a lack of rainfall, but **contamination of raw water sources** — particularly odour pollution and chemical spills from illegal industrial activity upstream.

The logic is simple but harsh: when raw water comes in with pollutants exceeding the level that can be safely treated, plant operators cannot continue production. Shutting the plant down protects consumers from contaminated water, but the consequence is a supply disruption across a wide area.

The case of 23 July 2024 illustrates this pattern clearly. A foul odour was detected in **Sungai Kundang** at 1.30 am, then in **Sungai Sembah** by 4.30 am. The source was suspected to be effluent from a leaking tank at a factory processing acrylic materials for recycling, causing material believed to be scheduled waste (polymethacrylic acid) to overflow into drains and waterways, eventually reaching Sungai Kuang. Four water treatment plants had to be shut down and seven districts — including Petaling, Klang, Shah Alam, Gombak, Kuala Lumpur, Hulu Selangor and Kuala Selangor — experienced unscheduled supply disruptions.

This is not an isolated incident. According to a report on river reserves, river pollution forces the closure of water treatment plants almost every year in Selangor, and the 2023 closure lasted nearly **six days**, affecting around **830,000** households and businesses. It is the economic and social burden of these recurring disruptions that has made protecting raw water sources a policy priority, not merely an environmental issue.

## What are the lessons from the Sungai Kim Kim tragedy?

If the odour disruptions demonstrate the fragility of the system, the **Sungai Kim Kim** tragedy in Pasir Gudang, Johor in March 2019 demonstrates the serious public health risks arising from illegal discharges.

A tanker lorry operating under an illegal tyre recycling scheme dumped **between 20 and 40 tonnes of chemical waste** into Sungai Kim Kim. The pollutants identified included benzene, acrylonitrile and acrolein, and the list later grew to nine chemicals including hydrogen chloride, toluene, xylene and ethylbenzene. The toxic fumes produced caused residents and schoolchildren to suffer nausea, dizziness, breathing difficulties and eye irritation.

The impact unfolded in several waves: dozens of victims were rushed to hospital in the early days, and the number of schools closed rose from 13 initially to more than **100 schools** by the end of March. Several parties — involving both Malaysian and Singaporean nationals — were charged in connection with the illegal dumping. Kim Kim became a reminder that improperly managed industrial waste can turn into a public health crisis within a matter of hours.

## How do river reserves and catchment areas protect the supply?

If the problem is pollutants entering rivers, the structural solution is to keep risky activities away from riverbanks. This is where the **river reserve** plays its role.

In Selangor, river reserves operate under **Section 48(1) of the LUAS Enactment 1999**, which empowers the Selangor Water Management Board to impose restrictions on activities within protection zones and buffer zones. This zone extends **50 metres on both banks of the river**, and restricts things such as:

- erecting structures or building roads;
- storing chemicals;
- altering existing land contours;
- clearing riparian vegetation and growth; and
- discharging any effluent and waste.

Violations under this regime can attract a fine not exceeding **RM50,000** or imprisonment not exceeding **two years**, or both. In addition, according to a Macaranga report, 2023 amendments to the Environmental Quality Act 1974 increased the penalties for pollution by setting a minimum fine of RM5,000 and a maximum of up to RM10 million, along with a mandatory prison sentence of not more than five years. (These figures for the 1974 Act amendments come from a secondary report and should be verified against the official Gazette text.)

The challenge is implementation on the ground. Of the roughly **3,956 kilometres** of rivers in Selangor, around **2,078 kilometres** have been mapped for boundary demarcation, with the remainder scheduled to be mapped in phases. Boundaries that have not yet been gazetted mean protection zones are harder to enforce in those areas.

## What comes next

Malaysia's water security depends on two things working in tandem: monitoring that detects pollution early, and structural protection that prevents pollutants from reaching rivers. The WQI and the DOE's network of stations look after the first part; river reserves, enforcement of discharge standards, and the gazetting of river boundaries look after the second.

For readers who want to act on or keep track of this issue, some practical steps:

- **Check the current status** through the Department of Environment's open data and OpenDOSM to see the latest river basin quality.
- **Report pollution** — foul odours, discoloured water, or suspicious waste dumping — to the DOE or the state water authority as quickly as possible, because an early response reduces the scale of plant closures.
- **Follow developments on stricter wastewater discharge standards**, as these will determine how much pollution load is permitted to enter rivers in the future.
