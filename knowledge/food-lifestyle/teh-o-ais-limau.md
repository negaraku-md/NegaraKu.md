Order "teh tarik" and you get the rich, frothy, milky icon of the Malaysian
coffee shop. Order "teh-O ais limau" and you get its opposite number: no milk, no
foam, just cold black tea cut with the sharp, fragrant juice of a little green
lime. On a sweltering afternoon it is, for many people, the better drink — and it
is one of the most ordered glasses at any mamak.

## What teh-O ais limau is

Teh-O ais limau is **iced lime tea**: strong black tea, served black (no milk),
poured over ice and sharpened with lime. Where teh tarik is thick and sweet with
condensed milk, this is light, tart and clean — a thirst-quencher rather than a
treat. The tea is usually sweetened, and the lime does the rest, giving the drink
a bright citrus aroma and a sour edge that makes it especially good in the heat.
It is, in effect, teh tarik's milk-free, citrus cousin; the pulled-tea original is
covered in full in [teh tarik](/food-lifestyle/teh-tarik).

For where it sits among the country's wider drinks board, see the
[Malaysian drinks](/food-lifestyle/malaysian-drinks) hub.

## The name, word by word

The beauty of the order is that the name *is* the recipe. Each word is a modifier
in the coffee-shop code:

| Word | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| teh | Tea |
| O | Black — no milk |
| ais | Iced (also heard as "peng") |
| limau | Lime |
| kosong | No sugar (optional add-on) |
| panas | Hot (for the hot version) |

So "teh-O ais limau" reads straight off as *tea, black, iced, with lime*, and
"teh-O limau panas" is the hot version. Want it unsweetened? Add **kosong**:
"teh-O ais limau kosong". The full logic of *O*, *C*, *kosong*, *gao* and *peng*
runs across every drink at the counter and is laid out in the
[kopi, teh tarik and coffee-shop drinks](/food-lifestyle/kopi-teh-tarik-and-coffeeshop-drinks)
guide.

### What the "O" means

The one word with a story is **O**. It marks tea (or coffee) served black, without
milk — but where it comes from is debated. The most common explanation is that it
is the **Hokkien word for "black"**; some accounts instead gloss it as
**"original"**. Both readings are in circulation, so it is best to note the
dispute and move on: in practice, "O" always means no milk.

## Which lime goes in

The lime is not just any lime. Malaysia uses several different citrus fruits for
different jobs, and the one that typically goes into teh-O ais limau is the small,
round, intensely fragrant **calamansi** — *limau kasturi* in Malay. Its juice is
sharply sour and its skin aromatic, which is exactly what gives the drink its
distinctive lift. Swap in a big, blander Western lime and it is simply not the
same glass.

## Why it is a mamak staple

Teh-O ais limau earns its place for the plainest of reasons: it is cheap,
non-alcoholic, milk-free and extremely refreshing, which makes it an easy order
for anyone at the table and a natural choice when teh tarik feels too heavy in the
heat. It belongs to the same 24-hour, sit-and-talk world as the rest of the
coffee-shop menu — see
[kopitiam and mamak culture](/food-lifestyle/kopitiam-mamak-culture) for the
institution itself.
