Walk through any Malaysian wet market, kampung kitchen or kedai runcit shelf of bottled condiments and you will meet the same trio of names: **belacan**, **cincalok** and **budu**. They look and smell as though they belong to one family — all salty, all pungent, all made by letting small sea creatures ferment in salt — and in menus and recipes the names get used loosely. But they are three distinct things, made differently, from different starting points, and used in different ways. Knowing which is which saves you from toasting something that should be eaten raw, or pouring a fish sauce where a shrimp paste belongs.

The fastest way to keep them straight is to ask two questions: **what is it made from, and what form does it take?**

## The one-line distinction

- **Belacan** — tiny shrimp, made **solid**. A firm, dark, dried paste you cut from a block and **toast before cooking** it into a dish.
- **Cincalok** — tiny shrimp, kept **wet and whole**. Small shrimp suspended in a salty brine, the individual shrimp still visible, usually eaten **raw** as a dip.
- **Budu** — anchovies, turned to **liquid**. A dark, pourable **fish sauce** fermented for months, the anchor of the east-coast table.

Two of the three — belacan and cincalok — are made from the very same shrimp. The third, budu, is the one made from fish. Hold on to that and the rest falls into place.

## Belacan: shrimp made solid

Belacan is Malaysia's fermented **shrimp paste**, made from tiny shrimp known locally as *geragau* (genus *Acetes*, sometimes loosely called krill) and salt. The shrimp are salted, fermented, then repeatedly sun-dried and pounded before being pressed into firm, dark, dense blocks. By the end there are no individual shrimp left to see — it is a smooth, opaque paste.

Raw belacan is sharply pungent, so cooks **almost always toast it first** — dry in a pan, or wrapped in foil or banana leaf over heat — to mellow and round out its aroma before crumbling it into a dish. It is the umami backbone of **sambal belacan**, rojak, northern asam laksa and nasi goreng, and it belongs to a wider Southeast Asian family that includes Indonesia's *terasi*, Thailand's *kapi* and the Philippines' *bagoong*. It is made and sold nationwide, with a strong production association with **Penang and the west coast**.

> For the full story of belacan — how it is made, graded and used — see our dedicated article on [belacan](/en/food-lifestyle/belacan). Here it is the reference point for telling the other two apart.

## Cincalok: shrimp kept wet and whole

Cincalok (also spelled **cencaluk**) starts from the **same tiny shrimp as belacan** — but instead of being dried and pounded hard, the whole shrimp are packed into a salty brine, **often with a little rice added**, and left to ferment for only a short time. The result could not look more different from belacan: a **wet, pourable mixture in which the individual tiny shrimp stay visible**, salty with a sour-tangy edge. It is sold in jars, and because it ferments sealed and pressure can build up inside, it is worth opening the container carefully.

Cincalok is most strongly tied to **Melaka**, where it has long been part of Malay, **Peranakan and [Kristang](/arts-culture/kristang-eurasian-culture)** (Portuguese-Eurasian) cooking; the condiment is often traced, by tradition, to Melaka's Portuguese era. Unlike belacan, it is usually **eaten raw**. The classic preparation is a quick dip or sambal — cincalok stirred with **calamansi or lime juice, sliced shallots and bird's-eye chilli (cili padi)** — served alongside plain rice. It is also commonly fried into an **omelette (telur cincalok)**. If belacan is a cooking ingredient, cincalok is more often a condiment that arrives at the table more or less as it came out of the jar.

## Budu: fish turned to liquid

Budu is the one that breaks the shrimp pattern. It is a **fermented fish sauce**, made by salting **anchovies (ikan bilis)** and leaving them to break down over **months** into a dark, savoury, pourable liquid. There are no shrimp in it at all, which is why it pours like a sauce rather than sitting like a paste or a suspension of whole shrimp.

Budu is the defining condiment of the **east coast — Kelantan and Terengganu** — and of the Malay communities across the border in southern Thailand. It is the anchor for **nasi kerabu**, and a dip for hot rice, grilled or fried fish, and **ulam** (raw herbs and vegetables). Like cincalok, it is normally used **raw**: before serving, cooks commonly stir in lime juice, sliced shallots, chilli and a little sugar to balance its saltiness. Those are additions at the table, not part of the fermentation.

> Budu sits at the heart of the Kelantanese table — see our guide to [Kelantan food](/en/food-lifestyle/kelantan-food) for the dishes it accompanies.

## Side by side

| | **Belacan** | **Cincalok** | **Budu** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Made from** | Tiny shrimp (*geragau*, *Acetes*) + salt | Same tiny shrimp + salt (often + rice) | **Anchovies** (*ikan bilis*) + salt |
| **Form** | Firm dark **solid block** (smooth paste) | **Wet brine**, whole shrimp **visible** | **Dark liquid** fish sauce |
| **Fermentation** | Weeks to months, repeatedly dried and pounded | Short — a few days to about two weeks | Months |
| **Raw or cooked?** | **Toasted / cooked** into dishes | **Usually raw**, as a dip (also fried) | **Usually raw**, stirred with lime-chilli-shallot |
| **Heartland** | Nationwide; strong **Penang** / west coast | **Melaka** (Peranakan / Kristang) | **Kelantan & Terengganu** (east coast) |
| **Signature use** | Sambal belacan, rojak, asam laksa, nasi goreng | Cincalok dip; condiment with rice; telur cincalok | Dip for nasi kerabu, rice, fish and ulam |

## A note on using them

All three are **intensely salty and strongly flavoured**, which is the point: they are seasonings used in small amounts, not foods eaten on their own. Belacan is normally cooked; cincalok and budu are normally used raw, which is entirely traditional. If you are buying them for the first time, start with a small jar, use a little, and taste as you go — a teaspoon of any of them reshapes a plate of rice. And if a recipe simply says "shrimp paste," it means belacan: reach for the hard block you toast, not the wet jar of whole shrimp or the bottle of brown fish sauce.
