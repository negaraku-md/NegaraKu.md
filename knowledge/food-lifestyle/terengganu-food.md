Ask a Malaysian where to eat the country's best nasi dagang and keropok lekor,
and the answer is almost always the same stretch of coast: Terengganu. The
state curls along the South China Sea in the country's north-east, and its
kitchen is shaped by that geography more directly than almost any other in
Malaysia — fish first, coconut and rice close behind, and a sweetness that
quietly separates its cooking from its neighbours'.

## A fish-forward, sweet-leaning kitchen

Terengganu's food is, before anything else, a cuisine of the sea. The state's
tourism body puts it plainly: most of the local food is fish-based, with rice
and glutinous rice as the staple carbohydrates. A long coastline and a deep-
rooted fishing industry mean that fish turns up not only grilled or curried but
pounded into crackers, fermented into sauce and folded into snacks — a thrift
that turned surplus catch into some of the peninsula's most recognisable foods.

The second thread is a palate that many eaters and writers describe as leaning
sweet, with palm sugar (*gula apong* or *gula melaka*) softening curries and
sauces that elsewhere run saltier or sharper. This is a widely repeated
characterisation rather than a measured fact, and it is best treated as a
tendency; individual cooks and dishes vary. Terengganu's cooking is counted
among the distinctive regional branches of
[Malay cuisine](/food-lifestyle/malay-cuisine), and sits within Malaysia's
broader map of [regional and ethnic cuisines](/food-lifestyle/regional-and-ethnic-cuisines).

## Nasi dagang and its gulai ikan tongkol

If one dish stands for Terengganu, it is **nasi dagang** — literally "trader's
rice," a name usually explained by the idea of rice eaten by travelling
merchants, though the etymology is not firmly settled. It is above all a
breakfast, and it carries its own dedicated guide; see
[nasi dagang](/food-lifestyle/nasi-dagang) for the dish in full.

In short, the rice — in Terengganu typically a pale, white grain, often
described as a blend of regular and glutinous or jasmine rice — is partly steamed,
then finished with coconut milk, fenugreek and sliced shallots so that it cooks
into a rich, faintly nutty bed. It is served with a coconut-milk fish curry,
**gulai ikan tongkol**, made from tuna (*ikan aya*/*tongkol*) simmered in a
turmeric-and-tamarind gravy, sometimes with the sour note of *belimbing buluh*,
and rounded off with pickled vegetables (*acar*). Terengganu's version is
generally simpler and whiter than the Kelantan style next door, which uses a
wild rice that lends a purplish, speckled look. The Terengganu-versus-Kelantan
contrast — and the exact rice formula — is the kind of detail local cooks debate,
so treat the specifics as a guide rather than a rule.

## Keropok lekor and keropok keping

Terengganu's other great ambassador is a snack. **Keropok lekor** is a fish
cracker made by grinding fish — commonly *ikan tamban*, sardine or *selayang* —
into a paste, mixing it with sago (or tapioca) starch, salt and sugar, then
shaping the dough into long rolls. (*Lekor* is said to come from a Terengganu
word for the rolling or coiling motion.) The rolls are boiled and can then be
eaten soft (*lekor rebus*) or, more popularly, sliced and deep-fried until
chewy-crisp (*lekor goreng*), always with a dark, sweet-spicy chilli dip
particular to the state.

Its thin, brittle cousin is **keropok keping** — the same fish-and-starch dough
rolled or pressed flat, dried into discs and then deep-fried so it puffs into a
light, crunchy cracker. The two are easy to confuse by name, but the texture
tells them apart: *lekor* is dense and chewy, *keping* is crisp and airy.
A commonly repeated account traces keropok lekor to east-coast fishermen
preserving surplus catch during the monsoon season, when rough seas kept boats
ashore — a plausible but not well-documented origin story worth treating with
some caution.

## Sata, satar and otak-otak

The banana leaf does a lot of work in Terengganu. **Sata** (also written
**satar**) is spiced fish meat — minced and bound with grated coconut, chilli,
shallot, ginger and turmeric — packed into a flat banana-leaf parcel, pinned to
a thin bamboo stick and grilled slowly over charcoal until the leaf chars and
the filling sets into a smoky, faintly sweet cake. It is sold in little
triangular or pyramid-shaped bundles, often several to a skewer, and is
associated especially with Kemaman in the state's south. Reference sources
generally treat "sata" and "satar" as the same dish under two spellings, though
some locals use the names for slightly different versions; the distinction is
not firmly fixed.

Close kin is **otak-otak**, a grilled or steamed fish paste in a leaf wrapper
that is found across Malaysia, Singapore and Indonesia in many regional forms
and is generally thought to blend Malay and Peranakan origins. On the east
coast it overlaps with sata; elsewhere — in Muar or among Penang's Peranakan,
for instance — it takes on quite different shapes, wrappers and sauces. The dish
has its own guide at [otak-otak](/food-lifestyle/otak-otak).

## Laksam and the noodle bowls

Terengganu shares with Kelantan a soft, comforting noodle dish called
**laksam**. Broad sheets of rice-flour noodle are steamed, rolled up like a
loose Swiss roll and sliced into thick coils, then bathed in a pale, mild gravy
of pounded fish and coconut milk and topped with finely shredded raw vegetables
and herbs. It is gentler and creamier than the sour, sharp laksa of the west
coast — closer in spirit to a savoury rice-noodle porridge — and belongs to the
same sprawling noodle family covered under laksa. The state also has its own
*laksa Terengganu*, a soupier relative distinct from laksam, underlining how the
east coast rings its own changes on familiar dishes.

## Nasi minyak and the festive table

For weddings, Hari Raya and other celebrations, Terengganu — like much of the
Malay peninsula — turns to **nasi minyak**, "oily rice": fragrant long-grain
rice cooked with ghee or oil, milk, and warm spices such as cardamom, clove,
cinnamon and star anise, often tinted and garnished with fried shallots,
raisins and nuts. It is a dish of hospitality, served alongside richer meat
curries, *ayam masak merah*, *daging masak hitam* and *acar* at kenduri (feasts).
Nasi minyak is not unique to Terengganu, but it anchors the state's festive
table and shows the Indian- and Middle Eastern-influenced side of a kitchen more
often associated with everyday fish cookery.

## Budu, sambal and local kuih

Underpinning much of this cooking is **budu**, a fermented anchovy sauce that is
a defining condiment of the east coast. Small fish are salted and left to
ferment for months, often with palm sugar and tamarind added, breaking down into
a dark, pungent, umami-rich liquid. It is eaten as a dip or dressing with rice,
grilled fish and raw vegetables (*ulam*) — frequently as the base of a *budu*
sambal — and is increasingly sold under the gentler label *sos bilis*, or
anchovy sauce.

Terengganu's sweet side shows in its *kuih*. The state is known for traditional
cakes and snacks with distinctive local names — among them *akok* (a baked
egg-and-palm-sugar custard cake), *keropok* sweets, and rolled or steamed kuih
sold at markets such as Kuala Terengganu's Pasar Payang. As with its savoury
cooking, coconut and palm sugar run through these sweets, and many are prepared
in quantity for the fasting month and festive seasons. Together — the fish
snacks, the coconut rice, the grilled parcels and the sweet kuih — they make
Terengganu one of the most self-contained and recognisable regional kitchens in
Malaysia.
