Wake any Malaysian at three in the morning and ask where you can get something to eat, and the answer is almost certainly the same: mamak. It opens before dawn, closes who knows when, while the kopitiam has been pouring coffee since our grandparents' day — and it is between these two spaces that the people of a multiethnic nation meet every day. Not in official halls, not at ceremonies, but at grubby marble tables and wobbly plastic chairs.

In December 2024, the world finally agreed that this matters. UNESCO inscribed "Breakfast culture in Malaysia" on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity — a glass of teh tarik and a piece of roti canai now stand alongside the world's other great heritage.

## What exactly are kopitiam, mamak and food courts?

All three are everyday eateries, but each has a character of its own.

**Kopitiam** is a traditional coffee shop. The word itself tells the story: a blend of *kopi* (Malay) and *tiam* (Hokkien, meaning shop). Many kopitiam were founded and passed down by Chinese families, especially the Hainanese community. The atmosphere is slow and steady — ceiling fans turning lazily, marble tables, and coffee served in flower-patterned porcelain cups.

**Mamak** refers to shops or stalls run by the Indian-Muslim community. This is the pulse of Malaysian nightlife — many operate late into the night until the early hours, and some never close. Big screens broadcast football matches, teh tarik is pulled high, and roti canai is flung in the open kitchen.

**Food courts** (and roadside stalls) are clusters of many hawkers under one roof or along a walkway, each a specialist in one dish — nasi lemak at one stall, char kuey teow at another, drinks at a separate counter.

What unites all three is not the menu but the social function. These are all default spaces for Malaysians to meet, regardless of ethnicity, rank or the size of one's wallet. The 2024 UNESCO recognition states plainly that hawker stalls and kopitiam are the "heart" of the nation's breakfast culture — places where people from various socio-economic backgrounds socialise and make friends while enjoying a meal.

## Where did kopitiam culture come from?

The story begins with waves of migration. Among the Chinese dialect groups that came to Malaya, the Hainanese community arrived relatively late compared with the others. Because of this, the job opportunities left to them were often food-related — cooking in the homes of British colonial families, in hotels and aboard ships.

Having mastered Western cooking, many of them opened their own businesses and blended European tastes with local ingredients. From this was born the classic kopitiam menu still found today: toast spread with kaya (coconut jam), soft-boiled eggs, and thick local coffee. The Hainanese community is still often cited as the pioneer of the Chinese food and beverage industry in Malaysia, with businesses passed down from father to son.

Take, for example, the heritage Hainanese kopitiam around Petaling Street, Kuala Lumpur. The secret of its coffee lies not in an espresso machine but in the way the beans are roasted. Robusta beans are roasted together with butter and sugar at high heat until the sugar caramelises, coating the beans and giving off a dark aroma like rich chocolate. This is the foundation of the kopitiam coffee flavour, utterly different from modern "specialty" coffee.

The preparation has an art of its own too. The coarse coffee grounds are usually brewed in a long cloth filter resembling a sock — which is why it is often called "kopi sock" — then poured back and forth to make it thick and fragrant.

## How did mamak become Malaysia's 24-hour space?

If the kopitiam is Malaysia's morning, the mamak is its night.

The word "mamak" comes from the Tamil word for "uncle", and in Malaysia it refers to Indian-Muslim men. These Indian-Muslim traders came during the British colonial era, many from Tamil Nadu in South India, some continuing the tradition of older trading communities from the Coromandel Coast.

The two mamak icons — teh tarik and roti canai — each have their own migration history. Teh tarik is traced to the *sarabat* stalls set up by Indian-Muslim traders after the Second World War, near rubber estates to serve the estate workers. To mask the bitterness of cheap tea, they added condensed milk and poured the tea back and forth between two containers from a height — the "pulling" process that is not just a gimmick but cools and froths the tea at once. Roti canai, meanwhile, is closely related to the South Indian *parotta*, a layered flatbread brought by Indian immigrants to the Peninsula.

What makes the mamak special is its operating hours. Many mamak shops open late into the early hours, making them the nation's informal "supper" spot — a destination after a work shift, after watching football, or when hunger strikes at midnight. This is where you see the most Malaysian scene of all: Malays, Chinese, Indians, expatriates and tourists sharing the same space, each with a teh tarik in hand.

## Why did UNESCO recognise Malaysia's breakfast culture?

On 30 March 2023, the Department of National Heritage under the Ministry of Tourism, Arts and Culture submitted the nomination to UNESCO — the first time Malaysia nominated something related to food and gastronomy.

Almost two years later, at the 19th session of the Intergovernmental Committee for the Safeguarding of the Intangible Cultural Heritage held in Asunción, Paraguay in December 2024, "Breakfast culture in Malaysia: a dining experience in a multiethnic society" (nomination No. 02113) was inscribed on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

Interestingly, the recognition was not merely for the food. The official UNESCO decision mentions flagship dishes such as nasi lemak, roti canai and teh tarik, but the real core is the *practice* — having breakfast together, spending time at the table. The decision states that the preparation and enjoyment of Malaysia's breakfast foods, "although originally practised by a particular community, eventually became common and enjoyed by all, regardless of ethnic background." The Committee concluded that this element "strengthens the unity and social bonds that reflect Malaysia's identity as a multiethnic nation."

In other words, what the world elevated is the kopitiam and mamak table itself — as a space of unity.

## Kopitiam, mamak, modern café or food court — what's the difference?

| Feature | Traditional kopitiam | Mamak shop | Café / modern kopitiam | Food court / stalls |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Typical founding community | Chinese (mostly Hainanese) | Indian-Muslim | Various, modern franchises | Various ethnicities |
| Peak hours | Morning to midday | Afternoon to late night | All day | Breakfast & lunch |
| Iconic dishes | Kaya toast, soft-boiled eggs, noodles | Roti canai, nasi kandar, mee goreng | Western & local breakfast | Nasi lemak, char kuey teow, laksa |
| Drinks | Local coffee & tea | Teh tarik, Milo | Local coffee & espresso | Separate drinks counter |
| Atmosphere | Slow, nostalgic | Lively, open, late night | Air-conditioned, "Instagram" | Busy, many stalls |

Bear in mind that these lines are not rigid. Many of today's modern "kopitiam" are actually air-conditioned franchise chains serving classic dishes in a contemporary setting, while some mamak stalls also serve breakfast in the morning. What endures is their role as relaxed public spaces.

## How do you order at a kopitiam and mamak?

This is the part that often confuses visitors: ordering drinks at a kopitiam has its own language, brief but precise. Once you understand the pattern, you can order anything.

The basics go like this — the drink name first (kopi or teh), followed by codes for milk, sugar and temperature:

| Order | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Kopi | Coffee with sweetened condensed milk |
| Kopi O | Black coffee with sugar, no milk |
| Kopi O kosong | Black coffee, no sugar, no milk |
| Kopi C | Coffee with evaporated milk and sugar |
| Kopi peng / kopi ais | Iced cold coffee |
| Teh | Tea with sweetened condensed milk |
| Teh tarik | Milk tea "pulled" until frothy |
| Teh O | Tea without milk (usually with sugar) |
| Teh O ais limau | Iced tea with a squeeze of lime |
| ...kurang manis | Less sugar on any of the orders above |

A few notes on the terms: the letter "O" is often associated with the Hokkien word for black (i.e. without milk), while "C" is often associated with the Hainanese term for fresh milk. "Kosong" means without sugar. "Peng" is Hokkien for ice.

At the mamak, the emphasis shifts to roti. Plain "roti canai", "roti telur" with egg, "roti tisu" thin and tall like a cone, "roti bom" sweeter and more buttery, and "roti canai banjir" drenched in dhal curry. For noodles there's "mee goreng mamak", spicy-sweet, and "maggi goreng" — instant noodles fried with egg and vegetables. Nasi kandar, a rice dish with an assortment of curries and side dishes mixed together ("campur" or *banjir*), is the emblem of the Penang mamak.

## Just how big is this industry?

The official figures show this sector is not just culture — it is an economic backbone.

According to the Economic Census 2023 by the Department of Statistics Malaysia (DOSM), for the reference year 2022:

- **136,453 establishments** in the food and beverage services sector
- **RM99.0 billion** in gross output
- **RM43.8 billion** in value added
- **1,079,843 workers** — over a million people depend on this sector
- **RM15.5 billion** in salaries and wages paid

In terms of distribution, Selangor leads with 24,625 establishments (18.0 per cent of the national total), followed by Johor (15,727) and the Federal Territory of Kuala Lumpur (13,849). That figure of a million workers gives a sense of how large the people's economic dependence is — from teh-pulling hands to roti-flinging cooks to family stall traders — on this everyday dining ecosystem.

## How do you choose where to go?

Depending on the time and mood, this short guide can help:

- **Want a quiet, nostalgic breakfast?** Look for an old kopitiam — order kaya toast, soft-boiled eggs and coffee. Go early, because the famous ones fill up fast.
- **Midnight hunger or after watching football?** The mamak is the answer. Roti canai, maggi goreng and teh tarik at this hour "hit different".
- **Want to try many dishes at once?** A food court lets everyone in the group order from different stalls and share a table.
- **Bringing foreign visitors?** The mamak offers the most all-round experience — a lively atmosphere, reasonable prices, and a cross-ethnic menu in one place.
- **Want an air-conditioned setting with classic dishes?** Modern kopitiam chains offer comfort without losing the local flavour.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Thinking "kopitiam" and "café" are the same.** A traditional kopitiam is not a Western-style coffee shop; don't expect latte art or croissants — what you get is sock coffee and toast.
- **Confusing the ordering terms.** "Kopi O" is not sugar-free coffee — it is black coffee that still has sugar. For truly unsweetened, ask for "kopi O kosong".
- **Assuming all mamak are open 24 hours.** Many open late, but plenty of small stalls and town shops close at night. Check first if you're counting on it.
- **Assuming halal status automatically.** Mamak shops typically do not serve pork or alcohol, but if official certainty is needed, check for the displayed JAKIM halal certificate — not every premises holds one.
- **Being surprised by "shared" tables.** During busy hours, sharing a table with strangers is the norm, not rudeness.
- **Equating roti canai only with modern Indian breakfast.** In Malaysia it is eaten all day, across all ethnicities — part of the daily routine, not a special dish.

## What's next

The 2024 UNESCO recognition lifted Malaysia's dining culture onto the world stage, but it also raises a question of preservation: how do you sustain heritage kopitiam when the third and fourth generations begin to leave the family business, and when modern franchises replace marble tables with Instagram tables?

If you want to explore further, a good place to start is reading the official UNESCO decision 19.COM 7.B.47 at *ich.unesco.org* to understand the scope of the recognition, along with the DOSM Economic Census data for a picture of the industry's scale. For those interested in venturing into their own food business, the related articles on food and beverage licences and premises licences in Malaysia will explain the registration and permit requirements.

But the best way to understand this culture remains just one: find a nearby kopitiam or mamak, order a teh tarik, and take your time. That, precisely, is what the world recognised.
