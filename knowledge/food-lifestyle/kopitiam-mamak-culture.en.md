Wake any Malaysian at three in the morning and ask where they can find food, and the answer is almost certainly the same: mamak. Opening at dawn and closing who-knows-when, while the kopitiam has been serving coffee since the days of our grandparents — and it is between these two spaces that the people of a multi-ethnic nation meet every day. Not in official halls, not at ceremonies, but at grimy marble tables and wobbly plastic chairs.

In December 2024, the world finally agreed that this matters. UNESCO inscribed "Breakfast culture in Malaysia" on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity — a glass of teh tarik and a piece of roti canai now stand alongside the world's other great heritages.

## What exactly are kopitiam, mamak and food courts?

All three are everyday dining spaces, but each has a character of its own.

**Kopitiam** is a traditional coffee shop. The word itself tells the story: a combination of *kopi* (Malay) and *tiam* (Hokkien, meaning shop). Many kopitiams were founded and passed down by Chinese families, especially the Hainanese community. The atmosphere is slow and steady — ceiling fans turning gently, marble tables, and coffee served in flower-patterned porcelain cups.

**Mamak** refers to shops or stalls run by the Indian-Muslim community. This is the lifeblood of Malaysian nightlife — many operate late into the night until the early morning, and some never close. Big screens broadcast football matches, teh tarik is pulled high, and roti canai is flung in the open kitchen.

**Food courts** (and roadside stalls) are clusters of many hawkers under one roof or along a walkway, each a specialist in a single dish — nasi lemak at one stall, char kuey teow at another, drinks at a separate counter.

What unites all three is not the menu but the social function. These are all default spaces for Malaysians to meet, regardless of ethnicity, rank or the contents of their wallet. The 2024 UNESCO recognition explicitly states that hawker stalls and kopitiams are the "heart" of the nation's breakfast culture — places where people from various socioeconomic backgrounds socialise and make friends while sharing a meal.

## Where does kopitiam culture come from?

The story begins with waves of migration. Among the Chinese dialect groups who came to Malaya, the Hainanese community arrived relatively late compared to the others. Because of this, the job opportunities left for them were often food-related — cooking in the homes of British colonial families, in hotels, and aboard ships.

Having become skilled in Western cooking, many of them opened their own businesses and blended European tastes with local ingredients. From this came the classic kopitiam menu still found today: toast spread with kaya (coconut jam), half-boiled eggs, and thick local coffee. The Hainanese community is to this day often cited as the pioneer of the Chinese food-and-beverage trade in Malaysia, with businesses passed from father to son.

Take, for example, the heritage Hainanese kopitiams around Petaling Street, Kuala Lumpur. The secret of their coffee lies not in an espresso machine, but in how the beans are roasted. Robusta beans are roasted with butter and sugar at high heat until the sugar caramelises, coating the beans and giving a dark aroma like rich chocolate. This is the flavour base of kopitiam coffee, entirely different from modern "specialty" coffee.

Its preparation, too, has an art of its own. The coarse coffee grounds are usually brewed in a long cloth filter resembling a sock — which is why it is often called "kopi sock" — then poured back and forth to make it thick and fragrant.

## How did mamak become Malaysia's 24-hour space?

If the kopitiam is Malaysia's morning, the mamak is its night.

The word "mamak" comes from the Tamil word for "uncle", and in Malaysia it refers to Indian-Muslim men. These Indian-Muslim traders came during the British colonial era, many from Tamil Nadu in South India, some continuing the tradition of older merchant groups from the Coromandel Coast.

The two icons of the mamak — teh tarik and roti canai — each have their own migration history. Teh tarik traces back to the *sarabat* stalls set up by Indian-Muslim traders after the Second World War, near rubber plantations to serve estate workers. To mask the bitterness of cheap tea, they added condensed milk and poured the tea back and forth between two vessels from a height — the "pulling" process, which is not mere showmanship but cools and froths the tea at once. Roti canai, meanwhile, is closely related to the South Indian *parotta*, a layered flatbread brought by Indian migrants to the Peninsula.

What makes the mamak special is its operating hours. Many mamak shops stay open late into the early morning, making them the nation's informal "supper" spot — the destination after a work shift, after watching football, or when hunger strikes in the middle of the night. This is where you see the most Malaysian sight of all: Malays, Chinese, Indians, expatriates and tourists sharing the same space, each with a teh tarik in hand.

## Why did UNESCO recognise Malaysia's breakfast culture?

On 30 March 2023, the Department of National Heritage under the Ministry of Tourism, Arts and Culture submitted the nomination to UNESCO — the first time Malaysia nominated something related to food and gastronomy.

Nearly two years later, at the 19th session of the Intergovernmental Committee for the Safeguarding of the Intangible Cultural Heritage held in Asunción, Paraguay in December 2024, "Breakfast culture in Malaysia: a communal dining experience in a multi-ethnic society" (nomination No. 02113) was inscribed on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

Interestingly, the recognition was not merely for the food. The official UNESCO decision mentions anchor dishes such as nasi lemak, roti canai and teh tarik, but the true core is the *practice* — having breakfast together, spending time at the table. The decision states that the preparation and enjoyment of Malaysia's breakfast foods, "although originally practised by a particular ethnic group, eventually became common and enjoyed by all, regardless of ethnic background." The Committee concluded that this element "strengthens the unity and social bonds that reflect Malaysia's identity as a multi-ethnic nation."

In other words, what the world elevated is the kopitiam and mamak table itself — as a space of unity.

## Kopitiam, mamak, modern café or food court — what is the difference?

| Feature | Traditional kopitiam | Mamak shop | Modern café / kopitiam | Food court / stalls |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Typical founding community | Chinese (mostly Hainanese) | Indian-Muslim | Various, modern franchise | Various ethnicities |
| Peak hours | Morning to midday | Afternoon to late night | All day | Breakfast & lunch |
| Iconic dishes | Kaya toast, half-boiled eggs, noodles | Roti canai, nasi kandar, mee goreng | Western & local breakfast | Nasi lemak, char kuey teow, laksa |
| Drinks | Local coffee & tea | Teh tarik, Milo | Local coffee & espresso | Separate drinks counter |
| Atmosphere | Slow, nostalgic | Lively, open, late-night | Air-conditioned, "Instagram" | Busy, many stalls |

Bear in mind that these lines are not rigid. Many "kopitiams" today are actually air-conditioned franchise chains serving classic dishes in a contemporary setting, while some mamak stalls also serve breakfast in the morning. What endures is their role as relaxed public spaces.

## How do you order at a kopitiam and mamak?

This is the part that often confuses visitors: ordering drinks at a kopitiam has a language of its own, brief but precise. Once you understand the pattern, you can order anything.

The basics go like this — the drink name first (kopi or teh), followed by codes for milk, sugar and temperature:

| Order | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Kopi | Coffee with sweetened condensed milk |
| Kopi O | Black coffee with sugar, no milk |
| Kopi O kosong | Black coffee, no sugar, no milk |
| Kopi C | Coffee with evaporated milk and sugar |
| Kopi peng / kopi ais | Iced cold coffee |
| Teh | Tea with sweetened condensed milk |
| Teh tarik | Milk tea "pulled" until frothy |
| Teh O | Tea without milk (usually with sugar) |
| Teh O ais limau | Iced tea with a squeeze of lime |
| ...kurang manis | Less sugar on any of the orders above |

A few notes on the terminology: the letter "O" comes from the Hokkien word for black (that is, without milk). The origin of "C" is uncertain — some sources link it to the *Carnation* brand of evaporated milk, once among the most readily available, others to the Hainanese word for "fresh". "Kosong" means without sugar. "Peng" is Hokkien for ice.

At the mamak, the accent shifts to roti. "Roti canai" is the plain version, "roti telur" comes with egg, "roti tisu" is thin and tall like a cone, "roti bom" is sweeter and buttery, and "roti canai banjir" is drenched in dhal curry. For noodles there is "mee goreng mamak", spicy-sweet, and "maggi goreng" — instant noodles fried with egg and vegetables. Nasi kandar, a rice dish with various curries and side dishes mixed together ("campur" or *banjir*), is the icon of Penang mamak.

## How big is this industry, really?

The official figures show that this sector is not merely culture — it is an economic backbone.

According to the Economic Census 2023 by the Department of Statistics Malaysia (DOSM), for reference year 2022:

- **136,453 establishments** in the food and beverage services sector
- **RM99.0 billion** in gross output
- **RM43.8 billion** in value added
- **1,079,843 workers** — more than a million people depend on this sector
- **RM15.5 billion** in salaries and wages paid

In terms of distribution, Selangor leads with 24,625 establishments (18.0 per cent of the national total), followed by Johor (15,727) and the Federal Territory of Kuala Lumpur (13,849). That figure of a million workers gives a sense of how large the economic dependence is — from the teh tarik pullers and the roti flippers to family stall owners — on this everyday dining ecosystem.

## How do you choose where to go?

Depending on the time and mood, this quick guide may help:

- **Want a quiet, nostalgic breakfast?** Look for an old kopitiam — order kaya toast, half-boiled eggs and coffee. Go early, because the famous ones fill up fast.
- **Late-night hunger or after watching football?** The mamak is the answer. Roti canai, maggi goreng and teh tarik at this hour "hit different".
- **Want to try many dishes at once?** A food court lets everyone in the group order from different stalls and share a table.
- **Bringing a foreign visitor?** The mamak offers the most complete experience — a lively atmosphere, reasonable prices, and a cross-ethnic menu in one place.
- **Want an air-conditioned setting with classic dishes?** Modern kopitiam chains offer comfort without losing the local flavour.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Assuming "kopitiam" and "café" are the same.** A traditional kopitiam is not a Western-style coffee shop; don't expect an artful latte or a croissant — what's on offer is sock-brewed coffee and toast.
- **Confusing the ordering terms.** "Kopi O" is not sugar-free coffee — it is black coffee that still has sugar. For truly unsweetened, ask for "kopi O kosong".
- **Assuming all mamak are open 24 hours.** Many open late, but many small stalls and town shops close at night. Check first if you're relying on it.
- **Assuming halal status automatically.** Mamak shops usually do not serve pork or alcohol, but if official certainty is needed, check for the displayed JAKIM halal certificate — not every premise holds one.
- **Being surprised by "shared" tables.** During busy hours, sharing a table with strangers is customary, not rudeness.
- **Equating roti canai with modern Indian breakfast only.** In Malaysia it is eaten all day, across all ethnicities — part of the daily routine, not a special dish.

## What next

The 2024 UNESCO recognition raised Malaysia's dining culture onto the world stage, but it also brings a question of preservation: how do you keep heritage kopitiams alive when the third and fourth generations begin to leave the family business, and when modern franchises replace marble tables with Instagram tables?

If you want to explore further, a good place to start is reading the official UNESCO decision 19.COM 7.B.47 on *ich.unesco.org* to understand the scope of the recognition, along with the DOSM Economic Census data for a picture of the industry's scale. For those interested in entering the food business themselves, related articles on food and beverage licences and premise licences in Malaysia will clarify the registration and permit requirements.

But the best way to understand this culture remains just one: find a kopitiam or mamak nearby, order a teh tarik, and take your time. That, precisely, is what the world recognised.
