Cross the South China Sea from the Malaysian peninsula and the table changes. In
Sabah — the state on the northern tip of Borneo, once known as North Borneo — the
staples of the mainland give way to raw fish brightened with lime, fermented
relishes, a bewildering variety of town noodles, wild mangoes and seafood landed
that morning. It is one of Malaysia's most distinctive regional kitchens, and
understanding it means understanding the three streams that feed it.

## A cuisine of land, sea and migration

Sabah's food sits at the meeting point of three influences. The first is
indigenous: the [Kadazan-Dusun](/food-lifestyle/kadazan-dusun-food), the state's
largest native group, together with the Murut, Bajau, Rungus and others, cook a
larder of river and sea fish, hill rice, jungle ferns, wild fruit and fermented
preserves. The second is the coast — Sabah's long shoreline on nutrient-rich
waters supplies an abundance of fish and shellfish that shapes everyday eating.
The third is migration: Chinese settlers, many of them Hakka, arrived from the
late nineteenth century and brought the wheat- and egg-noodle traditions that
have since become town institutions, though the precise dialect-group attribution
is best treated with some caution.

The result is a cuisine that overlaps with, but is clearly distinct from, both
peninsular Malaysian cooking and its Bornean neighbour across the border,
[Sarawak](/food-lifestyle/sarawak-food). The sections below walk through the
dishes a visitor is most likely to meet.

## Hinava: Sabah's citrus-cured raw fish

The signature indigenous dish is **hinava**, a Kadazan-Dusun salad of raw fish
"cooked" not with heat but with acid. Fresh fish — commonly mackerel, though
sources vary on the species — is cut into pieces and cured in lime or calamansi
juice, then tossed with thinly sliced shallots, julienned ginger, chilli and,
distinctively, grated dried seed of the **bambangan**, a wild Bornean mango. The
citrus firms and whitens the flesh, which is why hinava is so often likened to
Latin American **ceviche**. That comparison is a useful description rather than a
proven history: some writers speculate about a Spanish or Iberian influence, but
there is no firm evidence, and the likeness is best read as culinary convergence.

Hinava is a fixture at weddings, community gatherings and the Kaamatan harvest
festival, and it has been recognised among Sabah's heritage dishes. The bambangan
seed is what sets it apart from a standard ceviche, lending a nutty, slightly
astringent depth beneath the sharp acidity.

## Fermentation and wild fruit: bosou, pinasakan and bambangan

Preserving the catch and the harvest is central to indigenous Sabahan cooking.
**Bosou** — also called **noonsom** or **tonsom** — is a tangy fermented dish made
from fish or raw meat packed with rice, salt and a natural fermenting and
preserving agent (pulverised *pangi*, or buah keluak, is traditional), then sealed
in a jar for weeks. The result is pungent and sour, eaten in small amounts as a
relish. A rice-free variant is cooked before eating.

**Pinasakan** is a home-style fish dish, typically using **basung** (big-eye
scad), simmered with fresh turmeric and a souring agent — the wild fruit
*takob-akob* or unripe bambangan — until tangy and aromatic. Sources describe both
braised and grilled preparations, so the exact method varies by cook and family.
The wild mango **bambangan** itself appears across the cuisine: its flesh is
eaten, its grated dried seed seasons hinava, and its half-ripe fruit is pickled
into a fermented relish. These dishes are usually eaten with plain rice, and
sometimes with the sticky sago staple ambuyat.

## A noodle state: tuaran mee and beaufort mee

Sabah is, quietly, one of Malaysia's great noodle regions, and several of its
noodles are named for the towns that made them — part of the wider world of
[Malaysian noodles](/food-lifestyle/malaysian-noodles). **Tuaran mee**, from the
town of Tuaran, is the best known: springy handmade egg noodles are first lightly
toasted or fried in oil, then wok-tossed "dry" with egg, vegetables and meat or
seafood, often served with slices of *chun kien* (Hakka-style egg rolls) and char
siu. A good plate is fragrant, springy and leaves a distinctly eggy aftertaste.

**Beaufort mee**, from the interior town of Beaufort, is prized for its smoky
char: handmade yellow noodles are fried hard over a fierce flame, then dressed
with a thick gravy, char siu or seafood and leafy greens. Aficionados note that
versions sold in Kota Kinabalu are usually an approximation, and that the genuine
smoky article is best eaten in Beaufort itself. **Tamparuli mee** is a related
local variant from yet another town. The pattern — one town, one noodle — is a
good lens on how deeply Chinese noodle-making settled into Sabah's districts.

## Beef and pork broths: ngiu chap and sang nyuk mian

Two noodle soups deserve their own mention. **Ngiu chap** (from the Hakka for
"mixed beef") is a hearty bowl of noodles — rice vermicelli, egg noodles or a mix
— in a slow-simmered beef or buffalo broth, loaded with a generous assortment of
beef slices, meatballs, tripe and other offal. Styles range from a lighter,
clearer Hainanese-leaning broth to a richer Hakka-influenced one, and some
village versions are adapted to indigenous tastes. Kota Kinabalu has long-running
specialists devoted to it.

**Sang nyuk mian** translates literally from Hakka as "raw pork noodles," but the
name is misleading: the pork is poached, not raw. It is a comforting bowl of
noodles in a peppery pork broth with slices of marinated pork and crisp lard bits,
popularly said to have originated in the east-coast town of Tawau before spreading
to Kota Kinabalu — an often-repeated account worth confirming against a local
source. Both dishes are especially popular with Sabah's non-Muslim communities.

## Butod and the adventurous end of the table

No honest account of Sabahan food skips **butod**, the larva of the sago palm
weevil. The grubs grow in decaying sago (*rumbia*) and sometimes coconut trunks,
feeding on the starch, and are harvested mainly in southwest Sabah around Kuala
Penyu. They have long been a traditional appetiser at weddings and gatherings
among some indigenous communities, and are described as high in protein. Butod are
eaten live — bitten from the head and chewed — or roasted until crisp, or cooked
with onions and dark soy sauce; some Kota Kinabalu restaurants have turned them
into novelties such as "butod sushi." Treated plainly, butod is simply a
long-standing local delicacy that happens to challenge first-time visitors; it is
a genuine part of the cuisine rather than a stunt.

## Fresh, cheap seafood in Kota Kinabalu

For most visitors, the gateway to Sabahan eating is the seafood of the state
capital, Kota Kinabalu. The city's position on nutrient-rich seas means a steady
supply of fresh fish and shellfish, much of it bought by restaurants straight from
local fishermen or kept live in tanks for diners to choose. The waterfront **Kota
Kinabalu Central Market** is the hub, known for daily catches — fish, prawns and
more — alongside fruit, spices and dried seafood, and it is widely regarded as the
best place to find fresh seafood at relatively affordable prices. Around the city,
seafood restaurants cook that catch to order, from butter prawns and chilli crab
to steamed fish, at prices generally lower than in Malaysia's larger mainland
cities — though, as ever, it is worth checking current prices before ordering by
weight.
