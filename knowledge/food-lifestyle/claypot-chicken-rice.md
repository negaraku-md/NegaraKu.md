Lift the lid off a claypot chicken rice and the first thing that reaches you is
steam and soy — glossy rice studded with ruby coins of waxed sausage and pieces of
chicken that have been staining the grains amber as they cook. Then comes the
sound: a faint crackle from the bottom of the pot, where the rice has pressed
against hot clay and turned crisp. For many eaters, that scorched layer is the
whole point.

## What claypot chicken rice is

The dish is exactly what its name says — chicken and rice cooked together inside an
unglazed earthenware pot, and brought to the table in that same pot. Raw rice goes
in with water or stock; on top sit pieces of chicken that have been marinated in
soy sauce, sesame oil and a splash of Chinese rice wine, along with slices of sweet
Chinese waxed sausage, the *lap cheong*. Salted fish and dried shiitake mushrooms
are common additions, and a spoonful of thick, dark soy sauce is drizzled over and
stirred through the moment the lid comes off. It belongs to the broader world of
[Chinese Malaysian cuisine](/food-lifestyle/chinese-malaysian-cuisine), carried
here by Cantonese cooks and settled into the everyday coffee-shop menu.

## How the pot does the work

What makes the dish is the vessel. As the pot heats, the rice at the bottom cooks
hardest, and when the cook stops stirring and lets it sit, the grains against the
clay toast into a crisp, golden crust — the feature the MICHELIN Guide calls the
"prized crisp, golden rice-crust," comparing it to the *socarrat* at the base of a
Spanish paella. In Cantonese that crust is often called *fan jiu*. Meanwhile the
chicken steams on top, its marinade and juices trickling down to flavour every
grain, and the salted fish is usually added near the end to cut the richness with a
salty, savoury edge.

The dish descends from Cantonese claypot rice — *bo zai fan* — eaten in Guangdong
and across the border in Hong Kong, where the same method turns out versions topped
with waxed meats, spare ribs or frog. In Malaysia the chicken version became the
hawker standard, though exactly when it took local root is not well documented.

## Charcoal or gas

Ask around a hawker court and you will quickly hit the dish's long-running debate:
charcoal or gas. The traditional stove is a short charcoal brazier, and the
MICHELIN Guide notes that while most hawkers have moved to gas for speed and
convenience, some cooks keep charcoal because they believe it adds a smoky aroma
that gas cannot match. Others argue charcoal heats the pot more evenly, which helps
build a better crust. Neither side is wrong; it is a matter of what a cook is
chasing. Either way the pots are cooked one at a time, which is why a plate of
claypot chicken rice almost always comes with a wait — part of the ritual, and part
of why it reads as comfort food rather than fast food.

## Where to find it

Claypot chicken rice is a creature of the [kopitiam and the night
market](/food-lifestyle/kopitiam-mamak-culture) — coffee shops, food courts and
roadside stalls where a row of pots sits bubbling over open flames. It is made to
order and shared straight from the pot, often with a plate of greens on the side.
The town of **Kampar**, in Perak, has become especially associated with the dish,
and Kampar-style claypot chicken rice now appears on menus well beyond the town
itself.

One practical note: because the classic recipe leans on pork-based waxed sausage —
and sometimes lard — it is generally not halal. If that matters to you, ask at the
stall. For another Chinese Malaysian rice dish built on very different principles,
see our guide to [Hainanese chicken
rice](/food-lifestyle/hainanese-chicken-rice), where the rice, not the pot,
carries the flavour.
