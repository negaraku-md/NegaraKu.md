Ask a visitor to name a Malaysian drink and they will usually say *teh tarik* or
*kopi*. Those two deserve their fame — and they have their own
[guide to the kopitiam order code](/food-lifestyle/kopi-teh-tarik-and-coffeeshop-drinks).
But stand at a busy coffee-shop counter, a mamak, or a
[night-market](/food-lifestyle/pasar-malam-and-street-food) juice cart and the
real range opens up: a pink milky syrup drink, a cup of black grass jelly, a
frothy soya bean, a mountain of Milo powder, a nutmeg cooler in Penang. This
hub maps that wider world of Malaysian drinks — what they are, and how to order
them.

## The order code, beyond kopi and teh

Most of these drinks ride on the same modular ordering system that governs
coffee and tea, so it is worth knowing before anything else. The base drink
comes a default way, and you stack short markers onto it: **ais** or **peng** for
iced, **kosong** for no sugar, **gao** for strong. "Sirap bandung peng" is just
sirap bandung over ice; "limau ais" is iced lime. The full grammar — including
why *O* means no milk and the debated roots of *C* — lives in the companion
[kopi, teh tarik and coffee-shop drinks](/food-lifestyle/kopi-teh-tarik-and-coffeeshop-drinks)
guide, so this article stays out of that lane and covers everything else on the
board.

One habit worth flagging: many of these drinks are classed in everyday talk as
"cooling" or "heaty", a folk framework borrowed from Chinese and Malay dietary
belief rather than from nutrition science. Treat those labels as cultural
shorthand, not medical advice.

## Sirap bandung and the syrup drinks

The most eye-catching non-coffee drink is **sirap bandung** — a sweet, rose-pink
cooler of rose-flavoured syrup stirred with condensed or evaporated milk, usually
iced and sometimes frothy like teh tarik. Time Out's KL guide describes a version
that "has froth, similar to a teh tarik, and is more rose syrup than condensed
milk." The milk is what turns it opaque pink; without it you have **air sirap**,
plain coloured (often rose or sarsi) syrup let down with water, a staple at
*kenduri* and roadside stalls.

The drink's name is often assumed to come from the Indonesian city of Bandung,
but that link is not settled; other accounts tie *bandung* to a Malay or Persian
sense of "paired" or "mixed", and the true origin is debated — one to leave
hedged rather than stated as fact. A close cousin is **sirap limau**, rose or
plain syrup brightened with a squeeze of fresh lime, which sits halfway between a
syrup drink and the citrus coolers below.

## Citrus coolers: teh o ais limau and air limau

When the day is hot, Malaysians reach for sour-sweet citrus. **Teh o ais limau**
is iced black tea (teh-O, no milk) cut with fresh lime — tart, bracing and far
lighter than milky teh tarik; Time Out notes versions served with a
"generous amount of lime." Drop the tea entirely and you get **air limau** (or
*limau ais*), simply fresh lime, sugar and water over ice, often made with the
small, fragrant *limau kasturi* (calamansi) rather than big lemons. These are the
default thirst-quenchers at mamak and are endlessly adjustable: more lime, less
sugar, extra ice.

## Cincau and air mata kucing: the herbal coolers

Two drinks anchor the "cooling" herbal end of the menu. **Cincau** is grass
jelly — a dark, lightly bitter-sweet jelly set from the *Platostoma* (formerly
*Mesona*) plant, cut into strips or cubes and served in sweetened water or soya
bean, sometimes with longan or in a *cincau* drink of its own. It is the same
jelly that turns up in [cendol](/food-lifestyle/cendol) and shaved-ice desserts.

**Air mata kucing** is the other classic: a chilled, sweet cooler commonly built
on dried **longan**, **luo han guo** (monk fruit) and **winter melon**, simmered
with rock sugar and served cold, as recipe sources such as Nyonya Cooking
describe. The name is a small trap for visitors — *mata kucing* ("cat's eye") is
the Malay name for the longan fruit, so the drink is named after what is in it,
not, as it is sometimes misheard, after "cat's tears". The most famous stall is a
long-running one on Petaling Street in Kuala Lumpur, though recipes and sweetness
vary from vendor to vendor.

## The soy counter: soya bean, tau foo fah and barley

A trio of pale, gently sweet drinks fills the "lighter" slot. **Soya bean milk**
(*air soya*) is fresh-pressed soy milk, served hot or iced and sweetened to
taste; it is often paired with **tau foo fah** (*douhua*), the silky tofu pudding
that doubles as a drink when served soft and loose in sweet syrup. **Barley**
(*air barli*) rounds out the set — pearl barley boiled with water, rock sugar
and often a knot of pandan or a squeeze of lime, drunk hot or cold and also
tagged "cooling" in everyday terms. All three are kopitiam and pasar-malam
mainstays, cheap and unfussy.

## Milo, and the "dinosaur"

Few drinks are as nostalgically Malaysian as **Milo**, the malted chocolate drink
that appears hot, iced (*Milo ais*), or blended. Its most famous local
incarnation is the **Milo dinosaur**: a cold, sweet Milo heaped with a generous
spoonful of extra, undissolved Milo powder on top — Time Out sums it up as
"milky, sweet Milo with a generous amount of Milo powder over it." Variations pile
on more ("Milo godzilla" adds ice cream). Who invented the dinosaur is genuinely
contested between Malaysia and Singapore, and the reporting does not settle it, so
it is fairest to call it a shared drink of the two countries rather than claim it
for one.

## Penang nutmeg, coconut and ABC

Some drinks are tied to a place or a fruit. In Penang, **nutmeg juice** (*air buah
pala*) is a local signature — a sweet-tangy, slightly astringent cooler pressed
from the flesh of the nutmeg fruit, sold at spots like Chowrasta Market and often
offered with an *asam* (sour) adjustment; travel guides link it to Penang's long
history of nutmeg growing, a heritage claim worth treating as background rather
than settled fact. For a full plate of the island's specialities, see
[Penang food](/food-lifestyle/penang-food).

Nationwide, **air kelapa** — fresh coconut water drunk straight from a chilled
young coconut — is the roadside refresher, and the jelly-like flesh is scooped
out afterwards. And blurring the line between drink and dessert is **ABC** (*air
batu campur*, literally "mixed ice") — a mound of shaved ice over red beans,
sweet corn, grass jelly and more, drenched in coloured syrups and condensed milk;
it is the drinkable sibling of the dessert tradition also seen in
[cendol](/food-lifestyle/cendol).

## Lassi and the bubble-tea wave

Malaysia's drinks map keeps widening. From the Indian side comes **lassi**, a
yoghurt drink served sweet (mango is popular) or salted, a mamak and
banana-leaf-restaurant fixture. And from Taiwan, by way of a regional craze,
came **bubble tea** (*boba*) — milk or fruit tea with chewy tapioca pearls — now a
fixture of malls and high streets with its own dense ecosystem of chains and
toppings. Together they show how the country's drink culture keeps absorbing new
arrivals, just as it long since made kopi, teh and sirap its own.
