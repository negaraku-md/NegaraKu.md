Walk through almost any Malaysian [night market](/food-lifestyle/pasar-malam-and-street-food)
in the evening and you will hear it before you see it: the scrape of a spatula
against a hot iron pan, the hiss of batter, and the sweet, toasty smell of
peanuts and margarine. The pancake being folded over at that stall is *apam
balik* — one of the country's most beloved street snacks, and one that comes in
two surprisingly different forms.

## A folded pancake, two different bowls of it

At its simplest, apam balik is a pancake cooked on a round griddle, spread with a
filling, then folded in half (*balik* means roughly "to turn over" or "fold")
and cut into wedges. Beyond that, two quite distinct styles share the name.

The first is the **thick** version: a soft, fluffy, cake-like pancake, pale and
spongy inside, that most people picture when they think of apam balik. The second
is the **thin** version: a wide, lacy round cooked until it is crisp and almost
brittle, then folded around its filling. English-language references note that the
texture "depends on the amount of batter and the pan," ranging from a crumpet-like
form to thin, crisp shells, and that the thin type is usually called *apam balik
nipis* (*nipis* meaning thin). As one home-cook guide puts it, the mark of a good
apam balik is a thick, fluffy middle with thin, crisp edges — which is really the
two styles meeting in a single pancake.

## The batter and the griddle

The batter is a simple one, typically built from flour, eggs, sugar and water,
loosened with coconut milk and lifted by a raising agent. Traditional recipes used
yeast; many modern ones use baking soda or bicarbonate of soda instead, which is
part of why textures vary so much from stall to stall.

What gives apam balik its character is the pan. The batter is poured onto a thick,
round iron griddle greased generously with palm margarine so it does not stick,
then cooked until the surface sets and bubbles. For the thick style the pan is
usually covered so the pancake puffs up and stays soft; for the thin style the cook
swirls a much smaller amount of batter out toward the edges to form a broad, thin
layer that crisps as it cooks and firms up further as it cools. The finished
pancake is spread with filling, folded, and sliced to order.

## Peanuts, sugar and sweetcorn: the fillings

The defining filling is crushed roasted **peanuts** mixed with **sugar**, scattered
over the hot pancake with a smear of margarine or butter so the sugar half-melts
into the surface. Very often a spoonful of creamed **sweetcorn** goes on as well —
so common a pairing that for many Malaysians corn is simply part of what apam balik
is. The combination of warm, buttery pancake, sweet-salty ground peanuts and soft
corn is the snack's signature.

From that base, stalls improvise freely. Modern fillings run to chocolate
sprinkles, grated cheese, kaya (coconut-egg jam), condensed milk and chocolate-hazelnut
spread, sometimes several at once. These newer versions sit alongside, rather than
replace, the classic peanut-sugar-corn, which remains the benchmark.

## The thin crisp: ban jian kueh and its names

The thin, crispy style has a life of its own under a cluster of Chinese-dialect
names. It is widely sold as **ban jian kueh** (also written *ban chang kuih*) and,
in the Teochew-influenced tradition shared with Singapore, as **min chiang kueh**
(or *mee chiang kueh*). Reference listings group these with apam balik as names for
the same broad dish, though usage on the ground is inconsistent and the thin crisp
is sometimes treated as a related-but-separate snack. Whatever it is called, it is
prized for the shatter of its lacy edges against the soft, peanut-filled centre — a
texture that recipe writers note only fully develops as the pancake cools and
hardens.

## Where you find it

Apam balik belongs above all to the **pasar malam** — the rotating weekly night
markets that are a fixture of Malaysian life — and to roadside and food-court stalls
where a vendor works a row of pans in the open air. It is an everyday snack rather
than a restaurant dish: cheap, handheld, bought warm in a paper sleeve or plastic
bag and eaten on the spot. Some stalls become local institutions in their own right;
Malay Mail, for instance, has profiled an Ipoh-famous apam balik stall that drew a
following after relocating to the Klang Valley. It sits comfortably within the wider
world of Malaysian [kuih-muih](/food-lifestyle/kuih-muih) and sweet
[desserts and snacks](/food-lifestyle/malaysian-desserts), though it is eaten at all
hours rather than only at a set time.

## A name, and a history still argued over

For all its familiarity, apam balik's origins are genuinely contested, and it is
worth saying so plainly. The dish is most often traced to **Fujian / Hokkien**
cooking in southern China, from where Hokkien and Teochew migrants are thought to
have carried it across Southeast Asia. One frequently repeated story attributes its
invention to the Qing-dynasty general Zuo Zongtang (Tso Tsung-tang) around 1855, as
a quick food for his soldiers — but even reference works that mention this flag that
the claim is made "without evidence," and it should be treated as folklore rather
than settled fact.

The name itself points in two directions at once: *apam* echoes the South Indian
*appam* pancake, while the dish's structure and many of its aliases are Chinese.
Those aliases are legion. In Indonesia it is usually **martabak manis** ("sweet
*martabak*") or **terang bulan** ("moonlight"); it also travels as *hok lo pan*,
*kuih haji*, *apom balek* and more, with further names in Hong Kong, Taiwan and
southern Thailand. English-language references commonly file apam balik and martabak
manis together as one dish, while noting regional differences in thickness, batter
and toppings. The honest summary is that apam balik is one snack with many names and
a disputed lineage — a reminder that much of Malaysia's street food carries a shared
regional history that no single country fully owns.
