The gravy is a thick, concentrated sauce of ground fish — commonly wolf herring (ikan parang) or mackerel — enriched with coconut milk, toasted coconut paste (kerisik) and a spice rempah, soured a little with tamarind. Pour a laksa gravy over spaghetti and you have either a mistake or a state's
proudest dish. In Johor, it is firmly the latter. **Laksa Johor** takes the
southern Malay version of laksa — a thick, concentrated fish gravy — and ladles
it not over the usual rice noodles but over cooked **spaghetti**, the one swap
that sets it apart from every other bowl in Malaysia's sprawling
[laksa family](/food-lifestyle/laksa).

## What makes it laksa Johor

The noodle is the signpost, but the gravy is the dish. Where Penang's asam laksa
is thin and sour, laksa Johor's sauce is dense and rich. Fish — wolf herring
(*ikan parang*) and mackerel are the names heard most often — is boiled, then the
flesh is pounded or blended until fine and stirred back into its own stock, so the
gravy is thickened with the fish itself rather than with flour. Coconut milk and
**kerisik** (toasted, pounded coconut) add weight and a nutty depth, a spice
*rempah* carries the heat and fragrance, and a little tamarind keeps it from
turning cloying. The result is thick enough to coat each strand of pasta.

On top goes the cool counterpoint: a generous heap of raw **ulam** and vegetables
— shredded cucumber, bean sprouts, sliced red onion, and herbs such as *daun
kesum* (laksa leaf) and finely sliced torch ginger (*bunga kantan*) — finished
with a spoon of sambal and a squeeze of lime. Cooks and eaters alike tend to treat
the raw herbs as the point, not a garnish: they cut the richness and give every
mouthful a fresh, bright edge.

## Eaten by hand

One detail marks laksa Johor as much as the pasta does. Because the gravy is so
thick, it clings to the spaghetti in a way a fork struggles with, and Johoreans
traditionally eat the dish with their fingers rather than with a spoon or fork —
mixing the gravy, pasta and ulam together by hand before each bite. It is a small
ritual that locals often mention first when describing how the dish should be
enjoyed.

As a Malay dish of fish, coconut and spice with no pork, laksa Johor belongs
comfortably to the halal mainstream of
[Malay cuisine](/food-lifestyle/malay-cuisine), though, as with any street
food, preparation varies from one cook to the next.

## The spaghetti and the palace

Why spaghetti at all? The explanation most often repeated is a royal one. By
popular account, **Sultan Abu Bakar of Johor** developed a taste for Italian pasta
while travelling in Europe in the late 19th century and had his kitchen pour the
traditional fish gravy over spaghetti in place of rice noodles; the habit is said
to have spread from the palace into Johor homes and stalls, until the rice-noodle
version all but vanished there. A plainer theory also circulates — that dried
wheat pasta simply kept well and was easier to prepare.

Both are worth holding lightly. The royal-origin story is charming and widely
told, but it is **commonly-repeated history rather than firmly documented fact**,
and the finer details — the sultan's exact reign, the year of the trip — differ
between retellings. What is not in doubt is the dish's standing today: laksa Johor
is treated as one of the state's signature foods and has been recognised among
Malaysia's intangible culinary heritage, though sources disagree on the exact year
that recognition was gazetted.

## How it sits in the laksa family

Laksa Johor is the clearest proof of how far one idea can travel. The through-line
across Malaysia's laksas — fish, a pounded spice paste, noodles and fresh herbs —
is all there; only the noodle has changed nationality. Set it beside the sour
northern bowls or Sarawak's sambal-belacan version and the contrast is the whole
story of the dish: the same southern Malay larder, a borrowed Italian pasta, and a
gravy thick enough to be eaten by hand.
