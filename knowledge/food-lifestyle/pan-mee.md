Walk into a coffee shop in Kuala Lumpur, Seremban or Ipoh and you will find a
stall shaping dough by hand — tearing it into ragged scraps, pinching it into
flat tongues, or feeding it through a little machine into strips. The pieces go
straight into a pot of anchovy broth and come back as a bowl of pan mee: plain,
cheap, deeply comforting and, by most accounts, Hakka to the core.

## Hakka roots and a contested name

Pan mee is one of the Chinese-Malaysian noodle dishes most closely tied to a
single dialect group. Sources credit the [Hakka](/food-lifestyle/chinese-malaysian-cuisine)
community — who migrated to Malaysia generations ago — with creating it, and it
remains most common in the central and southern parts of the peninsula where
most Hakka people settled.

The name itself is less settled than it looks. Pan mee is the usual Malaysian
spelling of the Chinese 板面 (*bǎnmiàn*), which reference and food sources gloss
variously as "board noodle," "block noodle" or simply "flat flour noodles." By a
widely repeated account the "board" sense comes from an old Hakka habit of
cutting the dough into straight strands against a wooden block used as a ruler.
That is a reasonable story, but the precise etymology is not firmly documented
and writers translate the name loosely, so it is best treated as plausible
rather than proven.

Pan mee also overlaps with another name, **mee hoon kueh** ("wheat snack" by one
rendering). The two are closely related and are often used for the same bowl;
where they differ, "mee hoon kueh" tends to point at the hand-torn or pinched
pieces while "pan mee" is used more broadly. The labels shift by dialect, region
and stall rather than following one fixed rule.

## The noodles: torn, pinched or cut

What unites every bowl is a simple wheat dough — flour, water, egg, a little
salt — and what varies is how it is shaped. Three forms dominate:

- **Hand-torn** (*mee hoon kueh*): the dough is rolled into a sheet and torn by
  hand into bite-sized, irregular scraps roughly a couple of centimetres across.
- **Finger-pinched**: cooks pinch off thin, flat tongues of dough, giving a
  springier, more even piece than tearing.
- **Knife-cut or machine-cut**: the dough is rolled and sliced into noodles with
  a knife, or passed through a pasta-style machine into flat strips — the most
  common shape at busy stalls.

The hand-shaped versions are chewier and more uneven; the machine strips are
faster and more uniform. Some stalls even offer "three-colour" noodles tinted
with vegetable juices. The variety is why pan mee sits comfortably within
Malaysia's wider family of wheat and rice [noodle dishes](/food-lifestyle/malaysian-noodles).

## The anchovy broth and its toppings

The heart of a traditional bowl is its broth, and the broth is built on *ikan
bilis* — dried anchovies. In the simplest home style the anchovies are simmered
in water (one recipe uses roughly a one-to-ten ratio of anchovies to water,
boiled for about fifteen minutes) and seasoned with salt and white pepper; many
cooks deepen it further by simmering the anchovies with pork bones for an hour or
more. The result is a clear, savoury, unmistakably anchovy-forward soup.

Over the noodles go a familiar set of toppings: fried minced pork (often
stir-fried with mushroom and garlic), a scatter of crisp deep-fried anchovies,
leafy greens — most often *sayur manis* (sweet-leaf, also called *cekur manis* or
*mani cai*), with sweet potato leaves, choy sum or spinach as stand-ins — and a
poached egg slipped into the hot broth until the white just sets. Dried mushroom
is standard, though sources describe shiitake more often than the wood-ear
mushroom some bowls use, so the exact mushroom varies by stall. Fried shallots
and spring onions finish it off.

## Soup or dry

Pan mee comes two ways, and regulars have firm preferences. The **soup** version
is the one described above: noodles and broth together in one bowl, warm and
mild. The **dry** version keeps the broth on the side and tosses the noodles
instead with a seasoning — dark soy sauce, XO sauce or sambal are all used — so
that the toppings and chilli, rather than the stock, carry the flavour. In many
shops the dry noodles and the soup are served separately, letting you sip the
broth between mouthfuls. Neither is more "correct"; they are two settings of the
same dish.

## Chilli pan mee: the dry Kuala Lumpur bowl

The most famous dry style has become a dish in its own right. **Chilli pan mee**
piles the noodles with minced pork (or minced chicken), fried anchovies, fried
onions and a poached egg, then arms the eater with a jar of dried-chilli
flakes — pounded dried chillies, sometimes with a fresh chilli and more crisp
anchovies worked in — to add fire to taste. The egg is usually stirred through so
its yolk coats the noodles before the chilli goes on.

Chilli pan mee is strongly associated with Kuala Lumpur and the surrounding Klang
Valley. Wikipedia places its origin in the Chow Kit area of the city; in food
media and among diners the dish is often attributed specifically to Restoran Kin
Kin, long treated as its standard-bearer. That precise attribution is not firmly
documented, so the safest statement is that chilli pan mee emerged in central
Kuala Lumpur and was popularised there.

## A Hakka dish on the move

Pan mee shows how a plain migrant-kitchen staple spreads and mutates. Beyond the
anchovy-broth standard, stalls now offer curry broth, chilli broths, versions
with pork belly or prawns, and the three-colour noodles mentioned earlier. The
same dough-and-anchovy idea travels under related names across the region, with
Hokkien-speaking areas — including Singapore, by popular account — leaning on the
label *mee hoon kueh* rather than *pan mee*; sources disagree on how neatly the
names divide. Either way, the dish belongs to the broader story of Malaysia's
[regional and ethnic cuisines](/food-lifestyle/regional-and-ethnic-cuisines),
where a single community's home cooking becomes something the whole country lines
up for.
