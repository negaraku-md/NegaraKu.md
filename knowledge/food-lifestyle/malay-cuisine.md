Sit down to a Malay meal and the shape of it is clear before you taste a thing: a
mound of rice in the middle, a cluster of small dishes around it, and a saucer of
*sambal* within reach. Malay cuisine — *masakan Melayu* — is the cooking of
Malaysia's Malay community and one of the main threads running through the
country's [national dishes](/food-lifestyle/national-dishes-of-malaysia). It is
built less on single plated mains than on a spice paste, a pot of coconut gravy,
and the habit of eating rice with many things at once.

## Rice at the heart of the meal

Rice is the anchor. A Malay meal is organised around *nasi* (cooked rice) eaten
with *lauk* — an array of side dishes of meat, fish, egg and vegetables shared
from the centre of the table. As *The Star* puts it, "It is hard to contemplate a
Malay meal today without the presence of rice." That centrality may be more
recent than it feels: citing food historian Khir Johari, the same report notes
that before roughly the 15th century sago was the main staple and rice "a limited
commodity consumed by those wealthy enough to afford it" — a claim worth treating
as a historian's reconstruction rather than settled fact.

Rice also carries some of the cuisine's most recognisable dishes.
[Nasi lemak](/food-lifestyle/nasi-lemak) — rice cooked in coconut milk and pandan,
served with fried anchovies, peanuts, cucumber, egg and sambal — is eaten across
the country, often at breakfast. The east coast adds its own rice plates:
[nasi kerabu](/food-lifestyle/nasi-kerabu), tinted blue with butterfly-pea flower
and heaped with herbs, and nasi dagang, a steamed rice-and-glutinous-rice dish
served with fish curry. In December 2024, UNESCO inscribed "Malaysian Breakfast
Culture" on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of
Humanity — the country's first food-related entry — though that recognition is
explicitly of the whole multi-ethnic breakfast table, not of Malay cooking alone.

## Rempah and santan: the flavour foundation

If rice is the structure, the flavour comes from two things above all: *rempah*
and *santan*. Rempah is a pounded spice paste — the backbone of most Malay
dishes. Depending on what is being cooked, it blends aromatics such as shallots,
garlic, dried and fresh chillies, lemongrass, galangal, ginger and fresh
turmeric, frequently with a knob of *belacan* (fermented shrimp paste). Singapore's
heritage body, describing the shared Malay larder of the region, lists belacan,
*santan* (coconut milk) and *asam jawa* (tamarind) among the defining
ingredients.

The paste is traditionally ground on a stone mortar (*batu giling*) or pounded in
a *lesung*, then fried slowly in oil — a step called *menumis* — until it darkens
and turns fragrant and the raw edge cooks off. Coconut milk is the second pillar:
*santan* gives *gulai* and *masak lemak* their richness and body, and lacing rice
with it is what makes nasi lemak *lemak* (rich). Tamarind or *asam gelugor*
supplies the sour note that balances the fat and chilli. Get the rempah and the
santan right and much of the dish is already decided.

## The signature dishes

From that foundation comes a long roster of dishes. The most famous abroad is
**rendang**, a dry, slow-cooked beef (or sometimes chicken) braise in which coconut
milk, rempah and *kerisik* (toasted grated coconut) are reduced over hours until
the meat is dark and almost caramelised; it is covered in its own
[rendang](/food-lifestyle/rendang) article. Its origins lie in the Minangkabau
cooking of West Sumatra and it is claimed by both Malaysia and Indonesia, so it is
best described as a shared regional dish rather than the property of one country.

Other mainstays include:

- **[Satay](/food-lifestyle/satay)** — skewered, marinated meat grilled over
  charcoal and served with a thick peanut sauce.
- **Gulai** — a spiced coconut-milk curry, looser than rendang, made with beef,
  chicken, fish or vegetables.
- **Masak lemak** — a rich coconut gravy, often turmeric-yellow, as in the Negeri
  Sembilan speciality *masak lemak cili api*.
- **Asam pedas** — a hot-and-sour tamarind gravy, usually with fish such as
  mackerel or stingray.
- **Ayam percik** — an east-coast grilled chicken basted with a spiced coconut
  sauce.
- **Ikan bakar** — fish grilled over charcoal, commonly wrapped in banana leaf
  with a sambal or spice rub.

None of these stands alone on a plate in the Western sense; each is a *lauk* to be
spooned over rice alongside others.

## Ulam, sambal and the fermented larder

Against all that richness, Malay meals set fresh and pungent counterpoints.
**Ulam** is a tradition of eating raw or lightly blanched herbs, shoots and
vegetables — *ulam raja*, *pegaga* (pennywort), *petai* (stink bean), young mango,
cucumber and more — usually dipped into sambal. It brings crunch, bitterness and a
cooling edge to the table, and is often praised for its healthfulness.

**[Sambal](/food-lifestyle/sambal)** — a chilli relish in countless forms, from
raw *sambal belacan* pounded with shrimp paste and lime to cooked *sambal tumis* —
is almost obligatory. Alongside it sits a distinctive fermented larder: **budu**, a
dark anchovy sauce from Kelantan and Terengganu; **cencaluk**, a tangy preserve of
tiny shrimp associated with Melaka; **belacan**, the shrimp paste woven into
rempah and sambal; and **tempoyak**, fermented durian used as a condiment and in
gravies. These fermented notes give Malay food much of its depth, and vary sharply
from state to state.

## How Malay food is cooked

The techniques are as defining as the ingredients. *Menumis* (frying the rempah)
opens most dishes. From there, cooks lean on slow braising in coconut milk
(*gulai*, *masak lemak*), long reduction (*rendang*), grilling over charcoal
(*bakar*, *panggang*) for ikan bakar, satay and ayam percik, deep- or shallow-
frying (*goreng*), and boiling or simmering (*rebus*). *Kerisik* — grated coconut
dry-toasted and pounded to a paste — is a characteristic enricher. Banana leaf is
used both as a wrapper for grilling and, in some settings, as a plate. The common
thread is patience with aromatics: the flavour is coaxed out of the rempah first,
not added at the end.

## Halal as the default

Malay cuisine is halal by default. Malays are constitutionally defined as Muslim in
Malaysia, and the cooking reflects Islamic dietary rules as a matter of course
rather than as a special category: no pork, no alcohol, and meat slaughtered
according to Islamic practice. This shapes the whole cuisine — the fats are coconut
and vegetable oil rather than lard, and beef, chicken, mutton, fish and seafood
carry the protein. (Whether a given eatery holds formal halal certification is a
separate, administrative question.)

## A regional map: east coast, west and south

Malay cooking is not uniform across the country, and the differences map onto
geography. The **east coast** — Kelantan and Terengganu — is widely described as
the sweetest, fond of coconut milk, sugar and palm sugar, and home to nasi kerabu,
nasi dagang, ayam percik and budu. The **west coast and central** peninsula,
around the old trading ports, shades into the Peranakan and wider Malaysian
mainstream and tends to be more chilli-forward. The **south**, Johor, is known for
coconut-rich gravies and dishes such as its own laksa. These are generalisations
rather than firm boundaries, and they sit within Malaysia's broader patchwork of
[regional and ethnic cuisines](/food-lifestyle/regional-and-ethnic-cuisines).

## Kenduri and festive food

Malay food is also communal food. The *kenduri* — a feast marking a wedding,
circumcision, religious occasion or Hari Raya — is where the cuisine shows its
fullest form: big pots of rendang and gulai, *nasi minyak* or *nasi briyani*,
satay grilling in the open, *lemang* (glutinous rice roasted in bamboo) and
*ketupat* (rice cooked in woven palm-leaf pouches) served with the richer dishes.
These dishes recur across the calendar and sit at the centre of
[festive foods across Malaysian festivals](/food-lifestyle/festive-foods-across-malaysian-festivals),
where Hari Raya in particular turns the home kitchen into a feast for streams of
visiting guests.
