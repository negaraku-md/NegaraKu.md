On a hot afternoon at a Malaysian coffee shop, one of the cheapest ways to cool
down is a tall glass of something dark and slippery: cubes of near-black jelly
bobbing in sweet iced water, slurped up through a fat straw. This is *cincau* —
grass jelly — and despite the grassy name and the faintly medicinal look, it is
one of the country's best-loved everyday refreshers.

## What cincau is

Cincau is a soft herbal jelly. The common black version is made by boiling the
dried stems and leaves of the **Mesona** plant — a member of the mint family —
until the water turns dark and faintly bitter, then straining it and letting it
cool and set, sometimes with a little starch to firm the gel. The result is cut
into cubes and served cold. On its own the jelly tastes mild, herbal and lightly
bitter; most of the pleasure is the cool, wobbly, easy-to-slurp texture rather
than a bold flavour, which is why it is almost always paired with something sweet.

For where cincau sits among the country's wider drinks board — sirap bandung,
air mata kucing, Milo and the rest — see the
[Malaysian drinks](/food-lifestyle/malaysian-drinks) hub. This article is the
deep dive into the grass jelly itself.

## Black cincau and green cincau

Not all cincau is the same plant. The dark, almost black jelly that most
Malaysians mean by "cincau" is **cincau hitam** (black grass jelly), set from
Mesona. There is also a **cincau hijau** (green grass jelly), made from the
leaves of a different plant, *Cyclea barbata* — softer, greener and more strongly
associated with Indonesia. Both are plant-based herbal jellies, but they come
from different plants and are prepared differently, so they are best treated as
cousins rather than the same thing.

## A "cooling" drink

In Chinese food tradition, grass jelly is classed as a **"cooling"** food — the
kind you reach for when the body is "heaty" from hot weather, fried food or lack
of sleep. That idea belongs to traditional dietary belief rather than proven
medicine, and it is best described as part of the culture around the drink, not a
health claim. What is not in dispute is that a glass of iced cincau is genuinely
refreshing in the tropical heat, which is reason enough for its popularity.

## How it is ordered

Cincau turns up in several guises, and knowing the names helps:

| You say | You get |
| --- | --- |
| air cincau / cincau | Grass jelly cubes in sweet iced water |
| soya cincau | Soya-bean milk poured over black grass jelly |
| "Michael Jackson" | The nickname for soya cincau, for its black-and-white layers |
| cincau + cendol / ABC | Grass jelly as a topping in those iced desserts |

The **soya cincau** — soy milk over black jelly — is the famous one, its two-tone
black-and-white look earning the affectionate "Michael Jackson" nickname at
hawker stalls. Grass jelly is also a standard component of
[cendol](/food-lifestyle/cendol), ais kacang (ABC) and the mixed-ingredient
*leng chi kang*, and it pairs naturally with the kopitiam's other soy drink —
see [soya bean](/food-lifestyle/soya-bean).

## Where to find it

Cincau is everywhere cheap and cold drinks are sold: kopitiam, hawker centres,
dessert stalls and *pasar malam*. For the hot, sit-down version of the
coffee-shop world it belongs to, see
[kopitiam and mamak culture](/food-lifestyle/kopitiam-mamak-culture). It is also
one of the most common ready-made drinks on the shelf — the canned and carton
grass-jelly drinks (Yeo's among them) are sold in every supermarket and provision
shop, which is how many people first meet it.
