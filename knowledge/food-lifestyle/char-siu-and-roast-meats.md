Push open the glass of a Chinese roast-meat shop and the display tells you
everything: glossy slabs of barbecued pork hung on hooks, a lacquered duck or
two, a tray of pork belly with a blistered, pebbled crust, and a cook with a
heavy cleaver chopping to order on a thick wooden block. This is *siu mei* —
the Cantonese family of roasted meats — and it is one of the most recognisable
fixtures of Malaysia's Chinese food landscape.

## Siu mei: the roast-meat tradition

*Siu mei* (燒味) is, in essence, the Cantonese rotisserie style of cooking —
meats seasoned, hung and roasted over fire or in tall ovens until the surface
crisps and glazes. Unlike most other Cantonese dishes, the category "solely
consists of meat, with no vegetables," as Wikipedia's survey of Cantonese
cuisine puts it, and it sits under the broader umbrella term *siu laap* that also
takes in cured and braised meats. The headline members are char siu (barbecued
pork), *siu yoke* (roast pork), roast duck and roast goose.

In Malaysia the tradition travelled with Cantonese and other southern Chinese
migrants and settled into a distinct institution: the neighbourhood roast-meat
shop, usually a small shopfront or a corner stall inside a
[kopitiam](/food-lifestyle/kopitiam-mamak-culture). It is a core strand of
[Chinese Malaysian cuisine](/food-lifestyle/chinese-malaysian-cuisine), and the
meats below are its mainstays.

## Char siu: honey-glazed barbecued pork

**Char siu** (叉燒; also spelled *char siew* locally) is marinated barbecued
pork, usually made from a boneless cut such as collar or shoulder. The marinade
typically blends sweet and savoury notes — hoisin and soy sauces, Chinese rice
wine, sugar and five-spice are common — and the meat is glazed as it roasts so
the outside turns sticky and caramelised, with charred edges that give the dish
its appeal. Maltose or honey in the glaze is what produces char siu's
characteristic sheen and high surface sweetness.

The name is generally explained as "fork-roasted," after the skewers or forks on
which the pork was once suspended over the fire, though how literally that still
describes oven-roasted versions is better left to a culinary-history source (see
*Verification needed*). Char siu's familiar reddish tinge is also contested: some
of the colour comes from the marinade and caramelisation, but the bright red
often owes something to added food colouring rather than to the meat itself.
Malaysian food writing dwells on texture and balance as much as colour — a 2024
Malay Mail review praised one Kuala Lumpur stall's "crystal char siu" for a
glassy, tender finish, while reviewers reaching for a distinct "KL-style" char
siu describe it as fattier and more heavily caramelised than elsewhere. That
regional claim comes from restaurant reviews rather than a settled culinary
authority, so treat it as a popular characterisation.

## Siu yoke: crispy roast pork belly

If char siu is the sweet, glazed member of the family, **siu yoke** (燒肉; widely
spelled *siew yoke* in Malaysia) is its salty, crunchy counterpart. It is roast
pork — in practice usually pork belly — seasoned far more simply, often with
little more than salt, and roasted hard so the skin blisters into a crackling,
pebbled crust. Good roast pork is judged on that contrast: "crisp skin and juicy
and tender meat," in the words of the English-language description of *siu yuk*,
over alternating layers of fat and flesh. It is normally served plain with the
skin on, sometimes with a dab of mustard or a little sauce on the side. Malaysian
reviews single out "ultra-crispy" skin as the mark of a good plate.

## Roast duck and roast chicken

Beyond pork, the roast-meat shop usually keeps poultry on the hook. **Roast duck**
(*siu ngap*, 燒鴨) is the most common — a whole bird marinated, air-dried and
roasted so the skin turns burnished and the meat stays moist — and the larger,
richer **roast goose** (*siu ngo*, 燒鵝) appears at more specialised shops.
Cantonese roast duck should not be confused with Peking duck, which is a separate
northern preparation; the siu mei version is chopped, bone-in, and served as part
of a rice or noodle meal rather than wrapped in pancakes.

**Roast chicken** and smooth poached ("white-cut") chicken round out the counter
at many shops, which is why roast-meat stalls and chicken-rice shops so often
overlap; the Sin Yuan shop profiled by Malay Mail, for instance, pairs its siew
yoke and char siu with poached chicken. That overlap connects siu mei to the
wider world of Malaysian poultry-and-rice cooking, including
[Hainanese chicken rice](/food-lifestyle/hainanese-chicken-rice).

## At the roast-meat shop: chopped to order

The defining experience of siu mei is how it is served. You order by the meat and
by how much — a portion of char siu, a strip of siu yoke, a quarter duck — and the
cook lifts it off the hook and chops it to order on a wooden block, bones and all,
into bite-sized pieces arranged on the plate. From there the common formats are:

- **Over rice** — the everyday order, *char siu fan* (char siu rice): meat laid
  over steamed white rice with a spoonful of light, sweetish sauce and a few
  blanched greens. A **mixed rice** (*siu mei fan*) combines two or three meats on
  one plate.
- **With noodles** — char siu and roast meats are a classic topping for
  [wonton noodles](/food-lifestyle/wonton-mee), where glossy slices sit beside the
  dumplings over springy egg noodles.
- **By weight, to take away** — a portion chopped and boxed to carry home, often
  for a family meal.

A siu mei meal, as the tradition is summed up, "comprises meat atop plain white
rice or noodles," frequently with a side of greens such as *choy sum* or *kai
lan*.

## The pork context

Two of the three headline meats — char siu and siu yoke — are pork, which shapes
where siu mei belongs on Malaysia's food map. These dishes are a Chinese-community
food, sold at Chinese-run roast-meat shops and inside non-halal sections of
kopitiam; they are not found at Malay or mamak outlets, and a traditional
roast-meat stall is not halal and is not certified as such. Even the duck and
chicken on the same hooks are not halal when prepared and sold on non-halal
premises. In a multi-religious country where the majority of the population is
Muslim and does not eat pork, this is simply useful orientation rather than a
value judgement — part of the broader patchwork of
[regional and ethnic cuisines](/food-lifestyle/regional-and-ethnic-cuisines) that
different communities keep.

## Char siu beyond the plate

Char siu earns a second life as an ingredient. Diced or sliced, it is the filling
of *char siu bao* — the soft steamed or baked buns found at dim sum and in
bakeries — and it flavours fried rice, turns up folded into *chee cheong fun*
(rice-noodle rolls), and tops bowls of noodle soup. This versatility is part of
why char siu, of all the roast meats, is the one most Malaysians encounter even
without visiting a roast-meat shop: a sweet, savoury, faintly smoky thread that
runs through Chinese-Malaysian cooking from the hawker stall to the bakery shelf.
