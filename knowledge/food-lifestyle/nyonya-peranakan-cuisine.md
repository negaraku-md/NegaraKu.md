Spoon into a bowl of Nyonya cooking and you taste two worlds at once: the
fermented, soy-and-pork depth of a Chinese kitchen, carried on the lemongrass,
belacan and coconut of a Malay one. For roughly five centuries the Straits-born
Chinese of the old port cities cooked exactly this way — not as fusion for its
own sake, but because their households sat astride both cultures. The result is
one of Malaysia's most distinctive culinary traditions, and one of its most
labour-intensive.

## A cuisine of two kitchens

Nyonya — or Peranakan — cuisine is the home cooking of the Baba-Nyonya, the
descendants of early Chinese migrants who settled in the Malay-speaking ports
and, over generations, adopted much of the local language, dress and cooking
while keeping Chinese religious and family customs. *Peranakan* is a Malay word
meaning, roughly, "locally born"; the women of the community are called *Nyonya*
and the men *Baba*, and it is the women's kitchen that gave the food its name.
The two terms, Nyonya and Peranakan, are used almost interchangeably for the
cuisine in Malaysia.

What makes the cooking coherent is its method rather than any single dish: Malay
and wider Nusantara techniques — pounding a spice paste, souring with tamarind,
enriching with coconut milk — applied to a larder that still reaches for Chinese
staples such as fermented soy beans, pork and wheat or rice noodles. Scholarship
on the cuisine frames it as a product of assimilation and hybridisation, the
Peranakans adapting Chinese foodways to local ingredients and taste over a long
period. It sits alongside the country's other community kitchens on the wider map
of [regional and ethnic cuisines](/food-lifestyle/regional-and-ethnic-cuisines).

## Who the Peranakans are

The community is rooted in three Straits Settlement ports — Melaka, Penang and
Singapore — where Chinese traders settled and intermarried with local women, and
where the heritage remains most firmly established today. By the common account,
the lineage reaches back to the era of the Melaka Sultanate, with Chinese
settlement often dated to around the 15th century and tied to legends such as
that of Princess Hang Li Po; these origin stories are widely repeated but
historically contested, and are better treated as tradition than as settled fact
(see *Verification needed*).

Later, during British colonial rule, some Peranakan families moved from Melaka to
form enclaves in Penang and Singapore, which is why the three cities share a
cuisine yet cook it differently. The food is bound up with a whole material
culture — porcelain, beadwork, embroidered *kebaya* and ornate shophouses — that
visitors can still see in the Baba-Nyonya heritage houses of Melaka and George
Town. Today the tradition is prominent enough that the MICHELIN Guide's Malaysia
selection features a cluster of Peranakan restaurants, with Penang's Nyonya
kitchens especially praised by its inspectors.

## What defines the cooking

If there is a single technique at the centre of Nyonya food, it is the **rempah**:
a spice paste of aromatics pounded together in a stone mortar and then fried
patiently in oil until the raw edge cooks off and the kitchen fills with its
scent. A typical rempah draws on chillies, shallots, garlic, lemongrass, galangal,
turmeric, candlenut (*buah keras*) and **belacan**, the dense fermented shrimp
paste that gives so many dishes their savoury base note. The pounding and the slow
frying are the unglamorous heart of the cuisine — a well-made rempah is what
separates a proper Nyonya dish from an approximation.

Around that core sit a set of recurring flavours: **tamarind** (*asam*) and, in
the north, lime for sourness; **coconut milk** (*santan*) for richness; **pandan**
leaf and the deep-blue **blue pea flower** (*bunga telang*) for fragrance and
natural colour, most visibly in the kuih; and *cincalok*, a condiment of fermented
tiny shrimp, served as a tart, salty dip. Because the community is Chinese in
descent, the pantry also keeps distinctly Chinese ingredients — fermented soy-bean
paste (*taucu*), preserved vegetables and, in many households, pork — which sets
Nyonya food apart from the mostly halal Malay kitchen it otherwise resembles.

## The signature savoury dishes

The savoury repertoire is where the two-kitchen character shows most clearly.
Among the best-known dishes:

- **Ayam / babi pongteh** — a homely braise of chicken (*ayam*) or pork (*babi*)
  cooked down with fermented soy-bean paste and a little palm sugar into a thick,
  savoury-sweet gravy, usually with potatoes and mushrooms. The soy-bean base is
  unmistakably Chinese; the result is a weeknight Nyonya staple.
- **Kari kapitan** — a Nyonya chicken curry, milder and more aromatic than an
  Indian one, built on a rempah with candlenut, lemongrass and coconut milk, often
  finished with kaffir lime. Its curious name is the subject of an oft-told but
  unverified origin story (see *Verification needed*).
- **Assam fish** (*ikan masak asam*) — fish simmered in a sharp, tamarind-soured
  gravy, a dish that shows off the sour register Penang Nyonya cooks favour.
- **Ayam buah keluak** — chicken (or pork ribs) braised with the dark, pungent
  nut of the *kepayang* tree, whose kernel is laboriously prepared to make it safe
  and edible. It is one of the cuisine's most prized and painstaking dishes.
- **Otak-otak** — spiced fish paste bound with coconut and herbs, wrapped in
  banana leaf and grilled or steamed.
- **Itik tim** — a comforting soup of duck simmered with salted preserved
  vegetables, sour and savoury at once.
- **Perut ikan** — a Penang Nyonya speciality of fish stomach cooked with a herb
  and spice paste, firmly in the sour-and-spicy camp.
- **Kueh pie tee** — crisp, cup-shaped pastry shells (sometimes called "top hats")
  filled with a sweet-savoury mix of shredded turnip, prawns and garnishes; a
  standard at celebrations.
- **Inchi kabin** — Penang-style spiced fried chicken, marinated and deep-fried;
  whether it is strictly Nyonya or of mixed origin is debated (see *Verification
  needed*).

Several of these — babi pongteh and ayam buah keluak among them — are commonly
made with pork, which is simply part of the community's Chinese heritage and is
noted here for diners who avoid it. The Peranakan table also has its own branch of
the national noodle-soup family: a coconut-rich Nyonya [laksa](/food-lifestyle/laksa)
in the south and the sour Penang assam laksa in the north, both of which the laksa
guide traces back to Peranakan kitchens.

## Nyonya kuih: the sweet counterpart

No account of the cuisine is complete without **Nyonya kuih** — the bite-sized
cakes, often steamed and brightly coloured, that are the community's pride. Many
are built on glutinous rice, coconut and palm sugar (*gula melaka*), tinted and
scented with pandan and blue pea flower, and finished with grated coconut.
Familiar examples include *kuih lapis* (steamed rainbow layers), *kuih talam*
(a two-tone pandan-and-coconut set), *ondeh-ondeh* (glutinous-rice balls filled
with molten palm sugar and rolled in coconut) and *angku kuih* (a moulded, filled
"red tortoise" cake used at celebrations). The kuih are so central that Penang's
specialist makers of handmade Nyonya *koay* are singled out in restaurant guides.
Alongside them sits the wider Malaysian dessert canon, including iced sweets such
as [cendol](/food-lifestyle/cendol) that the Peranakan sweet tooth shares with the
rest of the country.

## The tok panjang feast

For weddings, birthdays and Chinese New Year, Peranakan hospitality centres on the
**tok panjang** — literally the "long table", a lavish spread of many dishes laid
out for guests to graze along. By most accounts the long communal table was
encouraged by British dining fashion and adopted by better-off Peranakan
households; in Penang the custom is known as *t'ng tok* and is linked to ancestral
worship as well as celebration. A tok panjang might carry ayam buah keluak, babi
pongteh, kueh pie tee, *nasi ulam*, sambal dishes and a parade of kuih all at
once — a display of a household's cooking that doubled as a statement of status.
The feast remains the most vivid expression of a cuisine built, above all, around
family and occasion.

## Penang versus Melaka

Because the community settled in three different ports, the cooking acquired two
broad regional accents. The table below lines them up:

| Aspect | Penang (northern) style | Melaka & Singapore (southern) style |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dominant register | Sour, tangy, hotter | Sweeter, milder, richer |
| Key accents | Tamarind, lime, herbs; a Thai influence | Coconut milk; an Indonesian influence |
| Emblematic bowl | Sour Penang assam laksa | Coconut-rich Nyonya laksa |
| Typical dishes | Assam fish, perut ikan, inchi kabin | Babi pongteh, ayam buah keluak |

The contrast is a question of proportion rather than a hard border — both kitchens
pound a rempah and both reach for belacan — but it is real enough that a Penang
Nyonya cook and a Melaka one will often disagree about how sour, how sweet and how
hot a dish should be. That regional framing (Penang leaning Thai-sour, the south
leaning Indonesian-rich) recurs across the sources and is worth confirming against
the scholarly studies cited below. Either way, Nyonya cooking earns its place
among Malaysia's [national dishes](/food-lifestyle/national-dishes-of-malaysia) as
the clearest edible record of how two cultures learned to share a kitchen.
