Cross the South China Sea from Peninsular Malaysia to Sarawak and the food
changes before you have finished your first bowl. The noodles are tossed dry
instead of drowned in gravy; a chicken arrives steaming from a length of
bamboo; the "salad" turns out to be raw fish cured in lime; and the vegetable on
the table is a jungle fern no supermarket on the peninsula stocks. Malaysia's
largest state, on the island of Borneo, cooks to its own map of ingredients and
peoples — and that is the first thing to understand about Sarawak food.

## A cuisine shaped by Borneo

Where peninsular cooking grew out of a Malay-Chinese-Indian meeting, Sarawak's
grew out of a different crowd: the state's many indigenous communities and the
particular mix of Chinese dialect groups who settled its towns. The result is a
cuisine often described as less spicy and more subtle than food elsewhere in
Malaysia, leaning on fresh seafood and herbs such as turmeric, lemongrass,
ginger and tapioca leaves rather than on heavy curries.

Its base is the rainforest and the river. Sarawak kitchens reach for wild ferns,
bamboo, sago, freshwater and sea fish, native herbs and fruits that rarely travel
far beyond Borneo — ingredients a tourism guide sums up as a larder of jungle and
coast rather than of the spice trade. Kuching, the state capital, is the showcase
for all of it, and in 2021 was named a UNESCO Creative City of Gastronomy —
reported as the first Malaysian city to earn the title.

This guide is a map of the state's signature plates, not a ranking; Sarawak food
sits within Malaysia's wider patchwork of
[regional and ethnic cuisines](/food-lifestyle/regional-and-ethnic-cuisines).

## The peoples behind the plate

Sarawak's dishes make most sense once you know whose kitchens they came from. The
state's indigenous groups each contribute their own techniques: the Iban are
associated with bamboo cooking (*pansuh*), the Bidayuh with dishes such as *asam
siok*, the Melanau with sago-based foods, and the Orang Ulu of the interior with
*garam barrio*, a prized mountain salt used to preserve food. Collectively these
communities are often grouped under the broad term Dayak, though each has its own
distinct larder and festival table.

Layered on top is the Chinese contribution, which in Sarawak skews towards dialect
groups less dominant on the peninsula. Foochow (Fuzhou) settlers, concentrated
around Sibu, gave the state *kampua mee*, *kompia* and other wheat-and-pork dishes,
while Teochew, Hokkien and Hakka cooks shaped its noodle and coffee-shop culture.
Malay cooking adds dishes such as *bubur pedas* and the famous layered cake. The
everyday Sarawak food scene is this meeting of indigenous, Chinese and Malay
tables in one town.

## Sarawak laksa: the state's calling card

If Sarawak has one dish that travellers chase, it is **Sarawak laksa**. Despite
the shared name it is its own creature, built not on curry powder or on the sour
tamarind broth of the north but on a complex paste of sambal belacan, tamarind,
lemongrass and aromatics. The broth is chicken- and prawn-based and fragrant
rather than fiery; whether it contains any coconut milk is genuinely disputed,
with some cooks adding a little and others none at all. It is served over fine
rice vermicelli (*bihun*) and topped with shredded chicken, prawns, strips of
omelette, bean sprouts and coriander, finished with a squeeze of calamansi lime
and a dab of extra sambal on the side.

By the most-repeated account the dish was popularised by a Teochew immigrant,
Goh Lik Teck, who sold it in central Kuching in the 1940s; the details of that
origin are not firmly documented and are best treated as local lore. What is
certain is its fame: the late American chef Anthony Bourdain praised it as a
"breakfast of the gods" after eating it in Kuching, a phrase Sarawakians have
embraced ever since. Because the full laksa family is covered elsewhere, this
guide only sketches the Sarawak bowl — see the dedicated
[laksa](/food-lifestyle/laksa) guide for how it sits among Penang asam laksa,
curry laksa and Johor laksa.

## Kolo mee and the Foochow noodle table

For locals the true everyday staple is not laksa but **kolo mee** (also written
*mee kolok*): springy egg noodles blanched, drained and tossed dry in a savoury
dressing of shallot oil, lard and a little vinegar, then topped with minced pork,
slices of *char siu* and fried shallots, usually with a bowl of clear soup on the
side. Eaten most often at breakfast, it is so central to the state's identity
that it has been recognised among Malaysia's national heritage dishes; halal
versions swap in beef or chicken for the pork.

Around Sibu the Foochow table offers close cousins. **Kampua mee** is a drier,
soy-and-lard-dressed noodle of Fuzhou origin, saltier than kolo mee and easy to
confuse with it. **Kompia** is a round, sesame-topped baked bun — sometimes called
the "Foochow bagel" — served plain or stuffed with minced, braised meat. These
wheat-based dishes are a reminder that Sarawak's Chinese food leans on dialect-group
traditions you meet less often on the peninsula; for the broader picture see
[Malaysian noodles and national dishes](/food-lifestyle/national-dishes-of-malaysia).

## Cooked in bamboo, served raw: manok pansoh and umai

Two dishes capture the indigenous heart of Sarawak cooking. **Manok pansoh** (also
*ayam pansuh* or *manuk pansuh*) is chicken packed into a hollow bamboo tube with
lemongrass, ginger and *daun ubi* (tapioca leaves) and cooked over an open fire;
the sealed bamboo steams the meat in its own juices and lends a faint woody
aroma. It is an Iban and Bidayuh dish traditionally made for big occasions,
especially the Gawai Dayak harvest festival, though it is now served year-round
in restaurants.

**Umai** comes from the Melanau of the coast and is, in effect, a Bornean ceviche:
firm boneless sea fish such as *tenggiri* (mackerel) sliced thin and "cooked"
only by lime, tossed with shallots, chilli, ginger and onion and eaten cold,
often with toasted sago pearls. Once a fisherman's quick meal, it is now a prized
starter and a fixture of Melanau areas such as Mukah.

## From the jungle and the river

Much of what fills a Sarawak table is foraged or native. **Midin** is a wild
jungle fern (*Stenochlaena palustris*) whose young fronds stay crisp when
stir-fried, usually with garlic, chilli and belacan; it grows in Borneo and is a
near-mandatory order at a Sarawak meal. **Terung asam**, a tart wild eggplant
roughly the size of a small orange, is simmered into sour soups — the Dayak *sup
terung* — that cut richer dishes. Other Bornean ingredients turn up constantly:
**dabai**, a wild black "olive" native to Sarawak, is softened and eaten with rice
or ground into a fragrant fried-rice paste, and sago appears as the Melanau staple
*linut*, a glue-like starch eaten with sour, spicy dips.

In the rural interior, Dayak cooking has long included hunted game such as wild
boar and venison, and older recipes document other forest meats. These are part
of traditional upland diets; they are not halal, are no longer everyday fare, and
raise wildlife-conservation questions, so they sit well outside the mainstream
restaurant menu most visitors will meet. They are noted here only to describe the
full historical range of the cuisine.

## Sweet finishes: kek lapis and tebaloi

Sarawak's most famous sweet is **kek lapis Sarawak**, an intricately layered cake
in which each thin, buttery layer — and, in the showiest versions, each
geometric pattern — is baked and assembled by hand, a process that can take hours
per cake. Modern bakeries, clustered in Kuching, sell dozens of flavours from
chocolate to cheese and pandan, and the cake is the souvenir visitors carry home.
Its roots are usually traced to Indonesian (Betawi) migrants who brought a layered
cake to Sarawak in the 1970s, after which local bakers made it their own.

Older and plainer is **tebaloi**, a thin, crisp sago cracker made with egg,
grated coconut and sugar and traditionally associated with the Melanau. Light and
faintly sweet, it is a classic Sarawak snack and travels well — the kind of
everyday biscuit that, like kek lapis, tells you a dish came from Borneo rather
than the peninsula.
