Walk past a Malay kitchen in the days before Hari Raya and you will often hear
rendang before you see it — the slow scrape of a wooden spoon against a heavy
pot, over and over, for hours. Rendang is not a dish you rush. It is coaxed,
not cooked, from a soupy coconut curry into something dark, dense and almost
dry, with every spoonful of liquid traded for flavour. The patience is the
recipe, and it is why rendang has travelled from a Sumatran highland kitchen to
the top of global "best food" lists.

## What exactly is rendang?

Rendang is a meat dish slow-cooked in coconut milk and a thick paste of herbs
and spices until the liquid all but disappears and the meat is coated in a
dark, concentrated crust. Beef is the classic choice — traditionally the
tougher cuts, and historically water buffalo, that reward long cooking — though
chicken rendang is everyday fare in Malaysia, and duck, lamb and even shellfish
versions exist.

What sets rendang apart from an ordinary curry is precisely what it is *not*:
it is not saucy. A gulai is a wet curry; rendang is its dry cousin, cooked far
longer so that the coconut oil separates out and the meat fries gently in its
own spices. That single difference — liquid driven off rather than ladled over
— is the heart of the dish and the source of its deep, slightly caramelised
taste. Alongside nasi lemak and others, it is one of the dishes most often named
when people reach for the idea of
[Malaysia's national cuisine](/food-lifestyle/national-dishes-of-malaysia).

## The slow cook: spice paste, coconut milk and kerisik

The method is simple to describe and hard to hurry. Meat is simmered in freshly
pressed **coconut milk** together with a fragrant spice paste, then stirred
over a low flame for several hours — commonly four to six — until the sauce
thickens, darkens and finally dries to a glossy coating.

The aromatics do the heavy lifting. A typical Malaysian rendang leans on
**galangal** (*lengkuas*), **lemongrass** (*serai*), **turmeric leaf** (*daun
kunyit*), kaffir lime leaf, and a base paste of shallots, garlic, ginger, dried
and fresh chillies. The signature finishing touch is **kerisik** — grated
coconut toasted until golden and pounded to an oily paste — which thickens the
dish and adds a nutty, roasted depth that distinguishes a proper rendang from a
mere dry curry. According to Indonesia's official tourism site, the word
*rendang* comes from the Minang *merendang*, referring to
"the slow-cooking process at the heart of the dish."

That long cook had a practical payoff before it ever had a culinary one: a
well-made rendang keeps for days or even weeks without refrigeration, because so
much moisture is removed and the spices preserve the meat.

## Minangkabau roots and the Malaysian table

Food historians trace rendang to the **Minangkabau people of West Sumatra** in
Indonesia. The historian Fadly Rahman points to early written traces of the
word in Malay literature, including the sixteenth-century *Hikayat Amir Hamzah*,
and to J.L. van der Toorn's 1891 Minangkabau-Malay-Dutch dictionary as an early
recorded Minangkabau mention — though the precise dating of rendang's origins is
still debated among scholars.

How it reached Malaysia is a story of movement. The Minangkabau have a strong
tradition of *merantau* — leaving home to seek fortune elsewhere — and rendang,
with its long shelf life, was the perfect provision for travellers and traders
crossing the Strait of Malacca. Over generations those migrants settled, most
famously in **Negeri Sembilan**, whose customs and cuisine still carry a deep
Minangkabau imprint; the state is known for its own dark *rendang* styles. The
dish became so thoroughly woven into Malay cooking that it now sits comfortably
within Malaysia's own
[regional and ethnic cuisines](/food-lifestyle/regional-and-ethnic-cuisines)
rather than reading as foreign.

Because both Malaysia and Indonesia hold rendang dear, ownership is sometimes
argued online. The more useful framing is the one most food scholars use: it is
a **shared heritage of the Malay world**, with each country nurturing its own
beloved versions.

## A festive centrepiece

Rendang is special-occasion food. In Malaysia it is most strongly tied to
**Hari Raya Aidilfitri**, where it appears beside *ketupat* and *lemang* as a
centrepiece of the open-house spread, and it features again at Hari Raya Aidiladha
and at weddings and *kenduri* feasts. In its Minangkabau homeland it carries
similar ceremonial weight, served at life's milestones from births to marriages
and religious festivals.

Part of the reason is practical — a dish cooked slowly in big batches, keeping
well, suits a day of feeding a stream of guests — and part is symbolic, with the
patience it demands read as a mark of care for those you host. Its festive role
places it squarely among the
[festive foods eaten across Malaysian celebrations](/food-lifestyle/festive-foods-across-malaysian-festivals).

## Wet, dry and regional variants

Not all rendang is equally dry, and the difference is mostly a matter of how
long the pot stays on the heat. Stop early and you have a lighter, saucier dish;
push on and you reach the dark, concentrated classic.

| Style | What it is | Texture & colour |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Kalio ("wet" rendang) | Rendang cooked for less time, liquid not fully reduced | Lighter brown, saucy, milder |
| Rendang (dry) | The fully reduced classic, oil separated, meat coated | Dark brown to near-black, dry, intense |
| Rendang ayam | Chicken version, common in Malaysia | Quicker to cook, lighter than beef |
| Negeri Sembilan styles | Minangkabau-rooted local versions, incl. yellow and black | Range from pale to very dark |

Many Malaysian rendangs sit closer to the *kalio* end — a little saucier and
lighter than the deeply dried Minangkabau original — which is a difference of
house style and taste, not of right and wrong. Regional cooks also swap in local
ingredients, from green-chilli versions to coastal shellfish rendang, so the
dish is better understood as a family of recipes than a single fixed formula.

## How rendang became globally famous

Rendang's leap from regional favourite to international icon owes a lot to a
single poll. In 2011, **CNN** ran a feature on the world's most delicious foods;
after readers voted, rendang topped the ranking, and the dish has appeared on
CNN's widely shared "World's 50 Best Foods" lists in the years since. For a
slow-cooked home dish with no marketing budget, that global billing was a
turning point, and rendang is now a fixture on "must-eat" lists for visitors to
both Malaysia and Indonesia.

The fame has a serious side too. Rendang's standing has fed into formal heritage
recognition on both sides of the strait, part of the broader story of
[Malaysian food as national and world heritage](/food-lifestyle/malaysian-food-as-national-and-unesco-heritage).
The exact year-by-year CNN placements and heritage-listing dates vary between
retellings, so the figures here are given in outline rather than as a precise
scoreboard.
