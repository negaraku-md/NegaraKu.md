Malaysia's savoury food gets most of the attention, but the country has a deep,
distinctive sweet repertoire — and much of it arrives cold, in a bowl, eaten with
a spoon. This guide covers the spooned and iced desserts: shaved-ice mountains,
warm coconut-milk sweet soups, soft puddings and a handful of fried favourites.
For the bite-sized, hand-held steamed and baked cakes — *onde-onde*, *kuih
lapis*, *seri muka* and the rest — see the separate guide to
[kuih-muih](/food-lifestyle/kuih-muih); the two worlds share flavours but differ
in form.

## Three flavours that run through everything

Before the individual desserts, it helps to know the flavour base that ties them
together. Where many Western sweets lean on dairy and refined white sugar,
Malaysia's lean on a recurring trio: **coconut milk** (*santan*) for richness,
**[pandan](/food-lifestyle/pandan)** for its grassy, vanilla-like aroma and green
colour, and **[gula melaka](/food-lifestyle/gula-melaka)** — palm sugar boiled
down into dark, smoky-caramel blocks or syrup — for sweetness. A great many
Malaysian desserts are some combination of these three, which is why a bowl of
cendol and a bowl of sago pudding can taste like cousins despite looking nothing
alike.

This palette reflects the ingredients historically close to hand in the region's
kitchens, and it crosses communities: Malay, Peranakan (Straits Chinese) and
Chinese dessert traditions all draw on coconut, palm sugar and local starches,
borrowing freely from one another.

## Shaved-ice desserts: cendol and ais kacang

The two desserts most associated with a hot afternoon are both built on finely
shaved ice. **[Cendol](/food-lifestyle/cendol)** is the one with soft, green,
worm-shaped jelly made from rice flour and coloured with pandan, served over ice
with coconut milk and gula melaka syrup, usually with red beans and sometimes
sweet corn, glutinous rice or durian. **[Ais kacang](/food-lifestyle/ais-kacang)**
— widely called **ABC**, from *air batu campur* ("mixed ice") — is instead a tall
mound of shaved ice piled over a mix of red beans, sweet corn, grass jelly
(*cincau*), *attap chee* (palm seed) and jellies, then drizzled with rose syrup,
gula melaka and evaporated or condensed milk.

The names point to their differences: *ais kacang* literally means "bean ice,"
after its original red-bean topping, while cendol is defined by its pandan jelly
in coconut milk. The two are often sold side by side at the same stall, and both
come in endless local variants — "air batu campur" variations differ stall to
stall, and both are frequently topped with ice cream, peanuts or durian. Both are
also eaten across Southeast Asia, and their precise origins are contested, so the
dedicated guides treat single-origin claims as unresolved.

## Coconut-milk sweet soups (bubur)

A large family of Malaysian desserts falls under the loose label **bubur** —
sweet, warm or room-temperature "porridges" and sweet soups, most of them
enriched with coconut milk and sweetened with gula melaka.

- **Bubur cha cha** is the most celebrated: a colourful coconut-milk soup of
  sweet potato and yam (taro) cubes, often with tapioca pearls, black-eyed beans
  and chewy coloured jellies. It is widely described as a Peranakan (Nyonya)
  dessert, though that attribution and the origin of its playful name are not
  firmly documented and should be treated with caution. It can be served warm or
  chilled.
- **Pengat pisang** is a banana dessert in a thick, sweet coconut-and-gula-melaka
  sauce, sometimes with sago or sweet potato; it is traditionally associated with
  festive occasions in some communities.
- **Sago gula melaka** is among the simplest and most elegant: cooked sago
  (tapioca) pearls set into a soft pudding and served with coconut milk and a
  generous pour of gula melaka syrup — essentially the flavour trio in its
  barest form.
- **Bubur kacang** (often *bubur kacang hijau*, mung-bean porridge, or the
  black-bean and sago variants) is an everyday sweet soup of beans simmered soft
  in coconut milk with palm or cane sugar, sometimes scented with pandan or
  ginger.

These vary considerably by household and stall, and several have close relatives
elsewhere in the region, so recipes are best read as typical rather than fixed.

## Pulut hitam and tau fu fah

Two spooned puddings turn up almost everywhere, from hawker stalls to *kopitiam*.
**Pulut hitam** — black glutinous rice porridge — is made by simmering black (or
dark-purple) glutinous rice until it breaks down into a thick, nutty, faintly
sweet porridge, then serving it with a swirl of coconut milk and gula melaka or
sugar. It is comforting, warm and only lightly sweet, and is eaten as both a
dessert and a breakfast.

**Tau fu fah** (also written *tau foo fah* or *taufufa*; known more broadly as
*douhua* or tofu pudding) is a silken, custard-soft beancurd served warm or
cold, traditionally with a clear sugar or gula melaka syrup and, in Malaysia,
sometimes with pandan or ginger syrup. It belongs to a wider Chinese
soft-beancurd tradition carried into Malaysia by Chinese communities, and the
exact setting agent and texture vary by maker. Light and barely sweet, it sits at
the gentler end of the dessert spectrum.

## Fried and griddled sweets: pisang goreng and apam balik

Not every Malaysian dessert is cold or spooned. **Pisang goreng** — banana
fritters — are bananas (often firm, starchy cooking varieties) coated in batter
and deep-fried until crisp and golden, sold hot from street carts and markets as
an any-time snack. Variations exist across the region and some stalls dust them
with sugar or serve them with toppings, but the plain fritter is the classic.

**Apam balik** is a thick, griddled turnover pancake: a batter cooked in a
round pan until the base is set and the surface is bubbly and soft, then folded
over a filling of crushed peanuts, sugar and sweet corn, sometimes with butter or
margarine. A thin, lacy, crispy version is also popular. Both pisang goreng and
apam balik function as sweet snacks sold at roadside stalls and night markets
rather than as sit-down desserts.

## The pandan, coconut and gula melaka base

Return, finally, to the flavour trio, because it explains how to read any
Malaysian dessert you meet. If a sweet is green and fragrant, pandan is usually
doing the work; if it is rich and creamy without being dairy-heavy, that is
coconut milk; and if it carries a dark, almost smoky caramel sweetness, that is
gula melaka rather than ordinary sugar. Durian — Malaysia's famously pungent
"king of fruits," covered in the guide to
[durian culture and varieties](/food-lifestyle/durian-culture-and-varieties) —
joins the party as a premium add-on in cendol, ABC and various *bubur*, though it
is a modern luxury topping rather than part of the traditional base.

Understanding this base also makes the region's shared heritage clearer: because
the same few ingredients underpin desserts from Penang to Melaka to the Borneo
states — and well beyond Malaysia's borders — many of these sweets are genuinely
regional rather than the invention of any one place.

The easiest place to meet most of these desserts at once is a night market or
street-food cluster, where a single lane may offer cendol, ABC, pisang goreng and
apam balik within a few steps of one another — see the guide to
