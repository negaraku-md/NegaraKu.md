In Malaysia, durian is not just a fruit — it is called the king of fruits, and at
peak season it can close roads, drive up stall rents, and draw tourists from across
the sea just for a single lobe of dense yellow flesh.

Behind brands like Musang King and Black Thorn lies an orderly registration system.
Every major durian clone carries a **D**-lettered code assigned by the **Department
of Agriculture Malaysia**. That code is what distinguishes a true variety from a mere
sales name.

## What does the 'D' code in a durian's name mean?

Market names like *Musang King* and *Black Thorn* grab attention, but a clone's true
identity lies in its registration code. The code begins with the letter **D**
followed by a number, in the order it was registered by the Department of
Agriculture.

This matters because durian is propagated **vegetatively** — through grafting, not
from seed — so that every clonal tree produces fruit with uniform characteristics. A
kampung durian grown from seed may be delicious, but it is not a registered clone and
its taste is not guaranteed to be consistent.

The three best-known names are usually referred to by three different codes:

| Market name | Clone code (commonly cited) | Brief characteristics |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Musang King (Raja Kunyit) | D197 | Dense turmeric-yellow flesh, bitter-sweet, star-shaped core |
| D24 (Sultan) | D24 | Classic clone, lighter and sweeter, often recommended for beginners |
| Black Thorn (Duri Hitam) | D200 | Reddish-orange flesh, soft, premium price |

Bear in mind: the clone codes above — particularly D197 for Musang King and D200 for
Black Thorn — are commonly used in the market and the media, but their official
registration numbers should still be checked against the Department of Agriculture's
list of registered clones before being treated as a definitive reference.

Beyond these three, varieties such as **IOI** are also mentioned among the clones
approved for fresh durian export to China, alongside Musang King, D24 and Black Thorn.

## Why is Musang King so protected?

Musang King is not only expensive — it is legally protected. The name is registered
as a **Geographical Indication** in Malaysia, one of the earliest and best known in
the country.

The effect of that protection is simple: only durian grown in **designated areas** —
mainly **Pahang and Johor** — may legally be marketed under the Musang King name.
This means producers in other countries cannot label their durian as Musang King even
if it looks almost identical. Geographical Indication protection acts as a brand
shield for a product that has now become a pillar of agricultural exports.

It is part of a larger framework: as of December 2024, Malaysia had **127 registered
Geographical Indications** across a range of products.

## When is the durian season, and where is it grown?

Durian is not a year-round fruit. It flowers and fruits seasonally, and understanding
that cycle saves money and avoids disappointment.

In general, there are two periods (estimates, not an official schedule):

- **Main season** — usually around **June to August**, peaking around June and July.
  This is when supply is most abundant and prices most competitive.
- **Minor season** — around the **end of the year (December to January)**, with a
  smaller but still meaningful harvest.

The exact timing varies by region and weather. Highland areas such as **Raub and
Bentong in Pahang** are renowned as Musang King strongholds, while **Penang** has a
reputation of its own for clones like Ang Heh (Red Prawn). Heavy rain during
flowering can delay or shorten a season, so the dates are never fixed.

In terms of national supply, durian is actually in surplus relative to domestic
demand. According to the Department of Statistics Malaysia, durian recorded a
**self-sufficiency ratio (SSR) of 103.7% in 2024** — meaning local production exceeds
domestic needs, and it is that surplus which supports the export market.

## How do you buy and eat durian wisely?

Buying durian has its own etiquette, and a little knowledge saves money and avoids
disappointing fruit.

A few practical pointers:

- **Buy by weight, not by count.** Most sellers weigh the durian whole (the entire
  fruit). A price per kilogram means a large, thick-fleshed durian is not necessarily
  the best value.
- **Ask the seller to open it in front of you** if you are buying to eat right away,
  so you can see the colour, thickness and condition of the flesh before paying.
- **The colour of the flesh hints at the variety.** Musang King tends to be dense
  yellow, D24 paler, and Black Thorn reddish-orange.
- **Self-dropped durian** is usually riper and more aromatic than fruit picked early
  — this is the main reason local durian is hard to match with durian harvested
  unripe for long-distance shipping.

As for eating etiquette, durian is often shared in a group, sometimes paired with
**mangosteen**, which is regarded as "cooling". Many people also avoid durian with
alcohol. Because of its strong smell, durian is banned in many hotels, on public
transport and on flights.

## How did durian become a billion-ringgit export?

China's appetite reshaped the economics of Malaysian durian. For years, only
**frozen** durian and durian **paste/pulp** were allowed into the Chinese market.
That changed in 2024.

The **Protocol of Phytosanitary Requirements** for the export of **fresh** durian
from Malaysia to China was signed in **June 2024**. Under it, only farms and packing
houses that are **registered with the Department of Agriculture** under the **myGAP**
standard and **approved by China's customs authority (GACC)** are eligible to export.
The first shipment of fresh durian arrived at **Zhengzhou International Airport on 25
August 2024** — about **40 tonnes** across several shipments from **eight companies**,
carrying the Musang King, D24, IOI and Black Thorn varieties, for distribution to six
major Chinese cities.

The trade value is substantial. According to a parliamentary reply reported by
Bernama, Malaysia exported durian worth **RM6.37 billion** — about **115,359 metric
tonnes** — to China between **2018 and 2025**. A breakdown for selected years shows
the shift:

| Year | Frozen (tonnes / value) | Paste & pulp (tonnes / value) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2022 | 13,401 t / RM1.277 billion | 5,703 t / RM326 million |
| 2023 | 13,983 t / RM738 million | 12,321 t / RM623 million |

For the period **2024 to June 2025**, **fresh** durian accounted for **773 tonnes
worth RM50 million** — small for now, but a category that previously did not exist at
all. The government is targeting durian export value of **RM1.8 billion** by 2030,
involving **69,000 metric tonnes**.

## What's next

If you want to try Malaysian durian for the first time, start with the softer **D24**
before jumping to the bitter-sweet Musang King, and buy at the peak of the season
around June to July when supply is high and prices are most reasonable.

If you follow the trade angle, two things are worth watching. First, how far the
**fresh** durian category grows relative to frozen and paste after the 2024 market
access, since fresh durian carries a premium but far stricter logistical demands.
Second, whether **Geographical Indication** protection for names like Musang King —
and efforts to extend it to Black Thorn — can curb counterfeit durian in overseas
markets.

For official figures, refer to the **Department of Statistics Malaysia** releases for
the self-sufficiency ratio and utilization, and the **Department of Agriculture**
website for the list of registered clones and the latest export protocol requirements
— both are updated periodically and supersede third-party summaries.
