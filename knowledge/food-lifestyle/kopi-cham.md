    a: "It is a blend of brewed coffee and brewed tea in one glass, sweetened and enriched with condensed milk (sometimes evaporated milk and sugar). The ratio varies by shop, but the point is to get the roast of coffee and the tannin of tea together, rounded out by the sweet milk. For anyone who sits down at a Malaysian coffee shop and cannot decide between coffee
and tea, the counter has a tidy answer: have both, in the same glass. **Kopi cham** —
coffee and tea blended together, sweetened with condensed milk — is one of the
kopitiam's small strokes of genius, and a neat lesson in the coffee-shop's ordering
language.

## What kopi cham is

Kopi cham is a blend of **brewed coffee and brewed tea** in one glass, sweetened and
enriched with **condensed milk**. The ratio shifts from shop to shop, but the idea is
constant: marry the roast and body of coffee with the tannin and lift of tea, then
round the whole thing out with sweet milk. The result is fuller and more fragrant
than either drink alone — a little coffee bitterness, a little tea astringency, a lot
of comfort.

For where kopi cham sits among the country's wider drinks board, see the
[Malaysian drinks](/food-lifestyle/malaysian-drinks) hub. This article is the deep
dive into the coffee-tea mix itself.

## What "cham" means

The name is the recipe. **"Cham"** comes from the **Hokkien** word meaning *to mix*
or *join together* — so "kopi cham" is simply "mixed coffee", coffee joined with tea.
It belongs to the largely Hokkien-and-Hainanese vocabulary that governs every drink
at the counter: *kopi-O* (black coffee, no milk), *kopi-C* (with evaporated milk),
*kosong* (no sugar), *gao* (strong), *peng* (iced). The full logic of that code is
laid out in the
[kopi, teh tarik and coffee-shop drinks](/food-lifestyle/kopi-teh-tarik-and-coffeeshop-drinks)
guide; this article stays with the mix itself.

## Hot, iced, and the Hong Kong cousin

Kopi cham is served **hot** by default. For the iced version, ask for **ais kopi
cham** or **cham peng** — *ais* being Malay for ice, *peng* the Hokkien term — and,
as ever, add **kosong** for no sugar.

The drink has a famous relative abroad: Hong Kong's **yuenyeung** (*yinyeung*), which
blends coffee with Hong Kong-style milk tea and is often described as the ancestor of
the Malaysian version. The two share the same clever idea, but the Malaysian name
comes from the Hokkien word *cham*, not from the Cantonese name — a small detail that
says a lot about how the kopitiam's language was built, largely by Hainanese and
Hokkien coffee-shop families from the early 20th century.

## Where to find it

Kopi cham is poured from the same counter as [teh tarik](/food-lifestyle/teh-tarik)
and plain kopi, at **kopitiam** and **mamak** stalls across the country. It is an
any-time drink — morning pick-me-up, afternoon pause — for anyone who refuses to
choose between coffee and tea. For the institution that pours it, see
[kopitiam and mamak culture](/food-lifestyle/kopitiam-mamak-culture).
