Along the east-coast roads of Terengganu, the smell of frying fish paste drifts
out of roadside stalls, and the thing sizzling in the oil is almost always
keropok lekor: a fat, pale, slightly chewy fish sausage that locals eat by the
bagful with a cup of sweet, fiery chilli dip. Few snacks say *Pantai Timur* —
Malaysia's east coast — quite so plainly.

## What keropok lekor is

At its simplest, keropok lekor is fish turned into a snack. Fresh fish is
pounded or ground to a paste and worked together with sago or tapioca flour,
salt and a little sugar into a stiff, pale dough. That dough is the starting
point for a family of crackers that runs through much of
[Malay cuisine](/food-lifestyle/malay-cuisine).

The fish matters. Ikan tamban (a small herring) is widely named as a favourite,
prized for flavour, while sardine (selayang), kerisi and ikan parang also appear
in different recipes. Which fish makes the *best* lekor is a matter of local
opinion rather than a settled rule, and stalls guard their own preferences.

## Lekor versus keping

The same dough leads two lives, and the difference is in the shaping and the
cooking.

Rolled into thick ropes and boiled, it becomes **keropok lekor** proper — soft,
dense and faintly oily, usually given a quick fry to crisp the skin before it is
cut into chunks and served. The name *lekor* is often linked to the rolling of
the dough or the long shape of the sticks, though accounts of the word differ.

Rolled thin, sliced into coins and dried in the sun before deep-frying, the same
mixture turns into **keropok keping** — a light, brittle cracker, the puffed
chip you find in packets nationwide. The two are cousins, not rivals, and a good
Terengganu stall sells both from the same counter. (Keropok lekor should not be
confused with keropok keping, even though they begin as one dough.)

## The chilli dip

Lekor is rarely eaten plain. The partner is a homemade dip that leans sweet,
sour and hot at once — typically built on ground chilli, vinegar, salt and palm
sugar (gula Melaka), sometimes thickened or seasoned to a stall's own formula.
The sugar rounds off the vinegar's sharpness, and the sauce's sweet-sour bite
cuts cleanly through the oily, savoury sausage. It is as much a part of the
snack as the fish itself.

## Terengganu and Losong

Keropok lekor is bound up with east-coast fishing life. A common account traces
it to fishermen looking to use and preserve a catch before the monsoon
(*tengkujuh*) closed the season, turning surplus fish into something that kept.
Today it is street food you meet everywhere along the coast — at
[pasar malam and roadside stalls](/food-lifestyle/pasar-malam-and-street-food)
and at highway stops — but Kuala Terengganu is its heartland, and the Losong area
in particular is closely tied to the snack's reputation. Exactly which village
first made it, and whether "losong" names a style or only the place, are points
local sources still disagree on.

For visitors, the practical tip is simple: eat it hot and fresh off the fryer,
when the outside is crisp and the inside still yields. Kept too long it firms up
and the fishiness grows stronger. As a Malay fish snack it is generally halal,
and it sits comfortably among the country's best-loved regional bites — a good
companion read is our guide to Malaysia's
[national dishes](/food-lifestyle/national-dishes-of-malaysia).
