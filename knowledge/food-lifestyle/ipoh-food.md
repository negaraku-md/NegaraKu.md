Ask anyone in Malaysia where to eat and sooner or later they will send you to
Ipoh. The Perak state capital, cradled in the Kinta Valley between dramatic
limestone hills, built its fortune on tin and its reputation on food — and the
two stories are the same story. The miners who poured into the valley in the
19th century, and the Chinese communities who fed them, left a dense hawker
culture that the town has polished ever since.

## Tin, limestone water and a Chinese kitchen

Ipoh grew explosively in the 1880s after large tin deposits were found nearby,
and for a time it was among the richest cities in the country. That boom drew a
large Chinese population — Ipoh remains a Chinese-majority city, popularly
described as predominantly Cantonese, though Hakka and Hainanese communities also
shaped its kitchens. The result is a food scene that leans heavily Cantonese:
clear broths, poached and roasted meats, dim sum, and an obsession with the
quality of a few core ingredients rather than with heat or heavy spice.

Running under almost every Ipoh food legend is one claim: the water. Locals
widely credit the mineral-rich water that filters down through the valley's
limestone hills for the town's famously crunchy bean sprouts and its unusually
silky flat rice noodles. It is worth hedging — food writers repeat this as local
belief and acknowledge that the scientific backing is thin. As one Malaysia
Airlines guide puts it, the limestone water is "famed" and credited with the
sprouts and the noodles, but presented as tradition rather than proven fact. True
or not, the dishes it is invoked to explain are very real.

The town also divides, usefully, into two halves. **Old Town**, on the west bank
of the Kinta River, is the heritage core — tiled shophouse "five-foot ways" and
the vintage kopitiams most associated with white coffee and classic hor fun.
**New Town**, developed later across the river, is busier and more commercial,
thick with restaurants and dessert shops. A first-time eater usually starts with
an Old Town kopitiam crawl.

## Ipoh white coffee (kopi putih)

The town's single most famous export is a drink. **Ipoh white coffee** — *kopi
putih* — is said to have originated in Ipoh, and the "white" is a quirk of
translation rather than a description of the colour. The beans are roasted in
margarine (traditionally palm-oil margarine) *without* the sugar and wheat used
in Malaysia's darker *kopi O* roast, producing a lighter roast; the brewed coffee
is then stirred with sweetened condensed milk. "The coffee beans themselves are
not actually white," Wikipedia notes; "the colour comes from the milk stirred
into the end product."

By popular account the drink was perfected in Ipoh's coffee shops — some versions
credit Hainanese kopitiam operators, and some cite the mid-20th century — but
these origin details rest on food media and are best treated as unverified. What
is not in doubt is its reach: the OldTown White Coffee chain, with its global
instant sachets, grew out of Ipoh. For the broader Malaysian coffee-shop drink
tradition this belongs to, see
[kopi, teh tarik and coffeeshop drinks](/food-lifestyle/kopi-teh-tarik-and-coffeeshop-drinks)
and the wider [coffee culture in Malaysia](/food-lifestyle/coffee-culture-malaysia).

## Bean-sprout chicken (nga choy kai)

If white coffee is Ipoh's drink, **bean-sprout chicken** is its plate.
Known in Cantonese as *nga choy kai* and in Malay as *tauge ayam* (芽菜鸡), it is a
deceptively plain dish: poached or steamed chicken, carved and dressed with light
soy sauce and sesame oil, served alongside a mound of blanched bean sprouts and,
usually, a bowl of smooth flat rice noodles in clear chicken soup. It is a close
cousin of [Hainanese chicken rice](/food-lifestyle/hainanese-chicken-rice), with
the sprouts standing in for the fragrant rice.

The whole dish is a showcase for those celebrated local sprouts, which Wikipedia
describes as "crunchy and succulent, thanks to the hard water from the limestone
hills" of the Kinta Valley. SBS Food captures the local pride bluntly: "Locals
insist that this dish made anywhere else in the world won't taste the same." The
noodle half of the pairing is Ipoh's version of *hor fun* — the flat rice noodle
Cantonese kitchens call *sar hor fun* (沙河粉, from *shahe fen*) — valued here for a
silkiness the town again attributes to its water.

## Kai see hor fun and the noodle bowls

The town's other great noodle dish takes the same flat rice noodle and puts it in
a bowl of soup. **Kai see hor fun** (*kai see* meaning shredded chicken) is hor
fun in a clear, savoury broth simmered from chicken bones *and* prawn shells,
topped with slivered poached chicken, whole prawns and spring onion, sometimes
finished with a slick of reddish prawn oil. It is distinctively Ipoh, and the
chicken-and-prawn stock is the point — some cooks deepen it with dried anchovies,
though whether an "anchovy" version counts as a separate named dish is unclear and
worth checking locally.

Ipoh's flat rice noodles turn up in other guises too — stir-fried with a dark
gravy as *chau hor fun*, or steamed into *chee cheong fun* — part of Malaysia's
broader world of [noodles](/food-lifestyle/malaysian-noodles). Stalls such as
Thean Chun in Old Town are long associated with the clear-soup version, though, as
always, specific stalls and operators change over time.

## Salt-baked chicken

A different Chinese community left its mark with **salt-baked chicken**, a Hakka
dish Ipoh has made its own. A whole bird is seasoned — often with *dong quai* and
other Chinese herbs — wrapped in paper, and buried in hot rock salt to bake slowly
for hours, yielding fragrant, tender, well-seasoned meat without a crust of salt
on the flesh. In Ipoh the dish is tightly linked with long-running specialist
shops in and around Old Town that have, in some cases, sold essentially this one
item for generations. It travels well, and many visitors carry a whole salt-baked
chicken home as an edible souvenir.

## Dim sum and the kopitiam sweets

Ipoh's Cantonese roots show most clearly at breakfast, when the town's **dim sum**
houses fill up with trolleys and steamers — a Cantonese speciality that locals
treat as a weekend institution. Alongside the savoury dumplings come Cantonese-style
**egg-custard tarts** (*dan tat*), which several Ipoh bakeries are known for.

The kopitiams add their own sweet signature: chilled **caramel custard**, a
set-cream-and-caramel dessert that classic Old Town shops such as Thean Chun are
famous for — and which, by many accounts, sells out by early afternoon. Between
the custards, the tarts and the white coffee, an Ipoh dessert stop is as much a
part of the ritual as the chicken and noodles.

## Sweet souvenirs: heong peng and Tambun pomelo

Two edible souvenirs round out the town's reputation. **Heong peng** (literally
"fragrant biscuit") are small, flaky baked pastries with a sweet, sticky filling
built on malt or molasses and a little shallot; the suburb of **Gunung Rapat** is
regarded as their traditional home, and boxes of them are a standard Ipoh
take-home gift.

The town is also famous for the **Tambun pomelo**, a sweet, large citrus grown in
the mineral-rich soil around Tambun on Ipoh's fringes — the same terroir, locals
like to point out, that gives the bean sprouts their snap. Roadside stalls stack
them high for passing travellers. Both biscuit and fruit sit within Malaysia's
wider map of [regional and ethnic cuisines](/food-lifestyle/regional-and-ethnic-cuisines),
where each town's larder tells you something about its history.
