Step into an Iban longhouse kitchen in the Sarawak interior and the pantry is
largely the land itself: a river for fish, the surrounding jungle for ferns and
game, a grove of bamboo, and the padi the community grows. Iban food is less a
fixed canon of recipes than a way of turning what is close at hand into a shared
meal — and its most famous trick is to cook dinner inside a tree.

## A longhouse and jungle-river kitchen

The Iban are the largest indigenous group in Sarawak and one of the several
peoples collectively called the Dayak of Borneo. Their traditional home is the
*rumah panjai*, the longhouse — a single raised structure of many family rooms
opening onto a shared gallery — set near rivers that double as larder and
highway. Because the Iban were historically not Muslim, their diet includes pork
and wild game alongside chicken, freshwater fish and foraged plants; this guide
describes those foods plainly, as a matter of record rather than endorsement.

The cooking that results is generally simple and ingredient-led: fresh catches
and greens, a short list of aromatics, and techniques — grilling in bamboo,
fermenting, wrapping in leaves — that suit a kitchen without much equipment. Iban
dishes form one of the strongest threads in the wider story of
[Sarawak food](/food-lifestyle/sarawak-food), the Bornean cuisine that sets the
state apart from the peninsula.

## Manok pansoh: chicken cooked in bamboo

The dish most associated with the Iban is **manok pansoh** — *manok* meaning
chicken and *pansoh* (also spelled *pansuh*) meaning cooked in bamboo. It is
widely described as an Iban traditional dish, and it appears on most lists of
Sarawak's signature indigenous foods.

The method is the point. Chicken is cut up and seasoned — a Malaysian government
cultural record lists a paste of pounded garlic and shallots with ginger, chilli
and salt, along with the shoots of torch ginger (*pucuk tepus*) — then packed
into a fresh length of bamboo with a little water. Young tapioca (cassava) leaves
are stuffed in to plug the ends, and the sealed tube is propped over an open fire
and grilled for around half an hour, until it begins to smoke from both ends.
Inside, the chicken steams in its own juices, picking up a faint grassy scent
from the green bamboo; the tapioca leaves wilt into the dish. Many recipes also
add lemongrass, though sources differ on whether it belongs in the most
traditional version, so treat the exact aromatic blend as a matter of regional
and family variation.

Beyond the kitchen, manok pansoh is a celebratory dish, cooked for the Gawai
Dayak festival and other gatherings, and it is also sold at food stalls around
Sarawak. It is not exclusively Iban — Bidayuh and Orang Ulu communities prepare
bamboo chicken too — so it is better understood as a shared Dayak technique that
the Iban are especially known for.

## Pansoh beyond chicken: fish and pork

*Pansoh* names a method, not a single recipe, and the same bamboo tube takes in
whatever the river and forest provide. River fish and shrimp are cooked *pansoh*
in the same way, and so is pork, which the Iban eat. The appeal is practical as
much as culinary: the bamboo is a disposable cooking vessel that needs no pot,
seals in moisture, and lends its own aroma — well suited to cooking in the field
or at a longhouse without a modern stove. The seasonings shift with the main
ingredient, but the logic stays the same: aromatics, a leaf wrapping, and slow
steaming inside the cane.

## Jungle greens: midin, paku and ensabi

Vegetables in Iban and wider Sarawak cooking are often gathered rather than
grown. The best known is **midin** (*Stenochlaena palustris*), a wild fern with
curling tips that is prized for staying crisp after cooking; it is usually
stir-fried hard and fast with garlic or with shrimp paste (*belacan*), and some
restaurants serve it cold as a salad with shallots, chilli and vinegar. Sarawak
tourism material describes midin as unique to the state, though botanical sources
treat the fern as more widespread, so that claim is best hedged.

A second fern, **paku** (sometimes *paku kubuk* or *paku uban*), is the
hairier-stemmed relative that accounts tie more closely to Iban farming
communities, who gather and eat it during the planting season; it too is commonly
stir-fried with belacan. The mustard green **ensabi** (*daun ensabi*), a Sarawak
leaf vegetable, rounds out the everyday greens — cooked with belacan and garlic,
simmered into a simple soup, or salted and pickled as *kasam ensabi*.

## Kasam: preserving the catch

With fresh fish and meat abundant but perishable, preservation is a long-standing
part of the cuisine, and **kasam** is one of its distinctive forms. Kasam refers
to fish — or meat — preserved by fermentation; in Sarawak markets it sits
alongside *salai* (smoked) and *ikan masin* (salted) products, and at least one
account distinguishes kasam, which is fermented together with rice, from *balur*,
a simpler salt cure. The technique is a Bornean relative of the Peninsular
fermented fish known as *pekasam*, and the result is a pungent, sour, intensely
savoury ingredient used to flavour a meal rather than eaten in quantity on its
own. The precise Iban method — fermentation times, the leaves and rice used — is
not well documented in general sources and would reward a dedicated culinary or
heritage reference.

## Pulut: glutinous rice

Rice is the heart of the Iban table, and **pulut** (glutinous or sticky rice)
carries special weight at festive times. It is steamed, pressed into cakes, or
packed into bamboo with coconut and cooked much like a Sarawak version of
*lemang*, giving a smoky, chewy accompaniment to grilled meats and *pansoh*
dishes. Glutinous rice is also the usual base for the community's most important
drink — which is where the harvest, the kitchen and the calendar come together.

## Tuak and the Gawai Dayak harvest festival

**Tuak** is a fermented rice wine, generally brewed from glutinous rice (palm
sago or coconut wine is a separate drink, *ijok*), and it is inseparable from
**Gawai Dayak**, the harvest thanksgiving festival held on 1 and 2 June that the
Iban call *Ari Gawai*. Brewing often begins weeks ahead so the tuak is ready for
the celebration, which marks the close of the padi harvest.

For many Dayak, tuak is more than a drink. It is served to welcome guests and
shared socially through the festival, and it is understood as a gesture of
thanksgiving — rice, the gift of a good harvest, processed into a liquid and, as
one Sarawak brewer put it, given back to the gods. Accounts of its ritual role
vary and some practices are changing as tuak reaches wider urban markets, so the
specifics are best taken as a living tradition rather than a fixed rite. Described
neutrally, tuak is the clearest example of how Iban food ties the longhouse, the
land and the farming year into a single table — one small part of Malaysia's
wider map of [regional and ethnic cuisines](/food-lifestyle/regional-and-ethnic-cuisines).
