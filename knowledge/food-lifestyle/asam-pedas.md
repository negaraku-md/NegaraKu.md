Few Malay dishes announce themselves as plainly as asam pedas. The name is a
flavour label — *asam* (sour) and *pedas* (spicy) — and a good bowl delivers
exactly that: a chilli-red gravy, glossy with oil, sharp with tamarind, built
around a piece of fish that has soaked up all of it. It is everyday food, cooked
in home kitchens and roadside *kedai makan* across the peninsula, yet two states
guard its reputation fiercely enough to argue over whose is best.

## What asam pedas is

At its heart, asam pedas is a fish stew in a tangy, spicy gravy. A base of
blended chillies, shallots, garlic, ginger and turmeric is fried into a fragrant
*rempah*, loosened with water, and sharpened with tamarind — *asam jawa* — which
gives the dish its defining sourness. Belacan (fermented shrimp paste) deepens the
savour. The fish goes in late so it stays intact, and the pot is finished with
aromatic herbs: **daun kesum** (Vietnamese mint, also the laksa leaf) and often
**bunga kantan** (torch ginger) lend a bright, almost citrusy perfume. Okra,
brinjal and tomato are common additions. It belongs squarely to the tradition
covered in our guide to [Malay cuisine](/food-lifestyle/malay-cuisine).

The fish is whatever is firm and fresh. In Malaysia the usual choices include
**ikan pari** (stingray), **ikan kembung** (mackerel) and **siakap**
(barramundi, or sea bass) — though cooks use snapper, tuna and freshwater fish
too, and some versions are made with chicken or meat instead.

## Melaka, Johor and the regional styles

If asam pedas has a spiritual home, two states claim it. **Melaka** leans into its
heritage association with the dish — its version tends to be soupier and is often
linked to the city's Nyonya and trading-port past, explored in our piece on
[Melaka Nyonya food](/food-lifestyle/melaka-nyonya-food). **Johor** is just as
proud: a 2013 remark by a Johor figure that asam pedas is common nationwide
irritated Malaccans who make their living from it.

Within Johor the style itself splits. Southern Johor tends toward a thicker,
more robust gravy, sometimes seasoned with black pepper and pickled mustard
greens (*sawi masin*), and in places with a little pineapple; northern towns such
as Muar, Tangkak, Batu Pahat and Kluang favour a thinner, lighter broth and
typically leave the black pepper out. **Negeri Sembilan**, too, counts asam pedas
among its everyday dishes, alongside the state's better-known coconut-and-chilli
cooking.

## A cross-border dish

Asam pedas is not any single community's invention. It sits within a food
heritage shared by **Malay and Minangkabau** peoples, and its closest relative
lives across the Strait of Malacca. In West Sumatra, Indonesia, the Minangkabau
cook **asam padeh** — the same sour-spicy idea, often thicker and a deeper red,
sometimes made with beef and soured with *asam kandis* rather than only tamarind,
and a fixture of Padang restaurants. Reading the two side by side is a reminder
that the cuisines of the region flowed back and forth with the people who carried
them, something our overview of Malaysia's
[regional and ethnic cuisines](/food-lifestyle/regional-and-ethnic-cuisines)
traces more widely.

## How to eat it

Asam pedas is a gravy dish, and it wants rice. Served hot with steamed white
rice, the sourness and chilli cut cleanly through the richness of the oily fish,
and the herbs keep each spoonful from sitting heavy. It is usually a shared main
at a family table or a plate-lunch counter rather than a solo snack. For where it
fits among the country's best-loved plates, see our guide to Malaysia's
[national dishes](/food-lifestyle/national-dishes-of-malaysia).
