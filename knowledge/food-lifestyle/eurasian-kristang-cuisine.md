Order a curry in a Melaka Malay kitchen and you taste coconut and lemongrass.
Order one two streets away, in the Portuguese Settlement, and the first thing
that hits you is vinegar — sharp, almost aggressive, cut with mustard and chilli.
That tang is the fingerprint of Kristang cooking, the food of Malaysia's
Portuguese-Eurasian community, and it is the clearest sign that this table grew
from somewhere other than the rest of the peninsula.

## Who the Kristang are

Kristang cuisine cannot be separated from the people who cook it. The Kristang
are a creole community of mainly Portuguese and Malay descent, with Chinese and
Indian ancestry woven in, whose roots reach back to the Portuguese capture of
Melaka. According to Wikipedia's account, a Portuguese fleet took the city in
1511 and held it until the Dutch seized it in 1641, and the community itself
"arose in Portuguese Malacca … between the 16th and 17th centuries" as Portuguese
men married local women. Malaysia's own national culture body, the Jabatan
Kebudayaan dan Kesenian Negara (JKKN), describes the Kristang in the same terms:
"descendants of 16th-century Portuguese settlers who intermarried with local
Malay communities."

Their name comes from *Kristang*, the Portuguese-based creole language the
community speaks — now endangered, and the subject of revitalisation efforts in
both Malaysia and Singapore. The food carries the same mixed inheritance as the
language: Iberian method, Southeast Asian larder, and a strong thread of
Christian festivity that shapes what gets cooked and when. The dishes below are
best understood as the record of that intermarriage, served on a plate.

## Devil's curry (debal): the Christmas dish

If Kristang cuisine has a flag-bearer, it is **devil's curry** — *curry debal* or
*kari debal*. It is a very spicy, vinegar-sharp curry built on a pounded paste of
candlenut, galangal and mustard seed, and it owes its tang to vinegar, an
ingredient the tradition shares with Portuguese cooking. Wikipedia links the dish
directly to the Portuguese voyage, noting that "Debal Curry traces its roots to
the Goan Vindalho," the vinegar-laced curry of Portuguese Goa.

The name tells the better story. In Kristang, *debal* means "leftover," and the
curry was traditionally "made out of leftovers from the Christmas meal" — the
turkey, roast and odds and ends of a feast, simmered down with chilli and vinegar
and eaten, by the usual account, a day or two after Christmas and on other
special occasions. The English nickname "devil's curry" is widely explained as a
play on the sound of *debal* combined with the dish's ferocious heat, though that
folk etymology is worth treating as a likely story rather than settled fact.
Today most Eurasian homes make it year-round from fresh ingredients rather than
scraps.

## Feng: the offal curry

Where devil's curry is the public face, **feng** (also written *fongh*) is the
insider's dish — a slow-cooked curry of offal and off-cuts that many Kristang
families reserve for Christmas. Cooks describe it as a frugal, deeply savoury
preparation that turns trimmings, liver and other variety meats into something
rich through long simmering and a battery of spices, a thriftiness often traced
to Portuguese seafaring kitchens that wasted nothing. The precise origin,
however, is contested, and those who cook it are the first to say so: food
history here is murky, and almost every family tells the story a little
differently. That uncertainty is noted in this article's verification flags.

Feng sits alongside a cluster of other offal-based dishes in the repertoire, and
these are among the preparations reported to be fading. The *New Straits Times*,
surveying the community's cooking, noted that some households now prefer lean
pork over offal for dishes like *sebak*, and that labour-intensive specialities
are harder to find on everyday tables than they once were.

## From sugee cake to pang susi: the sweet and savoury table

Beyond the curries, the Kristang table shows its Portuguese and European side
most plainly in its baking. **Sugee cake** (sometimes *suji*) is the celebration
cake — a dense, buttery cake made with semolina rather than much flour, often
enriched with ground almonds and brandy, and traditionally wheeled out for
weddings, Christmas and other milestones. Its name is commonly traced to the
South Asian word for semolina, and the recipe is frequently compared to the
British Madeira cake, a lineage that fits the community's layered colonial
history; both points are flagged below for verification.

On the savoury side, **pang susi** is a soft bun stuffed with spiced minced meat
— a small, portable bread that reads as Iberian in form and local in seasoning.
The *New Straits Times* observed that pang susi is now "normally seen at
weddings" rather than on home Christmas tables, and that homemade versions have
grown rare. Rounding out a festive spread are dishes such as the Portuguese-style
chicken **stew** — which, in the JKKN's description, uses "crushed cream crackers
as a natural thickener" and takes "a more prominent role during festive occasions
such as Christmas, Easter, and family gatherings" — and the grilled and baked
fish, typically smothered in sambal and cooked over banana leaf, that the
Portuguese Settlement's seafood restaurants are known for.

## A fusion of four kitchens

What holds this varied table together is a method of borrowing. Kristang cooking
grafts Portuguese and broader European technique — stews and roasts, the souring
power of vinegar, European cake-baking — onto the spice pastes, chillies,
shallots, candlenut and belacan of the Malay kitchen, then absorbs touches from
the Chinese and Indian communities the Kristang married into and lived beside.
Dutch and British influence from Melaka's later rulers adds another layer. Lonely
Planet captures the result neatly: the food has Portuguese roots but is "totally
different from Portuguese food," leaning hard on chillies and shallots.

In that sense Kristang cuisine is a sibling of Malaysia's other great creole
kitchen, the Straits-born Chinese tradition explored in
[Nyonya-Peranakan cuisine](/food-lifestyle/nyonya-peranakan-cuisine) and, in its
Melaka form, in [Melaka Nyonya food](/food-lifestyle/melaka-nyonya-food). Both
arose in the same trading ports from the same habit of marrying one culture's
ingredients to another's technique. The crucial difference is the Iberian and
Christian-festive backbone: where Nyonya cooking organises itself around the
Chinese calendar and larder, the Kristang year turns on Christmas and Easter, on
pork and vinegar and cake.

## Festivals and the Portuguese Settlement

Much of this food survives because of where and when it is eaten. The living
heart of the community is the **Portuguese Settlement** near Ujong Pasir in
Melaka, which, as Wikipedia records, was "established in 1933 with the goal of
gathering the dispersed Kristang community" and is today both a residential
enclave and a draw for visitors, its Portuguese Square lined with seafood
restaurants.

The calendar matters as much as the place. The Kristang mark a string of major
saints' days at the end of June, culminating in the feast of St Peter — **San
Pedro**, patron saint of fishermen — on 29 June, when the community's fishing
boats are blessed and decorated. Christmas and Easter remain the great cooking
occasions, the moments when devil's curry, feng, sugee cake and pang susi are
most likely to appear. That reliance on festivity is a double edge: it keeps the
old dishes alive, but it also means that as home cooking contracts, some of them
are becoming special-occasion rarities rather than everyday fare. The Kristang
kitchen is, for now, one of the most distinctive corners of Malaysia's
[regional and ethnic cuisines](/food-lifestyle/regional-and-ethnic-cuisines) — a
reminder that the country's food map was drawn by its history of arrivals and
intermarriage.
