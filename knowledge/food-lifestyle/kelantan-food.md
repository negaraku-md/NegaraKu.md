Cross the Sungai Kelantan into Kota Bharu and the food changes register. The
curries turn a shade sweeter, coconut thickens almost everything, and a dark,
funky anchovy sauce called *budu* appears on the table beside a plate of raw
herbs. This is Malaysia's northeastern corner, pressed against the Thai border,
and it cooks like nowhere else in the country.

## A sweeter, Thai-touched table

Kelantan is the Malay heartland of the peninsula's northeast, a conservative,
deeply traditional state whose cuisine is among the most recognisable in
Malaysia. Two features are usually singled out. The first is sweetness: cooks
here are widely said to use sugar and coconut more freely than elsewhere, so
that dishes savoury in other states arrive with a rounder, sweeter edge. This
is a broad characterisation rather than a measured fact — the clearest
documented examples are the state's egg-and-sugar royal sweets, such as *akok*
and *jala mas* — so it is best treated as a general reputation rather than a
rule.

The second is the Thai, or more precisely Patani, accent. The Kelantanese Malays
are closely related to the Malays of southern Thailand, especially the Patani
region just across the frontier, and the two communities share dialect, dishes
and culinary vocabulary. Many Kelantan staples have a Thai-language twin — nasi
kerabu is known in the south as *khao yam* — and the exchange of ingredients and
techniques runs in both directions. How much of any single dish is "borrowed"
versus independently grown on both sides of the border is debatable and better
left to food historians than to tourist copy. The broader point is that Kelantan
belongs to its own regional world, a reminder that Malaysia's table is really a
map of many [regional and ethnic cuisines](/food-lifestyle/regional-and-ethnic-cuisines)
rather than a single [Malay cuisine](/food-lifestyle/malay-cuisine).

## Rice dishes: kerabu, dagang and the cone of tumpang

If one plate stands for Kelantan, it is
[nasi kerabu](/food-lifestyle/nasi-kerabu) — a herb rice most famous in its blue
form, the grains tinted by steeping dried butterfly-pea (*bunga telang*)
flowers, though turmeric-yellow and plain white versions also exist. The rice is
piled with a *kerabu* of finely shredded raw herbs and vegetables, dressed with
grated-coconut sambal (*sambal nyior*), fried fish or chicken, crackers, salted
egg and a spoonful of budu. It is eaten across the northeast coast and carries
real symbolic weight for Malay identity in Patani as well.

[Nasi dagang](/food-lifestyle/nasi-dagang) — a mix of rice and glutinous rice
steamed with coconut milk and fenugreek, served with a rich tuna curry — is the
other great Kelantan (and Terengganu) breakfast, a world away from the fried
anchovies of peninsular [nasi lemak](/food-lifestyle/nasi-lemak). And then there
is **nasi tumpang**, perhaps the state's most ingenious format: rice and several
layers of fillings — typically an omelette, meat or fish floss (*serunding*), a
*gulai* curry and a sweet sambal — packed into a cone of banana leaf. Folklore
holds that it was devised as a portable meal for farmers, fishermen and
travellers to carry to work, which suits its neat, unwrap-and-eat shape; the
story is traditional rather than firmly documented.

## Ayam percik and the grill

Kelantan has a strong grilling tradition, and its best-known export in this line
is **ayam percik** — chicken grilled over charcoal while being repeatedly basted
with a thick, sweet-savoury coconut-milk and chilli sauce (*percik* refers to
the splashing or flicking of the baste). The result is smoky at the edges and
creamy-spiced throughout, the sauce reduced almost to a gravy that is spooned
over the bird again at serving. It has long since travelled beyond the state to
become a Ramadan-bazaar fixture nationwide, but it reads as east-coast cooking:
coconut-rich, mild of heat and, true to the regional reputation, leaning sweet.

## Solok lada, singgang and the fish kitchen

Facing the South China Sea, Kelantan is a fish-and-seafood kitchen at heart.
**Solok lada** is a characteristic side — large green chillies hollowed out and
stuffed with a filling of pounded fish and grated coconut, then simmered in a
light coconut gravy. (Sources, including recipe references, differ on whether the
stuffing is primarily fish or young coconut, so expect variation from cook to
cook.) It often appears alongside nasi kerabu.

**Singgang** is a plainer, older preparation: a clear, sour-savoury fish broth,
usually of mackerel, simmered with aromatics such as lemongrass, turmeric and
shallots and finished with a squeeze of lime — a light counterpoint to the
heavier coconut dishes around it. Together, stuffed chillies and a clean fish
soup show the other half of the Kelantan palate: not everything is sweet or rich,
and the sea is never far from the plate.

## Budu: the fermented soul of the table

No ingredient defines the Kelantan and wider east-coast table more than **budu**.
It is a fermented anchovy sauce made by salting small fish and leaving them to
break down over months — reference sources cite a window of roughly 140 to 200
days, though the exact figure is uncertain — into a dark, intensely savoury
liquid. The sauce is shared across Kelantan, Terengganu and the Malay provinces
of southern Thailand (Pattani, Yala, Narathiwat), a neat illustration of the
cross-border foodway.

Budu is used both as a cooking seasoning and, more memorably, as a dip. In its
classic form it is stirred with lime juice, sliced shallots, bird's-eye chilli
and a little sugar, then eaten with hot rice, grilled or fried fish and a pile of
raw *ulam* (herbs and vegetables). It is also the sauce that makes nasi kerabu
taste the way it should. For the uninitiated its smell can be startling; for
Kelantanese it is the flavour of home.

## Laksam, and the deep world of kuih

Among noodle dishes, Kelantan's own is **laksam** — broad, flat sheets of
rice-flour noodle rolled up and sliced, then bathed in a pale, thick gravy of
pounded fish and coconut milk and topped with shredded raw herbs and vegetables.
It is mild, soft and comforting, closer to a gentle rice-noodle dish than to the
sharp, sour laksa of Penang, and it is a staple of the morning market.

Kelantan also has one of Malaysia's richest repertoires of **kuih** —
traditional cakes and snacks. The sugar-and-egg royal sweets *akok* (baked in
small moulds) and *jala mas* ("golden net", egg yolk trailed into sweet syrup)
sit alongside countless coconut-and-palm-sugar steamed and griddled cakes. This
dense sweet-snack culture is itself part of the evidence for the state's
sweet-leaning reputation, even if a formal culinary study would be needed to pin
the generalisation down.

## Kota Bharu: eating at the market

To eat Kelantan properly is to eat at a market, and the state capital, Kota
Bharu, is built around them. The landmark is **Pasar Besar Siti Khadijah**, a
multi-storey central market whose produce floors and cooked-food stalls are
famously staffed in large part by women traders — a detail worth confirming
locally but one that recurs in nearly every description of the place. Here the
dishes above are sold to take away: cones of nasi tumpang stacked by the dozen,
tubs of budu, trays of kuih in the morning, and blue nasi kerabu wrapped and
ready. The market, as much as any single recipe, is where the sweet, Thai-edged
character of Kelantan cooking comes together in one noisy, fragrant place.
