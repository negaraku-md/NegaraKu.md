Walk into a mamak stall at dawn, or a banana-leaf restaurant at lunch, and
somewhere on the griddle a thin disc of pale batter is hissing into a crisp,
golden crepe. Fold it, tear a piece, drag it through coconut chutney or a pool
of dhal, and you have eaten thosai — one of the most familiar breakfasts in
Malaysia, and one of the oldest tricks in South Indian cooking.

## What thosai is

Thosai is a thin, savoury crepe made from a fermented batter of rice and urad
dal, the split black gram that Tamil cooks call *ulundu*. It is the same dish
that most of India knows as **dosa** (or *dosai*); the spelling *thosai* is the
one generally used in Malaysian and Singaporean English, while *tosai* is the
Malay form. All three names trace back to the Tamil *tōcai* and describe a
crepe "made from fermented lentils and rice originating from South Indian
cuisine," as Wiktionary's entry puts it.

In Malaysia the dish belongs squarely to
[Indian-Malaysian cuisine](/food-lifestyle/indian-malaysian-cuisine). By most
accounts it arrived with Tamil migrants who came to British Malaya as estate and
railway labour, and it has since become a shared staple eaten well beyond the
Indian-Malaysian community. A heritage page from the Jabatan Kebudayaan dan
Kesenian Negara (JKKN) describes tosai as "a traditional dish made from
fermented rice and *ulundu* beans" that "originated in South India and was
brought to Malaysia by the Tamil community" — though, as with many migration
stories, the exact period and route are not firmly documented.

## The fermented batter

What separates thosai from an ordinary pancake is fermentation. Rice and urad
dal are soaked separately, ground to a smooth batter, mixed with a little salt,
and then left to stand — usually overnight — so that wild fermentation can do
its work. Wikipedia's account of the dosa batter is characteristically plain:
"After adding salt, the batter is allowed to ferment overnight, before being
mixed with water to get the desired consistency."

That resting period is where the flavour comes from. The urad dal helps the
batter trap air and lightens the texture, while the fermentation gives thosai
its gentle sourness — a tang that is subtle in a fresh batch and sharper in an
older one. A properly fermented batter is what lets a thin thosai turn crisp and
lacy at the edges rather than merely dry. Most Malaysian versions keep the batter
simple: rice, urad dal, water and salt, sometimes with a little fenugreek, and
without the coconut milk that enriches some other local breads.

## The griddle: thin, crisp and soft at once

Cooking thosai is a quick, practised motion. A ladle of batter is poured onto a
hot, lightly oiled flat griddle (or *tawa*) and spread outward in a widening
spiral, so the disc thins to the edge. SBS Food's recipe describes the ideal as
a crepe that is "crispy outside and soft inside," not unlike a French crêpe.
Within a minute or two the underside turns golden and lacy; the thosai is then
folded — in half, into a triangle, or rolled — and lifted off.

The thinner the spread, the crisper the result, which is the whole idea behind
**paper thosai** (see below). The technique is cousin to the showmanship of
[roti canai](/food-lifestyle/roti-canai) next to it on the same mamak griddle,
though the two breads could hardly be more different: roti canai is a layered,
stretched wheat flatbread, while thosai is a poured, fermented rice crepe.

## A family of thosai

Thosai is less one item than a short menu. The common forms include:

- **Thosai kosong** — plain thosai, *kosong* meaning "empty" in Malay. The
  everyday version, served with chutney, sambar and dhal and nothing inside.
- **Masala thosai** — a crisper, often larger thosai wrapped around a filling of
  spiced mashed potato cooked with onion, mustard seed and turmeric. This is the
  one many people picture first.
- **Egg thosai** — an egg cracked and spread over the batter as it cooks on the
  griddle, giving a richer, fluffier crepe.
- **Ghee or butter thosai** — cooked with ghee or butter instead of oil, for a
  nuttier flavour and extra crispness.
- **Rava thosai** — made with *rava* (semolina), which gives a lacier, more
  brittle texture and a distinct nutty taste; it is typically mixed fresh rather
  than fermented.
- **Paper thosai** — spread extremely thin and cooked very crisp, then served
  huge, often folded into a cone or rolled into a long tube. It is as much
  spectacle as dish.

Some stalls also offer a **podi thosai**, dusted or smeared with *podi* — a dry
spice powder of ground lentils and chilli — and a growing roster of sweet and
novelty versions. The exact line-up varies from stall to stall, and newer
flavoured thosai come and go.

## Chutney, sambar and dhal

A thosai is rarely eaten alone. The classic trio of accompaniments is **coconut
chutney**, **sambar** (a spiced lentil-and-vegetable broth soured with tamarind)
and **dhal** (lentil curry). The JKKN heritage page lists coconut sambal,
chutney and dhal curry among the usual partners, and a plain thosai kosong leans
entirely on these for its flavour. Many restaurants also set out additional
chutneys — mint, tomato or onion — so that a single thosai can be tasted several
ways across one plate.

## Banana-leaf tables and mamak stalls

Thosai turns up in two overlapping settings. The first is the South Indian
vegetarian and
[banana-leaf restaurant](/food-lifestyle/banana-leaf-rice), where it is a
morning and tea-time fixture alongside idli and vadai. The second is the mamak
stall — the Indian-Muslim eateries at the heart of
[kopitiam and mamak culture](/food-lifestyle/kopitiam-mamak-culture) — where
thosai shares the griddle with roti canai and is served from early morning until
late at night. The JKKN page notes that tosai is "often served as breakfast,
especially in Indian restaurants in Malaysia, known as" mamak restaurants, which
is why it sits so comfortably on any list of
[Malaysian breakfast](/food-lifestyle/malaysian-breakfast) staples.

## A note on spelling

Few Malaysian dishes are spelt as many ways. *Thosai*, *tosai*, *dosai*, *dosa*
and even *tose* all appear on signboards and menus, reflecting the journey from
the Tamil *tōcai* into Malay and English. The question of a single standard Malay
form is genuinely unsettled: when the national language authority weighed in, its
preferred spelling drew public debate rather than a quiet consensus. For a reader,
the practical point is simple — thosai, tosai and dosa name the same crisp,
fermented crepe.
