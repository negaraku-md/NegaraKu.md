Ask a Malaysian what makes a meal taste like home and the answer is often not a
dish at all but a spoonful of chilli on the side. Sambal — the red, glistening
relish that sits beside the rice, the fish, the egg — is the thing people reach
for first and miss most when they are away. It can be a raw, nose-prickling dip
or a slow-fried paste sweet enough to eat by the spoonful, and almost no two
cooks make it quite the same way.

## The chilli at the centre of the table

Sambal is best understood not as a single recipe but as a whole category of
chilli-based sauces and pastes used across maritime Southeast Asia, Malaysia
very much among them. At its simplest it is chilli ground with salt; at its most
elaborate it carries a dozen aromatics and a fermented seafood base. What unites
the forms is a role rather than a formula: sambal is the condiment, flavouring
and side dish that supplies the heat, salt and savoury depth a plate of rice and
plainer cooking needs.

Where it came from is not firmly settled. The relish is generally placed in
maritime Southeast Asia, and the word is widely traced to Javanese roots, but
reputable references describe the exact emergence of sambal as uncertain and
label it, depending on the source, as "Indonesian" or "Malaysian." Rather than
claim a single birthplace, it is more accurate to say sambal is a shared
regional idea that each kitchen — and each household — has made its own. In
Malaysia it threads through [the national dishes](/food-lifestyle/national-dishes-of-malaysia)
and everyday meals alike.

## What goes into a sambal

Strip a Malaysian sambal back to its essentials and a short list of ingredients
recurs. **Chilli** is the constant — fresh red chillies for colour and body,
small bird's-eye chillies (*cili padi*) for sharp heat, or dried chillies
soaked and ground for a deeper, mellower paste. Around it cluster the aromatics:
**shallots** and **garlic**, often **lemongrass** and **galangal**, pounded or
blended into the chilli.

The ingredient that gives Malaysian sambal its unmistakable savour is
**belacan**, a dense fermented shrimp paste. Toasted or fried before use to tame
its raw smell and draw out its aroma, it lends a deep, briny, umami backbone that
chilli alone cannot. Balance comes from an acid — **calamansi lime** juice or
**tamarind** (*asam jawa*) — and sweetness from sugar or, in richer styles,
**gula melaka** (palm sugar). The grinding itself matters: traditionally the
ingredients are pounded in a stone mortar (*batu lesung*) or on a grinding stone
(*batu giling*), which bruises rather than purées them and gives the paste its
texture.

## Sambal belacan: the raw, pounded original

The most elemental Malaysian sambal is **sambal belacan**, and it is raw. Fresh
red chillies and a knob of toasted belacan are pounded together with a little
salt and sugar, then sharpened with a squeeze of calamansi lime. The result is a
coarse, pungent, fiery paste served as a dip — for *ulam* (raw herbs and
vegetables), fried fish, boiled eggs or simply alongside rice.

It is the sambal that most directly announces belacan's presence, which is why
the two names are so often spoken together. Variations abound: some cooks add
diced tomato or green mango for tartness, others grill the chillies first for a
smoky edge. A common Chinese-Malaysian version fries the belacan with the chilli
rather than leaving the paste raw. However it is made, sambal belacan is the
benchmark against which the cooked sambals are measured.

## Sambal tumis and sambal ikan bilis: the fried workhorses

Where sambal belacan is raw, **sambal tumis** is cooked — and it is the version
most Malaysians eat most often. *Tumis* means to stir-fry or sauté, and the
method is exactly that: a ground chilli paste (fresh or dried chilli with
shallots, garlic and belacan) is fried patiently in oil until the raw smell
cooks off, the paste splits from the oil and it darkens to a deep, glossy red.
Sugar or gula melaka rounds it out; tamarind keeps it from cloying.

Its most famous outing is **sambal tumis ikan bilis**, the sweet-spicy sambal
studded with crisp dried anchovies (*ikan bilis*) that is inseparable from
[nasi lemak](/food-lifestyle/nasi-lemak). The anchovies are usually fried
separately and folded in near the end so they keep some bite, and onion rings or
tamarind often go in too. A plate of nasi lemak lives or dies on this sambal's
balance of sweet, hot and savoury. The same base fries up into many other dishes
— a spoonful stirred through fried rice or noodles, for instance — which makes
sambal tumis the quiet engine of a great deal of Malaysian cooking.

## Beyond the basics: green, prawn, squid and durian

From those two poles — raw and fried — the family fans out. **Sambal hijau**
(green sambal) swaps red chilli for green, pounded with green bird's-eye chillies
and sometimes tomatillo-tart green tomato for a fresher, grassier heat. **Sambal
udang** simmers prawns in a rich chilli-tamarind paste until they are coated and
glossy, a festive favourite; **sambal sotong** does the same with squid. In the
peninsula's interior, **sambal tempoyak** blends chilli with *tempoyak* —
fermented, soured [durian](/food-lifestyle/durian-culture-and-varieties) — for a
funky, tangy relish especially associated with Pahang and Perak; it can be eaten
raw or cooked down with fish.

Some sambals on Malaysian tables arrive from across the region. **Sambal matah**
is a raw, uncooked relish of Balinese origin — sliced shallots, lemongrass,
chilli and kaffir lime leaf, sometimes finished with hot oil poured over to just
soften them — and it has found its way onto Malaysian menus as a fresh, crunchy
counterpoint to the cooked local styles. (*Matah* means "raw" in Balinese.)

## Sambal across Malaysia's kitchens

Sambal is not the property of any one community. In **Malay** cooking it is the
default accompaniment to rice, grilled fish and *ulam*, and the heart of dishes
like *ayam masak merah*. In **Nyonya** (Peranakan) kitchens, sambal pastes
underpin curries and the belacan-laced dishes that define Straits Chinese
cooking. Malaysian **Indian** and *mamak* stalls fry their own chilli sambals for
*mee goreng*, fried fish and the bright red relish served with *roti*. The same
instinct — a pounded chilli base, adjusted to taste — runs through all three.

Region shapes it, too. Coastal communities lean hardest on belacan and dried
seafood; inland Pahang gives us tempoyak-based sambals and the dark *sambal hitam*
soured with *belimbing*; and every state, town and family keeps its own
proportions of heat, salt and sweetness. Sambal is, in that sense, a map of
Malaysia's [regional and ethnic cuisines](/food-lifestyle/regional-and-ethnic-cuisines)
drawn in chilli.

## The common thread

For all its variety, sambal comes down to one idea executed countless ways:
chilli, made more than the sum of itself by pounding it with aromatics and a
savoury, sour and sweet counterpoint. Raw or fried, red or green, with anchovies
or prawns or durian, it is the element that turns rice and simple cooking into a
Malaysian meal — the small dish that carries the biggest flavour.
