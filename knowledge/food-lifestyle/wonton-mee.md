Order wonton mee in Kuala Lumpur and you get a small mound of glossy egg noodles
tossed in dark sauce, a few slices of barbecued pork fanned across the top, a
sprig of greens — and the wontons arrive *separately*, bobbing in their own little
bowl of soup. Order the same dish elsewhere and everything may come together in a
single bowl of broth. The noodle is the constant; how it is served is where the
regional personality shows.

## A Cantonese noodle with a Malaysian accent

Wonton mee — spelled *wantan mee*, *wan tan mee* or *wonton noodles*, among other
variants — is a dish of Cantonese origin. The name comes from the dumpling: *wonton*
transliterates the Chinese *húntún* (餛飩), a term in use since the Tang Dynasty,
though food writers disagree on when the noodle dish as we know it took shape, so
its precise age is best treated as uncertain.

The dish reached the peninsula with Chinese migration. Malaysia's national
cultural-mapping entry attributes it to the Cantonese and Hakka communities who
came to Malaya in the 19th century during British rule, many of whom worked as tin
miners and traders. Over generations it settled into a local idiom — above all the
dry, soy-dressed style now sold at hawker stalls across the country — and took its
place in the wider world of
[Malaysian noodles](/food-lifestyle/malaysian-noodles).

## What's in the bowl

At its core, wonton mee is built on thin, springy egg noodles. Good noodles are
the whole point: cooks advise using egg noodles that stay "springy and have a good
bite after cooking," which is why they are blanched briefly and tossed or served
at once rather than left to soften.

The standard toppings are slices of **char siu** — Cantonese barbecued pork,
glazed and slightly caramelised — and a little leafy green, usually a few stalks of
*choy sum* or *kai lan* (Chinese broccoli). Then come the **wontons** themselves:
small dumplings traditionally filled with minced pork and prawn, sometimes with
spring onion, either boiled or deep-fried. The combination of noodle, sweet-savoury
pork, green and dumpling is what defines the dish, however it is plated.

## The dry style: Kuala Lumpur and the south

The form most Malaysians picture is the **dry** style — *kon lou* ("tossed dry") in
Cantonese. Here the cooked noodles are tossed in a seasoning of thick dark soy and
regular soy with sesame oil, garlic oil and a little sugar, then enriched with
oyster or mushroom sauce until every strand is glossed and faintly sweet. The char
siu is laid on top and the greens alongside.

Crucially, the soup does not go over the noodles. Instead, as food accounts of the
Malaysian style describe it, the noodles are served "relatively dry," dressed with
oyster sauce, while the wontons sit in a small bowl of soup on the side. You eat the
noodles tangled and glistening, then spoon up the clear broth and its dumplings in
between. This dry-plus-side-soup presentation is strongly associated with Kuala
Lumpur and much of the peninsula, and it is a fixture of the
[kopitiam and mamak](/food-lifestyle/kopitiam-mamak-culture) breakfast table.

## The soup style

The older, simpler presentation is the **soup** style: the egg noodles, char siu
and wontons all together in a bowl of clear, usually pork- or chicken-based broth,
finished with spring onion. It is lighter and more restrained than the dry version —
closer to the Hong Kong and Guangzhou template of egg noodles and wontons in soup —
and many stalls will serve the dish either way on request. Where the dry style
foregrounds the caramel of the dark-soy dressing, the soup style lets the broth and
the noodle's egg flavour lead.

## Pickled green chillies and other condiments

Few things are as inseparable from wonton mee as the dish of **pickled green
chillies** set beside it. Sliced green chillies are steeped in vinegar with a little
salt and sugar — made a day ahead, by one hawker-style recipe — and served on the
side for diners to add to taste; the same recipe signs off by telling you to serve
the noodles "with pickled green chillies and a bowl of wantan soup." Their sharp,
vinegary heat cuts the richness of the char siu and the soy dressing, and they are
a near-universal accompaniment at Malaysian stalls. A dollop of chilli sauce or
*sambal* is often offered too, and some regional versions lean on it heavily.

## Regional styles: Penang, Ipoh and Sarawak

Like most Malaysian hawker staples, wonton mee shifts from town to town. Penang's
dry version is often described as darker and a touch sweeter, and some stalls there
serve it with the state's trademark *sambal belacan* on the side rather than plain
pickled chillies — though descriptions vary across sources and are worth checking
locally. Ipoh, a town famous for its silky noodles and smooth-skinned char siu, has
its own well-regarded following.

East Malaysia complicates the picture. Sarawak is better known for *kolo mee* — a
dry egg-noodle dish that is a close cousin but conventionally treated as its own
thing — and the relationship between Sarawakian wonton-noodle variants and kolo mee
is easily blurred, so Sarawak's take is best confirmed with a local source. These
town-by-town differences are part of the broader map of Malaysia's
[regional and ethnic cuisines](/food-lifestyle/regional-and-ethnic-cuisines).

## Pork, halal and vegetarian versions

By tradition, wonton mee is a pork dish: the char siu is barbecued pork and the
wontons are filled with pork and prawn, so the classic bowl is not halal. This is
stated here as a matter of ingredients, not of preference — both pork and
non-pork versions are an established part of the food landscape.

Because the dish is so popular, Muslim-run stalls across Malaysia sell halal
versions that swap the pork for chicken "char siu" and use chicken or prawn wontons,
keeping the noodles, sauce and pickled chillies the same. Vegetarian renditions also
exist, substituting wheat gluten or mushrooms for the barbecued pork. As ever at a
hawker stall, the surest guide is to ask the cook — the same good practice that
applies to pork-or-halal dishes like
[char kway teow](/food-lifestyle/char-kway-teow).
