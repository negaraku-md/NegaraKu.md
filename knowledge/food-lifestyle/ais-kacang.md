On a hot Malaysian afternoon, few things pull a crowd like the whir of an
ice-shaving machine. Out comes a snow-white mound of ice, teetering over a bowl
already hiding beans, corn and jellies, finished with ribbons of bright red
syrup and a slow drizzle of condensed milk. This is ais kacang — or ABC — a
dessert that looks like a small, edible mountain and tastes like the national
answer to tropical heat.

## What ais kacang (ABC) is

Ais kacang is a shaved-ice dessert common across Malaysia, and also eaten in
Singapore and Brunei. At its heart it is simple: finely shaved ice piled into a
tall cone over a layer of sweet, chewy toppings, then flooded with coloured
syrups and milk so that each spoonful mixes ice, bean, jelly and sweetness
together.

The ice is the stage; the toppings are the show. Underneath the mound you will
usually find red beans, sweet corn and some combination of grass jelly, attap
chee and agar cubes. Over the top go rose syrup, palm-sugar syrup and
evaporated or condensed milk. As one recipe from Australia's SBS puts it, it is
a "thirst-quenching dessert" — and that is exactly how Malaysians treat it,
less a formal pudding than a cooling snack eaten standing at a stall.

## The name and what it means

The two names describe the same bowl from two angles. *Ais kacang* means
roughly "bean ice" in Malay — a nod to the dish's earliest form, which was
little more than shaved ice heaped over red beans. *ABC* is short for *air batu
campur*, which translates as "mixed ice": *air batu* (literally "stone water")
is the Malay term for ice, and *campur* means "mixed". Both names are used
interchangeably, and you will hear a vendor answer to either.

The dish's exact origins are debated. Food historians generally place its rise
in the early twentieth century, once ice became cheap enough for street
vendors; some accounts trace early shaved-ice versions to Singapore around the
First World War, with the familiar mounded form appearing by the 1940s. Other
popular food writing instead credits a Peranakan origin in Malacca. These
accounts conflict, and no single originator is firmly documented — so the
history is best treated as approximate.

## The layers and toppings

What makes ais kacang endlessly variable is that almost nothing in it is fixed
except the ice. A classic bowl layers a handful of chewy, jelly-like
components, then relies on syrup and milk to tie everything together. The table
below sets out the toppings you are most likely to meet.

| Topping | What it is | Role in the bowl |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Red beans (kacang merah) | Sweetened adzuki beans | The original topping; the source of the name |
| Sweet corn | Whole kernels or creamed corn | Natural sweetness and body |
| Grass jelly (cincau) | Dark, slightly bitter herb jelly | Soft texture; considered "cooling" |
| Attap chee | Translucent immature fruit of the nipah palm | Mild sweetness, chewy bite |
| Agar jellies | Cubes of coloured agar-agar | Texture and bright colour |
| Roasted peanuts | Crushed or whole | Crunch and nuttiness |
| Rose syrup / sarsi | Bright red (or sarsaparilla) syrup | Signature colour and perfume |
| Gula melaka | Palm-sugar syrup | Caramel-like, smoky depth |
| Evaporated / condensed milk | Drizzled over the ice | Richness and creaminess |

Not every bowl carries every item. Some Malaysian stalls skip the coloured
syrups altogether and lean only on palm-sugar syrup for a darker, more caramel
profile; others pile on sago pearls, nata de coco or aloe vera. The ice itself
is produced by an ice-shaving machine — hand-cranked in the old days, now
usually motorised — which is why the texture is soft and snow-like rather than
crushed and crunchy.

## Where you'll eat it — hawkers and pasar malam

Ais kacang is street food at heart. You will find it in kopitiam (coffee
shops), hawker centres and food courts, and it is a reliable fixture at the
[pasar malam & street food](/food-lifestyle/pasar-malam-and-street-food) night
markets, where a vendor shaves each bowl to order. Prices are modest — typically
a few ringgit a bowl — which is part of its appeal as an everyday treat rather
than a special-occasion dessert.

Because the dish is assembled by hand, every stall has its own signature:
the ratio of beans to corn, how generous the milk is, whether the syrup leans
floral or caramel. Long-running stalls — such as heritage ais kacang vendors in
Penang — build loyal followings precisely because regulars know exactly how
their bowl will taste. Part of the fun is that two bowls from two stalls, both
called ais kacang, can look and taste noticeably different.

## Variations: durian, ice cream and regional twists

The mound is a canvas, and stalls decorate it freely. A scoop of ice cream on
top is common at modern stalls, as are crushed peanuts for crunch. In durian
country, a stall may crown the ice with fresh [durian](/food-lifestyle/durian-culture-and-varieties)
or durian purée, turning a light snack into something far richer. Cafés have
taken the idea further still, folding in fruit like mango, strawberry or
lychee, or flavourings such as Milo.

Regional habits shade the recipe too. Some accounts describe Johor stalls
leaning on smoky gula Melaka, and Sabah and Sarawak versions served with sago
pearls and mango — though these regional specifics come from limited sources and
are worth confirming locally. What stays constant is the format: a cold mountain
of shaved ice over a shifting cast of sweet, chewy things.

## Ais kacang vs cendol

Ais kacang is often mentioned in the same breath as cendol, and many stalls
sell both, but they are different desserts. Cendol is defined by its green,
worm-like jelly made from rice flour, served low in a bowl of coconut milk and
gula melaka, sometimes with red beans added. Ais kacang, by contrast, is built
upward — a tall cone of shaved ice over a mix of beans, corn, jellies and
syrups, finished with milk. Put simply: cendol is about the green jelly and
coconut milk, while ais kacang is about the mountain of ice and its many
toppings.
