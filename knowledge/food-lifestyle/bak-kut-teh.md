Few dishes announce themselves quite like bak kut teh. Before you see it, you smell
it: a dark, fragrant cloud of star anise, cinnamon and simmering pork drifting out of
a coffeeshop long before dawn has fully broken. In Malaysia, this bowl of ribs and
broth is more than breakfast — it is a Hokkien comfort food with a disputed history,
two rival styles, and, since 2024, a place on the nation's official heritage list.

## What it is, and the name

At its core, bak kut teh is pork — ribs and other cuts — simmered for hours in a broth
built from Chinese herbs and spices. A typical pot draws on star anise, cinnamon,
cloves, fennel seed, garlic, white pepper and medicinal herbs such as *dong quai*
(angelica) and codonopsis, rounded out with light and dark soy sauce. The meat turns
tender, the broth turns deep and savoury, and the whole thing is garnished with
coriander, spring onion and fried shallots.

The name is where newcomers stumble. "Bak kut teh" is Hokkien — written 肉骨茶 and
romanised *bah-kut-tê* — and it translates literally as "meat bone tea." Yet there is
no tea in the pot. The "tea" has two common explanations, neither fully settled: the
herbal broth itself resembles a medicinal tea, and, just as importantly, a pot of
strong Chinese tea is almost always brewed and served alongside the meal to cut
through the richness of the pork. Either way, the name stuck, and today it is one of
the most recognisable Chinese-Malaysian dishes in the country.

## Klang origins and the dock-worker story

Ask where bak kut teh comes from and you will start an argument — both Malaysia and
Singapore claim it. Within Malaysia, however, the answer is far less contested: **Klang**,
the old royal and port town in Selangor, is widely credited as the dish's Malaysian
home, and locals there treat it as a point of civic pride.

The most popular origin story is a working-class one. By this account, the dish grew
up among Chinese labourers around Port Klang in the early twentieth century, who needed
cheap, warming, energy-dense food to sustain long days of heavy physical work. Pork
bones and offcuts were inexpensive; herbs added nourishment and the comforting weight
of a tonic. Other versions credit a local *sinseh* (traditional physician) who brewed
the herbal recipe in the 1930s or 1940s, sometimes said to have carried it from Fujian.
These are folk histories rather than documented records, and they should be read as
tradition, not settled fact — but they all point back to the same place and the same
community of Hokkien migrants.

## Herbal versus peppery: the two main styles

Most of the variety in bak kut teh comes down to two broad styles, usually linked to
two Chinese dialect groups. The **Hokkien** style is the dark, herbal one most
associated with Klang: a robust, soy-rich, deeply fragrant broth. The **Teochew**
style is lighter in colour and clearer, leaning hard on white pepper and garlic for a
sharp, warming kick rather than herbal depth — the version that came to dominate in
Singapore. (Cooks also recognise a more medicinal Cantonese variant, and Klang is now
known for a "dry" bak kut teh, where the gravy is reduced to a thick, tangy glaze with
dried chillies, dates and squid.)

| Style | Broth | Associated region / community |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Herbal / dark (Hokkien) | Dark brown, rich with soy sauce and Chinese medicinal herbs; thick and savoury | Klang, Selangor — the Malaysian heartland |
| Peppery / clear (Teochew) | Pale, clear broth driven by white pepper and garlic; lighter but sharp | Singapore and southern Peninsula |
| Dry (Klang variant) | Reduced to a thick, tangy gravy with dried chillies, dates and squid | Klang, Selangor (a later innovation) |

Neither style is "correct" — they are parallel traditions, and many Malaysians have a
firm favourite.

## How it's eaten

Bak kut teh is a shared, sit-down meal, usually eaten for breakfast or lunch rather
than dinner. The broth and meat arrive in a claypot or bowl, almost always with a bowl
of plain white **rice** on the side to soak it up. Torn pieces of **you char kway** —
the fried dough fritter also known as *youtiao* — are dipped straight into the soup so
they drink up the broth.

A small saucer of **dark soy sauce** studded with sliced *chilli padi* and minced
garlic sits on the table for dipping the meat. And then there is the **tea**: a pot of
Chinese tea, often a roasted oolong such as *tieguanyin*, poured throughout the meal
and believed to help dilute the fat. Diners frequently order extra pots of ingredients
on the side — mushrooms, tofu puffs, leafy greens, offal — to drop into the soup. Much
of the pleasure is in the lingering: refilling the tea, topping up the broth, and
working slowly through the ribs. Like the broader world of
[kopitiam and mamak culture](/food-lifestyle/kopitiam-mamak-culture), it is as much a
social ritual as a meal.

## A note on halal

Bak kut teh is a pork dish, and that fact matters in a Muslim-majority country. Because
it is built around pork ribs and bones, it is **not halal**, and Muslims — who make up
the largest share of Malaysia's population — do not eat it. It is cooked and sold in
Chinese coffeeshops and restaurants rather than halal-certified premises, and you will
not find it at a Ramadan bazaar or a mosque canteen.

This is simply a feature of Malaysia's plural food landscape, where
[halal certification](/food-lifestyle/halal-certification-in-malaysia) clearly marks
which kitchens Muslim diners can use, and non-halal dishes occupy their own well-known
spaces. For those who do not eat pork but want the herbal-soup experience, some cooks
prepare a chicken version, occasionally nicknamed "chik kut teh" — a separate dish
rather than a halal-certified bak kut teh.

## Heritage recognition

Bak kut teh's status as a Malaysian dish became a national conversation in 2023. In
June that year, a member of Parliament urged the government to recognise it as a
national heritage food, partly in response to suggestions abroad that the dish might be
Singaporean. The proposal drew debate in the Dewan Rakyat, with some MPs questioning
whether a pork dish eaten mainly by one community could be called a shared national
food.

The outcome came the following year. In **February 2024**, the National Heritage
Department gazetted bak kut teh as a **national heritage food**, naming it as one of ten
food items declared that month. The declaration sits within the framework of the
**National Heritage Act 2005 (Act 645)**, the same law under which the department
recognises dishes as objects of national heritage through a notice in the
[Government Gazette](/glossary/warta-kerajaan). The recognition is one of several
official acknowledgements of Malaysian food, alongside the country's broader push for
[national and UNESCO heritage](/food-lifestyle/malaysian-food-as-national-and-unesco-heritage)
status for its cuisine.
