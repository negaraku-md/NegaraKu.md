Few sounds say East Coast Malaysia quite like the hiss of chicken over charcoal
being splashed with a thick, pale sauce. That splash is the whole point: *percik*
is Malay for to splash or splatter, and ayam percik takes its name from the spiced
coconut sauce that cooks repeatedly flick and spoon over the meat as it grills.
The result is chicken with caramelised, smoky edges and a rich, fragrant coating —
rustic food that has travelled from Kelantan night markets to menus across the
country.

## What ayam percik is

At its simplest, ayam percik is grilled chicken with a coconut-based basting
sauce. It is a Malay speciality most closely tied to the **East Coast**, and
especially to **Kelantan**, where Kota Bharu is widely described as its home; a
closely related grilling tradition is also found in neighbouring **Terengganu**.
It sits firmly within the wider world of
[Malay cuisine](/food-lifestyle/malay-cuisine), where grilling, coconut milk
and aromatic spice pastes are everyday building blocks.

The dish is defined less by a fixed recipe than by a technique. Chicken —
usually bone-in pieces or a whole spatchcocked bird — is marinated, then grilled
slowly over charcoal while the sauce is applied again and again. Each basting
layer sets against the fire, so the chicken builds up flavour and colour rather
than simply cooking through.

## How it is cooked

A good ayam percik starts with a marinade, often left on overnight, and some
cooks part-cook or braise the chicken before it meets the grill so the meat stays
tender. The grilling itself is done over charcoal for that smoky fragrance, with
the cook working a brush or spoon to keep the surface wet with sauce.

The sauce is the signature. It is built on **coconut milk** and a blend of
aromatics and spice, giving a coating that is creamy, savoury and gently sweet.
Exact ingredients vary from stall to stall and household to household, which is
part of why no two versions taste quite the same — there is no single canonical
formula.

## White, red and regional styles

Ayam percik is commonly sorted by the **colour of its sauce**. A **white (putih)**
style leaves out chilli paste, so it is milder and creamier, leaning on coconut
and aromatics. A **red (merah)** style takes its colour and heat from chilli,
giving a spicier, deeper-hued coating. Both rest on the same coconut-milk base.

These two styles are most often described in a Kelantan context rather than split
cleanly between states, and a tidy Kelantan-white-versus-Terengganu-red mapping is
not firmly established — East Coast cooks make both. Treat the colour simply as a
guide to how much chilli to expect.

## Where and how to eat it

Ayam percik is street and market food. It is a mainstay of **night markets**
(*pasar malam*) and, above all, of
[Ramadan bazaars](/food-lifestyle/ramadan-bazaars-and-fasting-month-food),
where rows of chicken smoke over open grills in the late afternoon before the
breaking of the fast. From its East Coast heartland it has spread to Malay stalls
and restaurants nationwide.

It is usually eaten hot off the grill with rice, and on East Coast menus it often
turns up near other regional specialities — most famously the blue-tinged
[nasi kerabu](/food-lifestyle/nasi-kerabu), the herb-and-rice salad that
shares its Kelantan roots. Whether as a bazaar snack or a plated meal, the appeal
is the same: smoky charcoal chicken under a sauce that was, quite literally,
splashed on.
