Lift the lid off a pot of coconut rice in a Malaysian kitchen and the first thing
you notice is not the rice but a scent: sweet, green, faintly like vanilla and
new-mown grass at once. That smell almost always comes from a few long, blade-like
leaves knotted and tucked into the pot — *daun pandan*. If vanilla is the quiet
background note of Western baking, pandan plays the same role across much of
Southeast Asia, and nowhere more than in Malaysia's sweets.

## The leaf and its grassy-vanilla scent

Pandan is the leaf of *Pandanus amaryllifolius*, a member of the screwpine genus
*Pandanus*, known in Malay as *daun pandan* (literally "pandan leaf"). The plant
grows as a clump of strap-shaped, bright green leaves and is cultivated across the
region for the kitchen rather than for any fruit. Culinary writers reach so often
for the comparison that pandan is widely nicknamed the "vanilla of Southeast Asia"
— a flavour analogy, since the two plants are entirely unrelated, but a useful one:
its fragrance reads as a blend of fresh-cut grass, a hint of vanilla and a little
coconut.

That aroma is commonly attributed to the compound **2-acetyl-1-pyrroline** (2AP),
the same molecule associated with the smell of jasmine and basmati rice and of
freshly baked bread. It is worth treating the single-compound story with some
caution: 2AP is credited with pandan's *characteristic* scent, but the leaf
contains many volatile compounds, and the full flavour is not reducible to one
molecule — the exact chemistry is best confirmed against an analytical source
rather than food media. What is not in doubt is the effect: a little goes a long
way, and a knotted leaf can perfume a whole pot.

## How cooks use it

Three techniques cover most of pandan's work in the kitchen. The simplest is to
knot whole leaves and drop them into rice or coconut milk (*santan*) as they cook,
then fish them out before serving — the leaf gives up its aroma to the pot and is
discarded. This is how pandan flavours coconut rice and many Malay rice dishes.

The second is to blend the leaves with a little water and strain the pulp into a
bright green **pandan juice**. Left to stand, the juice separates, with a dark
green sediment settling at the bottom; this concentrated extract is stirred into
batters and custards as both a flavouring and a natural colouring. The third is to
wrap leaves around food before cooking — most famously *ayam pandan*, in which
marinated chicken is folded into pandan leaves and fried, so the parcel steams and
perfumes the meat from the outside in.

## The green of the kuih tray

Walk past a kuih stall and the row of green, layered sweets owes its colour and
scent to pandan juice. The leaf is central to the Malaysian repertoire of
bite-sized traditional cakes — see
[kuih-muih](/food-lifestyle/kuih-muih) — where it turns up again and again.
*Kuih lapis*, the soft steamed layer cake, is often tinted and flavoured in
pandan green; *kuih seri muka* pairs a base of glutinous rice with a pandan
custard top; and *kuih talam* sets a pale coconut layer over a green pandan one.
In these sweets pandan does double duty, supplying both the perfume and the
natural colour that bottled dyes only imitate.

Pandan also flavours **kaya**, the coconut-and-egg jam spread on toast and folded
into kuih. The green-hued version of the jam, as opposed to the browner
caramel-toned style, gets its colour and scent from pandan leaves; it is the
pandan note that defines the classic breakfast of
[kaya toast](/food-lifestyle/kaya-toast). Kaya and many kuih also lean on palm
sugar — [gula melaka](/food-lifestyle/gula-melaka) — whose smoky caramel pairs
naturally with pandan's grassy sweetness.

## Pandan cake and chiffon

Pandan's best-known modern form is the **pandan cake**, also called pandan
chiffon: a light, airy sponge flavoured and coloured with pandan, instantly
recognisable by its pale jade crumb. The leaf is its signature ingredient and the
source of its green, though some bakers deepen the colour with added food
colouring. The cake is popular across Malaysia, Singapore and Indonesia; in 2017
CNN described it as a national cake of Singapore and Malaysia.

Its history is contested and worth hedging. Accounts trace the pandan-and-sponge
idea to European baking techniques brought to the Dutch East Indies (modern
Indonesia), where local pandan supplied flavour and colour, while the specific
chiffon format is often linked to twentieth-century American baking — so the cake
on the shelf today is a layered inheritance rather than a single origin story.
Either way, pandan cake is now a fixture of Malaysian bakeries and home ovens.

## In drinks and desserts

Pandan runs through the cold side of the menu too. It is one of the defining
scents of [cendol](/food-lifestyle/cendol), the shaved-ice dessert whose
worm-like green jelly noodles are made with pandan, served over ice with coconut
milk and palm sugar syrup. More broadly, pandan juice is the base of cooling
drinks and sweet soups, and it threads through the wider world of
[Malaysian desserts](/food-lifestyle/malaysian-desserts) — in agar jellies,
coconut custards and steamed puddings — wherever a cook wants a gentle,
unmistakable green perfume.

## Fresh, paste, extract and essence

Pandan reaches the kitchen in several forms, and the choice shapes the result.
**Fresh** leaves — increasingly sold frozen outside the region — give the truest
and most delicate aroma, but a softer, more muted green. **Pandan paste** and
**extract** are concentrated and convenient, boosting both flavour and colour.
Bottled **pandan essence** or flavouring is the most intense and the most
consistent, and often carries added green colouring for a vivid, uniform hue —
which is why shop cakes can look far greener than one tinted with juice alone.
Cooks after the purest scent favour fresh leaves; those after punchy colour and
shelf stability reach for paste or essence. The same recipe can look and taste
noticeably different depending on which is used.

## A quiet thread through the kitchen

Pandan rarely takes the spotlight the way a chilli or a curry paste does. Its job
is quieter — to lend a sweet, green, faintly vanilla scent that signals "something
good is cooking" before the first taste. From a knotted leaf in the rice pot to
the jade crumb of a chiffon cake, it ties together a surprising span of the
Malaysian table, savoury and sweet alike.
