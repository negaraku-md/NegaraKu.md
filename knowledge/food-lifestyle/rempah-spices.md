Lift the lid on almost any pot of Malaysian curry, *gulai* or *sambal* and the
same thing is working underneath the surface: a dark, glossy, deeply fragrant
paste that was pounded and fried long before any meat or fish went in. That
paste is **rempah**, and learning to read it is the fastest way to understand how
Malaysian food is built.

## One paste, a hundred dishes

In Malay, *rempah* simply means "spice," but in the kitchen it has a narrower,
working sense: a wet paste of ground aromatics and spices that forms the flavour
base of a dish. It is used as the foundation for most Malaysian curries, sambal
and gulai, and cooks reach for the strongest possible language to describe how
central it is — SBS Food calls it "the backbone of Malaysian cuisine," and the
chef it profiled estimated that around 80 per cent of his menu was built on
rempah. The MICHELIN Guide describes it plainly as "a paste of spices" that
gives a dish its intense flavour and serves as the base of many Malay, Indonesian
and Peranakan dishes.

What makes rempah a *base* rather than a seasoning is that it goes in first and is
cooked on its own before anything else joins it. A cook who has a good rempah
has, in effect, already decided what the dish will taste like. The same technique
underpins much of [Malay cuisine](/food-lifestyle/malay-cuisine), sits beneath
the chilli pastes of [sambal](/food-lifestyle/sambal), and is close kin to the
wet masalas of [Malaysian curries](/food-lifestyle/malaysian-curries) — a
rempah is often compared to an Indian wet masala or a Thai curry paste in form
and function.

## The wet aromatics: the rempah basah

The living heart of a rempah is its fresh, moist aromatics — the *basah* ("wet")
ingredients. Sources differ on which are essential, but a broadly typical set
includes:

- **Shallot (*bawang merah*) and garlic (*bawang putih*)** — the sweet, savoury
  bulk of the paste.
- **Ginger (*halia*)** — warm and pungent; the MICHELIN Guide and SBS both name
  chilli, garlic and ginger among the most common rempah ingredients.
- **Galangal (*lengkuas*)** — a woody, piney rhizome, sharper and more medicinal
  than ginger, for which it is not a true substitute.
- **Turmeric (*kunyit*)** — earthy and bitter, and the source of the yellow in
  dishes built on *rempah kuning* ("yellow rempah"), often paired with fish.
- **Lemongrass (*serai*)** — citrusy and fibrous; it is pounded along with the
  other aromatics to lend brightness.
- **Candlenut (*buah keras*)** — an oily nut used to thicken and enrich a paste
  and give it body; it is characteristic of the Peranakan style of rempah.
- **Dried and fresh chilli** — dried chillies (often soaked) for deep colour and
  mellow heat, fresh ones for sharper fire; the balance is adjusted to taste.
- **Belacan** — fermented shrimp paste, crumbled in for a salty, umami depth.

That last ingredient deserves a note of its own. [Belacan](/food-lifestyle/belacan)
is pungent and divisive, but a small amount folded into a rempah is what gives
many Malaysian dishes their savoury undertone; Wikipedia's entry on Malaysian
cuisine describes belacan being crumbled into "a ground spice paste called
rempah." Which of these aromatics are truly indispensable and which are regional
or dish-specific additions is not fixed — treat the list above as a representative
pantry rather than a single recipe.

## The dry spices: rempah kering and the "four siblings"

Alongside the wet aromatics, richer dishes — rendang, many gulai and the heavier
curries — draw on a second group of ground **dry spices** (*rempah kering*). The
usual suspects are warm, toasty and sweet:

- **Coriander seed (*ketumbar*)** — the workhorse, lending a citrusy, nutty base.
- **Cumin (*jintan putih*)** — earthy and savoury.
- **Fennel (*jintan manis*)** — sweet and aniseed-like.
- **Star anise, cinnamon, cardamom and clove** — the fragrant "sweet" spices that
  perfume rendang and beef curries; the MICHELIN Guide notes cinnamon and star
  anise among rempah additions for specific dishes.

Malay cooks often speak of a core trio or quartet of ground spices that recur
across so many recipes they are treated as a set — a grouping sometimes called
*rempah empat beradik*, literally "the four sibling spices." Coriander, cumin and
fennel reliably appear in such lists; the identity of the fourth member varies
between sources and households, so the phrase is best treated as a culinary
convention rather than a precisely fixed formula (see the verification note
below). Dry spices are frequently dry-toasted and ground, then blended into the
wet paste so the finished rempah carries both the fresh pungency of the
aromatics and the deep warmth of the seeds.

## From stone to pan: batu giling, tumis and pecah minyak

A rempah is only half-made once its ingredients are gathered; technique does the
rest. Traditionally the aromatics and spices are pounded together on a flat
grinding stone — the **batu giling** — or crushed in a stone mortar (*batu
lesung*). Many cooks hold that this hand method beats a blender: the MICHELIN
Guide argues that pounding releases more of the ingredients' oils and gives a
silkier texture than a blade, which tends to chop and aerate rather than crush.
A blender or food processor is nonetheless a widely accepted modern shortcut, and
most home kitchens now use one.

The decisive step comes in the pan. The raw paste is fried gently in oil — the
process called **tumis** — and this is not optional. Frying "matures" the rempah:
it drives off the harsh, raw edge, blooms the spices and spreads their flavour
compounds through the oil. Cooks watch for a specific signal to know when the
paste is ready: **pecah minyak**, "the oil splits," the moment when oil separates
out and glistens at the edges of and on top of the paste. Reaching pecah minyak
can take many minutes of patient, frequent stirring over medium-low heat. Skip
or rush the tumis and, as the MICHELIN Guide warns, the dish is left tasting
"raw and unbalanced." Only once the rempah has split its oil and turned fragrant
do the meat, fish, vegetables and liquid go in.

## Why the Straits of Melaka became a spice crossroads

Malaysia's extraordinarily deep spice pantry is not an accident of the kitchen
alone; it is a legacy of geography and trade. The peninsula sits on the **Strait
of Melaka**, one of the world's great maritime corridors, and in the 15th century
the port of Melaka grew within about a century from a small settlement into a
cosmopolitan entrepot, its rise driven by that location and by demand from both
East and West.

Melaka produced few spices of its own; its power lay in being the place where
they changed hands. It served as a collection point for cloves, nutmeg and mace
carried from the Maluku ("Spice") Islands, and redistributed cotton textiles
from India, porcelain and silk from China, and regional goods such as camphor and
sandalwood — the monsoon winds structuring who arrived and left each season. By
around 1500 it had become a leading centre for Indian cloth, Chinese goods and
Malay spices, valuable enough that the Portuguese seized it in 1511 to break the
existing spice networks. This centuries-long traffic — Arab, Indian, Chinese,
Javanese and European by turns — helped stock the Malay kitchen with the dry
spices, and the habits of using them, that a modern rempah still draws on,
although the precise line from trade route to spice paste is an inference food
historians draw rather than a single recorded fact.

## Rempah across the Malaysian table

Because rempah is a method as much as a recipe, it adapts to whatever a region,
community or dish demands. A fish *asam pedas* leans on tamarind and turmeric; a
rendang loads in the sweet dry spices and *kerisik*; a Peranakan dish may thicken
its rempah with candlenut and finish it with belacan, in the style that defines
much of [Nyonya and Peranakan cuisine](/food-lifestyle/nyonya-peranakan-cuisine).
The common thread is constant: grind the aromatics, blend in the toasted spices,
fry to pecah minyak, and build the dish on top. Learn to taste that base and the
sprawling variety of Malaysian cooking starts to resolve into a single underlying
grammar — one paste, endlessly reinflected.
