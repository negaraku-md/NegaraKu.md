Watch an appam cook and you understand the dish before you taste it. A ladle of
pale, slightly sour batter goes into a small, deep pan; a quick swirl sends it
climbing the sides, where it thins to a crisp, golden lace, while the pool at the
bottom stays thick and soft. Lift it out and you have a shallow edible bowl —
brittle at the rim, spongy in the middle. Few pancakes manage two textures at once,
and that contrast is the whole appeal of appam, known across Malaysia as *apom*.

## What appam is

Appam is a South Indian pancake, a long-standing part of
[Indian-Malaysian cooking](/food-lifestyle/indian-malaysian-cuisine) and a close
cousin of the dishes called *hoppers* in Kerala and Sri Lanka. The name travels with
the dish: on a Malaysian signboard it is as likely to read *apom* as *appam*. What
sets it apart from a flat griddle pancake such as
[thosai](/food-lifestyle/thosai) is its shape. Appam is cooked in an *appachatti*,
a small, rounded, wok-like pan, so instead of lying flat it curves up into a bowl.

## How it is made

The foundation is a fermented batter. Rice is soaked and ground, blended with
coconut — often as coconut milk — and left to ferment for hours. That rest does two
things: it lends appam its faint, pleasant sourness, and it builds the tiny bubbles
that burst into a delicate lace as the thin edge meets the hot pan. A ladle of batter
is swirled so it coats the sides and settles in the base; a lid traps the steam that
puffs the centre. The result is the signature split personality — crisp and airy at
the edge, soft and almost custardy at the heart.

## Plain, egg and sweet

Within that one method, appam comes in a few familiar forms:

- **Plain appam** — the batter alone, letting the lacy edge and soft centre speak for
  themselves; eaten with a curry or chutney.
- **Egg appam (appam telur)** — an egg cracked into the middle as it cooks, so the
  centre sets rich and soft while the rim stays crisp.
- **Sweet appam** — made richer with extra coconut milk and sugar for a softer, almost
  fudgy middle, eaten on its own as a snack.

Stalls add their own touches, and a Malay Mail account of one Kuala Lumpur appam
seller describes savoury versions finished with coconut sambol or with egg and plenty
of black pepper, and peanuts scattered over a sweet one.

## Not to be confused with apom balik

Here the names cause honest confusion. The Indian appam above is thin, fermented and
lacy-edged. The Nyonya-Malay **apom balik** is a different dish that happens to share
the root word: a thick, buttery pancake made from a flour-and-egg batter, folded over
a filling of banana, sweet corn and sugar — part of the sweet repertoire of
[Nyonya-Peranakan cooking](/food-lifestyle/nyonya-peranakan-cuisine). Penang food
writers are explicit that the local Chinese *apong* is very different from the much
thinner Indian apom. A further relative, **apom berkuah**, is a Baba-Nyonya pancake,
sometimes tinted with blue pea flower, served with a sweet banana-and-palm-sugar
coconut sauce called *pengat*. Related names, genuinely different plates.

## Where to find it

In Malaysia, appam belongs to the Indian stall and the market. It turns up at
Indian eateries, at wet markets in the morning, and at night markets (*pasar malam*),
where a vendor will often keep a whole row of little pans going at once, pouring and
lidding them in sequence so a queue never waits long. Penang, with its deep street-food
culture, is one of the places you will find the Indian apom and the Nyonya apong sold
within a few streets of each other — the clearest possible lesson in how one word can
mean two quite different, equally good things.
