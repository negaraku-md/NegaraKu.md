Ask ten Malaysians what "Malaysian food" is, and you may get ten different maps. Someone from Kota Bharu will start with the blue nasi kerabu and budu; someone from Kuching will mention Sarawak laksa and kolo mee; someone from Melaka might talk about Nyonya ayam pongteh or Kristang kari debal; and yet another will point straight to the mamak stall at the end of the street. None of them is wrong. Malaysia has no single national cuisine — it has a family of kitchens that grew up in different regions and communities, then spent centuries borrowing from one another.

The most honest way to read this cuisine is not to rank it, but to read it as history. Each tradition is a record of who came, who married whom, what grew in the soil, and what was brought in through the ports.

## Why can one country have so many kitchens?

The answer is geography and migration. The peninsula faces the Strait of Malacca, one of the busiest trade routes in history, so ports such as Melaka and Penang received Chinese, Indian, Arab and European traders for hundreds of years. Some of them settled and married locals, and the kitchen was the first place that mixing became visible.

Across the South China Sea, Sarawak and Sabah in Borneo have an entirely different story — indigenous communities such as the Iban, Bidayuh, Melanau, Kadazan-Dusun and Bajau built their cooking on what could be harvested from the rainforest and rivers. The result is not one cuisine, but an archipelago of flavour.

## What makes Nyonya cuisine so special?

Peranakan, or Nyonya, cuisine is often called one of the world's earliest fusion cuisines. It was born from intermarriage between Chinese traders who settled in the Straits Settlements and local women, with its main centres in Melaka and Penang. The term "Baba" refers to the men and "Nyonya" to the women — and because it was the women who ran the kitchen and set the standard, the cuisine took their name.

What makes it distinct is the way it combines two worlds: the technique and some symbolism of Chinese cooking, united with Malay ingredients such as coconut milk, lemongrass, tamarind, candlenut and daun kesum. Dishes such as Nyonya laksa, ayam pongteh, itik tim and colourful kuih display this blend. Because Peranakan culture is open, its cuisine also keeps traces of Portuguese, Dutch and English touches. George Town in Penang, now a UNESCO World Heritage Site, remains one of the best places to see this multicultural heritage still alive.

## Why does east-coast food taste sweeter?

Moving to the east coast of the peninsula — Kelantan and Terengganu — the flavour palette changes noticeably. The cuisine here is famous for leaning sweet and rich, relying heavily on coconut milk and sugar, including gula kabung and gula melaka. Sambal and gulai here are often sweeter than versions in other regions.

Its signature dishes read like a regional phrasebook. Nasi kerabu is famous for its rice tinted blue by butterfly-pea flowers, served with ulam, sambal, fish and budu — a fermented anchovy sauce that forms the flavour base of the east-coast kitchen. Nasi dagang, a classic breakfast, is rice steamed with coconut milk and served with a tuna-fish gulai. Ayam percik is grilled chicken coated in a sweet-spicy coconut sauce. The region's closeness to the sea shows in the use of budu and keropok, while its fresh ulam reflects a close bond with the land.

## What grows in the Borneo kitchen?

Sarawak and Sabah play by their own rules. Here, cooking is built on wild forest ingredients, fermentation, and cooking methods that use bamboo and embers.

In Sarawak, Sarawak laksa has a rich, fragrant broth from sambal belacan, coconut milk and stock, entirely different from other laksa in Malaysia. Kolo mee is dry, springy egg noodles, a popular breakfast dish. Manok pansoh — chicken cooked inside bamboo with lemongrass, ginger and local leaves over a fire — is a classic Iban cooking method that gives a smoky, earthy flavour. Umai, a raw-fish dish marinated with lime juice, chilli and onion, is popular among the Melanau community and was originally enjoyed by fishermen at sea. Midin, a wild jungle fern, is stir-fried with garlic or belacan.

In Sabah, hinava is the Kadazan-Dusun counterpart — raw fish marinated with lime, bird's-eye chilli, onion and ginger, sometimes with bambangan seeds (Borneo wild mango). Tuhau (wild ginger) and bosou (fermented fish) show how important forest ingredients and fermentation are in this indigenous kitchen. Among Borneo dishes, umai from Sarawak is also recognised in the national heritage-food programme.

## Where do nasi kandar and kari debal come from?

The last two traditions show how deeply migration shaped Malaysia's kitchens.

Mamak cuisine — a term referring to the Muslim-Indian community — originates from Muslim-Indian immigrants of the British colonial era, many from Tamil Nadu, who first hawked tea and snacks before building shops. Nasi kandar comes from Penang, its name coming from the kandar (carrying pole) that hawkers used to carry containers of rice and dishes. Roti canai, a layered and crisp flatbread, was introduced by South Indian immigrants and is now one of the country's most iconic dishes. Late-night mamak shops have also become a social space across communities.

Kristang cuisine is the heritage of the Eurasian community in Melaka, of mixed Portuguese and Asian descent, formed after the Portuguese presence in Melaka. Its most famous dish, kari debal (devil's curry), is a spicy curry flavoured with candlenut, galangal, mustard seed and vinegar — with that vinegar linking it back to Portuguese tradition and the vindaloo of Goa. The name "debal" comes from the word "devil" — that is, "devil's curry" — referring to its very spicy taste. According to popular tradition, this dish was once cooked using leftover meat after Christmas; however, that link is to a cooking practice, not to the meaning of the word "debal" itself. Today, this cuisine is considered endangered, and only a few families and restaurants continue to inherit it.

## How do these traditions compare?

The table below summarises each tradition fairly — not to rank them, but to show origins, flavour characteristics and a few dishes associated with each.

| East coast | Kelantan, Terengganu | Tends sweet and rich; lots of coconut milk and sugar; budu | Nasi kerabu, nasi dagang, ayam percik |

## Is all of this officially recognised?

Yes, and the recognition keeps growing. In December 2024, UNESCO inscribed "Malaysian Breakfast Culture: A Dining Experience in a Multi-Ethnic Society" on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, at the committee's 19th session held in Paraguay. This was Malaysia's first food-related nomination, submitted in March 2023, and it is now one of nine Malaysian intangible cultural heritage elements listed by UNESCO, alongside songket, silat, mak yong and dondang sayang. Importantly, the recognition is not about a single dish, but about the way a multi-ethnic society gathers at the morning table — a celebration of unity in diversity.

At the national level, the Department of National Heritage runs a heritage-food programme (Makanan Warisan) that celebrates dishes across regions, from Melaka's heritage cuisine to Sarawak's umai, tebaloi, kek lapis and bubur pedas, as well as roti canai and teh tarik. This framework treats every kitchen tradition as part of the nation's shared heritage, and not the exclusive property of any one group.

## What's next

Seeing Malaysian cuisine as one family of kitchens, not a scoreboard, changes the way you eat. The blue nasi kerabu, the smoky bamboo of manok pansoh, the sharp vinegar of kari debal, and the glistening oil on roti canai are not racing against one another — all of them are edible records of history about who lived here and how they met.

For readers who want to explore more deeply, the most meaningful step is to go to the source: eat nasi dagang in Kuala Terengganu, umai in Kuching, hinava in Kota Kinabalu, and kari debal in the Portuguese Settlement of Melaka. Each is best understood not through comparison, but through context — the state, community and history that brought it into being.
