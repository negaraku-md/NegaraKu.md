In many countries a festival belongs to the people who celebrate it. In Malaysia
it tends to belong to everyone in the street. During each major festival,
households throw open their doors in what is called a *rumah terbuka* — an open
house — and the food laid out is meant for whoever walks in, regardless of
religion or race. To follow Malaysia's festivals, then, is to follow a shared
food calendar that turns over through the year: fasting and feasting, reunion
dinners and harvest toasts, sweets and vegetarian devotion.

## The open house: Malaysia's shared table

The open house is the thread that ties the whole calendar together. A family
celebrating its own festival prepares a spread and welcomes friends, neighbours,
colleagues and often complete strangers to eat, so that over the course of a year
most Malaysians end up as guests at festivals that are not their own. The custom
runs across communities: it is practised at Hari Raya, Chinese New Year, Deepavali
and other festivals alike. Politicians, companies and government bodies stage
large public open houses, and the tradition is widely read as shorthand for the
country's multicultural coexistence. Its standing is such that, in 2025, *The
Star* reported a proposal to seek UNESCO recognition for the open house concept,
spanning Hari Raya, Deepavali, Chinese New Year and beyond — though at the time of
writing the outcome of any such nomination is unconfirmed.

The ethos matters as much as the menu. What a guest eats changes from festival to
festival, but the act — being fed at someone else's celebration — stays the same.
That shared habit is the reason Malaysia's festival food is best understood as one
calendar rather than a set of separate community cuisines.

## Ramadan bazaars and Hari Raya Aidilfitri

For Muslim Malaysians the food year's most intense stretch begins in the fasting
month of Ramadan, when, from late afternoon, *bazaar Ramadan* spring up in car
parks and along roadsides selling food to break the fast at sunset. These
bazaars, covered in their own right under
[Ramadan bazaars and the fasting month](/food-lifestyle/ramadan-bazaars-and-fasting-month-food),
are a seasonal institution that draws buyers of every background.

The fast culminates in **Hari Raya Aidilfitri**, the festival that opens the
country's best-known season of open houses. The table is built around a handful
of staples: *ketupat* (rice dumplings compressed and boiled in woven palm-leaf
pouches), *lemang* (glutinous rice cooked with coconut milk in bamboo over an open
fire), and *rendang* (meat slow-simmered in spiced coconut gravy until dry),
usually alongside satay and an array of *kuih*. Ketupat is bound tightly to Eid
across the Malay world — ketupat *palas* is, as the English Wikipedia article on
ketupat notes, "often served during festive occasions such as Hari Raya," and the
woven dumpling's diamond shape is used as a Raya decorative motif on homes and in
public spaces. Reporting around 2026 suggested the federal government would forgo
official ministry and agency open houses that year as a cost-saving step; this was
a single-source account and is best confirmed against an official statement.

## Chinese New Year: the reunion dinner and yee sang

For Chinese Malaysians the year turns on **Chinese New Year** and, above all, the
*reunion dinner* on the eve, when extended families gather for a multi-course
meal. The most distinctive Malaysian contribution to the table is **yee sang**
(also *lou sang* or *yusheng*) — a salad of raw fish, shredded vegetables and
sauces that is not so much eaten as performed. Diners stand, add the ingredients
in turn, then toss the pile high into the air with their chopsticks while calling
out auspicious wishes; the higher the toss, the better the luck is said to be, and
the ingredients are taken to symbolise prosperity and growth. It is most popular
in Malaysia and Singapore and is often eaten around *Renri*, the seventh day of
the new year.

Where the modern dish came from is genuinely disputed. One well-repeated Malaysian
account, as summarised by the English Wikipedia entry on yusheng, traces it to a
fish dish served by Loke Ching Fatt in Seremban, while a Singaporean account
credits a group of chefs there; the Singaporean claim carries a "failed
verification" flag on Wikipedia, and a descendant of Loke is quoted as concluding
in 2020 that the origin is hard to prove and not worth insisting on. Readers
should treat precise dates and inventors with caution.

## Deepavali: sweets and the festival of lights

For Hindu Malaysians, **Deepavali** (Diwali), the festival of lights, brings its
own open houses and its own repertoire, weighted towards sweets and savoury
snacks: *murukku* (a crunchy, coiled savoury), *laddu* and other confections,
served alongside curries. As with Hari Raya and Chinese New Year, the Deepavali
open house is a cross-community affair, and large public Deepavali open houses are
a fixture of the national calendar. The broader spread of festival dishes shared
across communities is mapped in
[festive foods across Malaysian festivals](/food-lifestyle/festive-foods-across-malaysian-festivals).

## Gawai and Kaamatan: Borneo's harvest festivals

East Malaysia keeps a different pair of festivals rooted in the rice harvest.
**Gawai Dayak**, celebrated by the Iban and other Dayak groups in Sarawak around
the start of June, and **Kaamatan**, the Kadazan-Dusun thanksgiving in Sabah in
late May, both give thanks for the harvest and both revolve around rice and rice
wine. *Tuak* — a wine brewed from glutinous rice and homemade yeast — is central to
toasts and ritual; as *The Star* reported in 2026, villagers offer *tuak pengayu*
(longevity rice wine) around midnight on Gawai eve, and the rice spirit runs
through the festivities.

The signature dishes differ by region. Sarawak's best-known festive dish is
**manok pansuh**, chicken cooked inside a bamboo tube — part of the wider
[Iban food](/food-lifestyle/iban-food) tradition — while Sabah's Kaamatan table
features **hinava**, described by *The Star* as a Sabahan-style fish "ceviche" of
fresh mackerel, lime, ginger and sliced bitter gourd, among the
[Kadazan-Dusun foods](/food-lifestyle/kadazan-dusun-food) prepared for the season.
Food wrapped and cooked in leaves — such as *linopot* and *nuba laya* — rounds out
the longhouse spread. Exact dish attributions vary across the many Dayak and
Kadazan-Dusun sub-groups, so regional accounts should be read as representative
rather than definitive.

## Thaipusam: vegetarian devotion

Not every festival is a feast. **Thaipusam**, the Hindu festival most visibly
associated with Batu Caves near Kuala Lumpur, is marked by fasting and strict
vegetarianism. Devotees — especially those who will carry a *kavadi* — prepare
with a period of abstinence, eating a single vegetarian meal a day; sources
variously give this as roughly 30 or 48 days, and some describe a stricter full
fast in the final day or two, so the exact regimen is best confirmed with a
devotional authority. On the festival itself, vegetarian food is shared freely
through *annadhanam*. *The Star* reported that this charitable feeding at Batu
Caves dates back to 1966, when what is now the Divine Life Society began serving
devotees on the eve and day of Thaipusam. Here the food is an act of devotion and
giving rather than celebration.

## Organised food festivals and pasar malam culture

Alongside the religious and harvest calendar sits a growing calendar of *organised*
food events. The best established is the **Penang International Food Festival**
(PIFF), which began in 2017 and has grown into the state's flagship food event; by
2023, as *The Star* reported, its main events and mini-festivals were held across
both the island and the mainland for the first time, trading on Penang's reputation
as the country's hawker capital.

Underneath the branded festivals runs the everyday infrastructure of Malaysian
eating in public: the weekly **pasar malam** (night market) and the seasonal
Ramadan bazaar, where much of the country actually encounters festival and street
food. These rhythms are explored in
[pasar malam and street food](/food-lifestyle/pasar-malam-and-street-food), and
the regional variation beneath them — how each state and community cooks — in
[regional and ethnic cuisines](/food-lifestyle/regional-and-ethnic-cuisines).

## The common thread

Lay the calendar out end to end and a pattern emerges that has little to do with
any one recipe. Ketupat and rendang, yee sang, murukku, tuak and manok pansuh,
and a plate of free vegetarian rice at Batu Caves belong to different communities
and different faiths. What links them is the open door: the expectation that a
festival is something you share, and that the surest way to mark your own
celebration is to feed someone else's. In Malaysia, the food tells you what is
being celebrated — but the open house tells you how.
