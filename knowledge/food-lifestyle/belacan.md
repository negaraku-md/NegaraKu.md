Few ingredients announce themselves as loudly as belacan. Unwrap a block and the
smell fills the kitchen; drop a knob into a hot pan and the whole street seems to
know. Yet this small, dark brick of fermented shrimp is one of the quiet
foundations of Malaysian cooking — the thing that gives a sambal its savoury
depth, a rojak dressing its funk, and a bowl of asam laksa its backbone.

## What belacan is

Belacan is a paste or firm block made from very small shrimp and salt, fermented
and dried. The shrimp in question are tiny — in Malaysia they are called
*geragau* or *bubuk*, members of the genus *Acetes*, and they are often loosely
described as "krill," though that label is not strictly accurate. Mixed with
salt and left to ferment, they break down into a dense, intensely savoury paste
that is then dried and pressed into shape.

A Malaysian government arts-and-culture heritage listing calls belacan "an
essential ingredient in traditional Malay cuisine," and that is no exaggeration:
it supplies the deep umami note — the fermented, oceanic savour — that sits
underneath a very large share of the Malay and
[Nyonya](/food-lifestyle/nyonya-peranakan-cuisine) repertoire. A little goes a
long way, which is why a single block can last a home cook for months.

## How belacan is made

The method is simple in outline but slow in practice. The freshly caught geragau
are first washed to remove sand, mud and other impurities, then mixed with salt —
by one heritage account in roughly a ten-to-one ratio of shrimp to salt, though
proportions vary by maker. The salted shrimp are sun-dried, pounded or minced
into a paste, and then fermented; the paste is dried again under the sun, pounded
once more, and the cycle is repeated until it matures into a firm, dark
reddish-brown mass. Finally it is pressed into blocks, slices or cylinders and
packed.

How long this takes depends on the producer and the weather. One long-running
Penang family business, SSL Belacan, described wrapping the pounded paste tightly
in banana leaves for an initial two to three weeks, drying it, then fermenting it
for a further ten to thirteen weeks — a total of three to four months per batch,
with the firm's younger generation noting that "the longer it is left to ferment,
the more pungent it will be." Other makers work to shorter cycles. Because the
drying still relies on the sun, modern producers increasingly carry it out under
clear-roofed shelters that keep off dust and rain while letting the light
through.

## The smell — and why cooks toast it first

There is no getting around the aroma. Raw belacan is powerfully pungent, and the
smell is strong enough that producers have historically sited their workshops
away from busy or residential areas. That pungency is a feature, not a fault: it
is the concentrated signature of the fermentation, and it transforms once heat is
applied.

For that reason belacan is almost always *toasted* before use. Cooks dry-fry a
small piece in a pan, or wrap it in foil or a banana leaf and heat it, until it
turns crumbly and its smell shifts from sharp and raw to deep, nutty and rounded.
The toasted paste is then pounded or crumbled into whatever dish needs it. Skip
this step and the raw note can dominate; toast it well and belacan melts into the
background as pure savour.

## Belacan in the kitchen

Belacan's most direct expression is [sambal](/food-lifestyle/sambal) belacan — toasted
belacan pounded with fresh chillies, shallots, a little sugar and a squeeze of
lime, served as a fiery relish alongside rice, raw vegetables (*ulam*) and fried
fish. It is one of the simplest and most beloved things on the Malay table.

From there its reach is enormous. It is the hidden engine of many
[rojak](/food-lifestyle/rojak) dressings, where its funk balances sugar, tamarind
and chilli over fruit and vegetables. It is pounded into the spice paste of
Penang's sour [asam laksa](/food-lifestyle/laksa) and is the defining base of
Sarawak laksa. It seasons *nasi goreng* (fried rice), *kangkung belacan*
(water spinach stir-fried with chilli and shrimp paste), *asam pedas* and a long
list of sambals and gravies. Across the Malay, Nyonya and wider Malaysian kitchen
it functions less like a single ingredient than like a seasoning of last resort —
the thing stirred in when a dish tastes good but not yet *deep*.

## Grades, quality and buying belacan

Not all belacan is equal. The most prized is made from nothing but shrimp and
salt; cheaper grades may be bulked out with filler or extra salt, which dilutes
the flavour. Colour, firmness and smell all vary with the shrimp used, the
salt-to-shrimp ratio and the length of fermentation — longer-fermented paste is
generally darker and more pungent. Blocks are commonly sold wrapped in paper or
plastic and, because they are dried and salted, they keep well without
refrigeration. Shoppers often judge quality by a clean, strong shrimp aroma and a
dense, even texture rather than by brand alone.

## Where belacan comes from

Belacan is made in coastal fishing communities where the geragau run, chiefly
along the Straits of Malacca and in the Borneo states. On the peninsula, Penang —
especially the Balik Pulau area — is a well-known centre with multi-generation
family producers, and the states of Perlis and Melaka and the island of Pangkor
are also associated with the trade; the precise ranking of these centres is
reported inconsistently and is worth confirming locally.

The craft is under real pressure. Because the drying depends on sunshine, the
northeast monsoon and increasingly erratic weather can badly disrupt production:
one Perlis producer told BenarNews that prolonged rain had cut "production and
income ... by nearly 20%." Supply of the shrimp itself has also thinned in some
areas — SSL Belacan, for instance, now sources its geragau from Perak because the
shrimp are no longer plentiful off Penang. Despite this, Malaysian belacan is
exported to markets across Asia, Australia and beyond.

## Belacan's regional cousins

Belacan is the Malaysian member of a shrimp-paste family found right across
maritime Southeast Asia. In Indonesia the equivalent is *terasi* (Javanese
*trasi*); in Thailand and Laos it is *kapi*; in Cambodia *kapi* (*kābi*); in the
Philippines *bagoong alamang*; in Vietnam the related *mắm tôm*; and in Myanmar
*ngapi*, which is often made from fish rather than shrimp. All are built on the
same idea of salting and fermenting small shrimp or krill, and most are
interchangeable in a pinch, but they differ in texture, saltiness, colour and
strength — which is why a recipe will usually name the one it expects. Belacan's
place in this map is part of what ties Malaysian food to its
[regional and neighbouring cuisines](/food-lifestyle/regional-and-ethnic-cuisines).
