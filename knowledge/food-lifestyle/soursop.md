Spiky, green and heavy, the **soursop** looks at first glance like a small durian —
which is exactly how it got its Malay name, **durian belanda**. But cut it open and the
likeness ends: inside is soft, white, fragrant pulp, and blended into a glass it makes
one of Malaysia's most refreshing tropical juices, sweet and tart and faintly creamy.

## What soursop is — and isn't

The soursop (*Annona muricata*) is **not a durian** at all. The two share only a
spiky skin; the name *durian belanda* — literally "**Dutch durian**" — records the
fruit's arrival from the **tropical Americas** through European contact, not any
botanical kinship. Inside, the white, fibrous flesh tastes nothing like durian:
sweet-sour and custardy, often compared to **pineapple, banana and citrus**. It is
this bright, perfumed flavour that makes the juice so popular.

For where soursop sits among the country's wider drinks board, see the
[Malaysian drinks](/food-lifestyle/malaysian-drinks) hub. This article is the deep
dive into the durian belanda drink itself.

## How the juice is made

Making soursop juice is straightforward: the ripe pulp is **scooped out**, the hard
**black seeds removed and discarded** (they are not eaten), and the flesh blended with
**water or milk, ice and a little sugar** until smooth and frothy. Water-based, it is a
light, tangy juice; with milk, it becomes a richer **smoothie**. Either way it is
served cold — a fragrant, pale, creamy glass that is a natural fit for the heat.

## An honest word on health claims

Soursop comes wrapped in a lot of **online health folklore** — above all the claim
that it "cures cancer." It is worth being clear and careful here: **there is no
reliable evidence that soursop treats or cures cancer in people.** The findings behind
those claims come from **laboratory and animal studies**, not proven results in
humans; reviews of the actual human evidence describe it as limited and inconclusive.
The plant also contains compounds with **recognised toxicity concerns**. None of this
means you cannot enjoy a glass of soursop juice — it is a perfectly good fruit drink —
but it should **never be used as a substitute for medical care**, and anyone with a
health condition should talk to a doctor rather than rely on folklore.

## Where to find it

Soursop juice is a **juice-bar and hawker** favourite, blended fresh wherever tropical
fruits are turned into drinks — fruit stalls, food courts and dedicated juice counters
across the country. Alongside [coconut water](/food-lifestyle/air-kelapa) and the
yogurt-based [lassi](/food-lifestyle/lassi), it is one of the cooling, fruit-forward
options on the Malaysian drinks board.
