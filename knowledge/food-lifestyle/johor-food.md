Cross the causeway from Singapore and the first Malaysian state you reach is
Johor — and its table tells you immediately that you have arrived somewhere with
its own history. Johor's food was shaped by three forces at once: the kitchens
of a long-established sultanate, waves of Javanese and Bugis settlers who brought
their own dishes, and a border so busy that Johor and Singapore argue happily
over who owns what. The result is a southern cuisine of thick coconut gravies,
slow-cooked rice and a few genuine one-of-a-kind dishes.

## A southern table: palace, migration and the strait

Three influences run through almost everything on a Johor plate. The first is
**royal cuisine** — the food of the Johor sultanate's court, which, as elsewhere
in the Malay world, prized rich, labour-intensive gravies and refined
presentation. The most famous example is laksa Johor itself, whose signature
quirk is popularly traced to the palace of Sultan Abu Bakar.

The second is **migration**. Johor received substantial Javanese and Bugis
settlement over the 19th and 20th centuries, and dishes such as *nasi ambeng*
(a shared platter associated with the Javanese community), *lontong* and *lodeh*
are commonly described as part of that inheritance. These attributions come
largely from food and travel media rather than documented culinary history, so
they are best read as widely held tradition rather than settled fact.

The third is **proximity to Singapore**. Many Johor dishes — mee bandung,
otak-otak, soto, laksa — appear on both sides of the strait, and the two
food cultures have grown up in constant exchange. Johor's cooking is, in short,
a particularly layered corner of Malaysia's broader map of
[regional and ethnic cuisines](/food-lifestyle/regional-and-ethnic-cuisines)
and of [Malay cuisine](/food-lifestyle/malay-cuisine) specifically.

## Laksa Johor: fish gravy over spaghetti

Johor's best-known contribution to the national table is also its strangest.
**Laksa Johor** belongs to the wider family of Malaysian
[laksa](/food-lifestyle/laksa) noodle soups, but breaks the mould in one
striking way: it is served not over rice noodles but over cooked **spaghetti**.
The gravy is thick and concentrated — grilled fish such as wolf herring
(*ikan parang*) or mackerel pounded into a spiced coconut base with *kerisik*
(toasted coconut paste), fish curry powder, *asam keping* (dried tamarind slices),
lemongrass and herbs — and the bowl is dressed with cucumber, bean sprouts,
shredded herbs, torch ginger flower (*bunga kantan*) and a squeeze of calamansi,
often eaten by hand.

The spaghetti is usually explained by a royal legend. According to the National
Department for Culture and Arts' cultural-mapping entry for the dish, Sultan Abu
Bakar developed a taste for pasta during a tour of Europe — particularly Italy —
around 1885 and instructed the royal cooks to work spaghetti into a local dish,
producing "a fusion of European influences and Malay tradition." The story is
repeated almost everywhere laksa Johor is described, but the precise origin is not
firmly documented and the sultan's reign dates and any heritage listing vary
between sources, so the legend should be treated as tradition rather than
established history.

## Muar: mee bandung and otak-otak

If Johor has a food capital, it is **Muar**, the royal town on the state's west
coast whose dishes travel far beyond it. Its signature is **mee bandung Muar**,
a noodle dish in a thick, savoury gravy built from dried shrimp, onion, spices,
shrimp paste and chilli, typically served with prawn, egg, meat, fish cake and
vegetables. The name misleads many visitors: it is generally said *not* to come
from Bandung in Indonesia, with "bandung" instead a Malay term for something
mixed or paired — the same sense as in the rose-and-milk drink *sirap bandung* —
though this etymology, while widely cited, is worth confirming against a
linguistic source.

Muar is equally associated with **otak-otak**, the leaf-wrapped grilled fish
paste that is one of southern Malaysia's great snacks. Muar's version — fish
blended with spice paste and coconut, wrapped in leaves and grilled over charcoal
— is widely held up as among the country's best, and the dish has a dedicated
guide of its own here: [otak-otak](/food-lifestyle/otak-otak). Both dishes are
central to the small-town breakfast-and-supper culture for which Muar is loved.

## Rice dishes: nasi beriani gam and soto

Johor's rice cooking runs from slow, aromatic feast dishes to everyday comfort
bowls. **Nasi beriani gam** is associated above all with **Batu Pahat**: a
biryani-style dish of Indian lineage adapted to the Malay palate, in which the
rice, meat and spices are cooked together — the method the name *gam* refers to —
so the grains absorb the meat's juices. It is a dish for weddings and feasts as
much as for restaurants.

More everyday is **soto**, a turmeric-tinged chicken broth of Indonesian origin
that is especially popular in Johor, usually served with compressed rice cakes
(*nasi impit*), shredded chicken, bean sprouts and fried shallots, and sometimes
with *begedil* (potato patties). Soto's prominence on Johor breakfast menus is
part of the state's strong Javanese-influenced streak, though, as with other
migrant-rooted dishes, the attribution rests on culinary tradition more than
documented record.

## Coconut comfort: lontong and lodeh

Coconut milk is the quiet workhorse of the Johor kitchen, and nowhere more than
in its vegetable gravies. **Lontong** pairs compressed rice cakes with *sayur
lodeh* — a mild coconut-milk stew of vegetables such as long beans, cabbage,
turnip, tempeh and tofu — often finished with *sambal*, hard-boiled egg,
*serunding* (meat floss) or *kerisik*. **Lodeh** itself, the coconut-vegetable
stew at the heart of the dish, is eaten across the Malay-Indonesian world and is
commonly traced to Javanese cooking.

These dishes, together with *nasi ambeng* — a communal platter of rice with an
array of side dishes shared from one tray — are the clearest everyday markers of
Johor's Javanese heritage. They anchor morning markets and *kenduri* (communal
feasts) across the state. The Indonesian and Javanese roots usually cited for
them are plausible and widely repeated, but drawn from food media rather than
rigorous history.

## Breakfast borrowings: kacang pool and roti kahwin

Two items define the Johor morning. **Kacang pool** is a stew of mashed
broad (fava) beans spiced and served with minced beef, a sunny-side-up egg,
chopped onion, chilli and lime, scooped up with toasted bread — a dish usually
described as Middle Eastern, often specifically Egyptian (akin to *ful medames*),
in origin before it became a Johor Bahru staple. That lineage is widely stated in
food media but not rigorously documented, so it is best hedged.

Alongside it sits **roti kahwin**, the classic *kopitiam* breakfast of toast
spread with *kaya* (coconut-egg jam) and a slab of butter; *kahwin*, meaning
"married," refers to the pairing of the two spreads. Taken with soft-boiled eggs
and *kopi*, it is the shared breakfast grammar of Johor and Singapore alike — a
small, everyday reminder of how closely the two sides of the strait eat.

## Durian country: Muar and Segamat

Johor is one of Malaysia's major durian regions, and the fruit is woven into its
food calendar. **Muar** and **Segamat** are both associated with durian
cultivation and seasonal durian tourism, and Johor is the home of well-known
cultivars — the state is often linked with the *Sultan* durian, popularly known
as **D24**, among others. For the fruit's varieties, grading and culture in full,
see [durian culture and varieties](/food-lifestyle/durian-culture-and-varieties).
As with the dish-and-town pairings above, specific "origin" claims for particular
orchards or cultivars reflect popular reputation and should be checked against a
local or agricultural source before being stated as fact.
