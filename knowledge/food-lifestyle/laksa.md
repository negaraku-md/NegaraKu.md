Order "laksa" in Penang and you get a sharp, sour fish broth that makes you
wince and reach for another spoonful. Order it in Kuching and a creamy,
spice-perfumed bowl arrives with shredded chicken and prawns. Order it in Johor
and the noodles turn out to be *spaghetti*. Same word, utterly different bowls —
and that is the first thing to understand about laksa.

## One name, many bowls

Laksa is best thought of as a surname shared by a sprawling family rather than a
single recipe. What the bowls have in common is a format — noodles in a strongly
flavoured, usually fish-forward broth, finished with fresh herbs and a hit of
chilli or shrimp paste — not a fixed set of ingredients. Beyond that, the
regional versions diverge so far that a lover of one can actively dislike
another.

Even the name's roots are contested. The word is widely traced to borrowings
that carried the sense of "noodles" or something slippery, though Persian,
Sanskrit and Hokkien origins have all been proposed, and no single account is
settled. What is clearer is the cultural lineage: the earliest Malaysian laksa is
generally credited to the [Peranakan](/arts-culture/peranakan) (Straits-born
Chinese) communities of the old trading ports, especially Melaka and Penang,
whose kitchens married Chinese noodles with Malay spice pastes and local souring
agents. That fusion is why "Nyonya laksa" — *nyonya* being the term for Peranakan
women — is used almost interchangeably with the richer coconut styles.

Laksa also sits near the heart of the national table. It appears on most lists of
the country's [signature dishes](/food-lifestyle/national-dishes-of-malaysia),
and Penang's version has carried the dish well beyond Malaysia's shores.

## Asam laksa: the sour northern bowl

The most internationally famous laksa is the one with no coconut at all. Penang
**asam laksa** (*asam* means sour) is built on a tamarind-soured fish broth,
traditionally made by poaching and flaking oily fish — mackerel is typical, often
alongside sardine and wolf herring — then simmering the stock with a spice paste
of chillies, shallots, lemongrass, galangal, *belacan* (fermented shrimp paste)
and the fragrant *daun kesum* (laksa leaf). The sourness comes from tamarind in
its dried-slice and *asam gelugor* forms, balanced with a little sugar.

It is served over thick rice noodles and crowned with a cool, crunchy tangle of
shredded cucumber, raw onion, pineapple, mint and finely sliced pink torch ginger
(*bunga kantan*), with a dark spoonful of sweet prawn paste (*hae ko*) drizzled
on top. Stall owners describe the whole art as getting "the correct balance of
the sour, sweet, and spicy flavours," as the family behind Penang Road Famous
Laksa told the MICHELIN Guide. The result convinced CNN, which ranked Penang
asam laksa seventh on its 2020 "World's 50 Best Foods" list.

## Curry laksa, laksa lemak and curry mee: the coconut camp

At the opposite pole sits the rich, coconut-based family — the bowl many people
outside Malaysia simply picture as "laksa." **Curry laksa**, also called **laksa
lemak** (*lemak* meaning rich or creamy, from coconut), floods noodles with a
curry-spiced coconut-milk gravy. The standard garnish is a comforting jumble of
puffed tofu (*tau pok*), prawns, fish cake, cockles, bean sprouts and mint, with
a dollop of chilli paste stirred in to taste. It is commonly served with yellow
alkaline egg noodles, rice vermicelli, or a mix of both.

This is the Klang Valley and Peranakan style, and it shades into Melaka's Nyonya
laksa. Confusingly, Penang has a near-identical dish it calls **curry mee** —
same coconut-curry idea, different name and local trimmings. The naming trips up
even locals: "curry laksa" and "curry mee" often point to the same bowl described
from two sides of the peninsula.

## Sarawak laksa: a bowl all its own

Across the South China Sea, Sarawak serves a laksa that belongs to neither camp.
**Sarawak laksa** is built on a complex paste — more than twenty ingredients by
some accounts — centred on *sambal belacan*, tamarind, lemongrass, galangal and
garlic, rather than on curry powder. The broth is chicken- and prawn-based and
fragrant rather than fiery; some cooks lightly enrich it with coconut milk, though
sources disagree on whether it belongs at all.

It is served over fine rice vermicelli (*bihun*) and topped with shredded
omelette, poached shredded chicken, blanched prawns, bean sprouts and coriander,
with a squeeze of lime and a side of extra sambal belacan to adjust the heat.
Because it leans on neither sourness nor coconut as its defining note, Sarawak
laksa is the clearest proof that "laksa" is a category, not a flavour.

## Johor laksa and other regional bowls

The peninsula's south offers the family's biggest surprise. **Johor laksa** pours
a thick, concentrated fish gravy — grilled wolf herring (*ikan parang*) and
prawns ground into a spiced coconut base, with *kerisik* (toasted coconut paste),
lemongrass and herbs — not over rice noodles but over cooked **spaghetti**. By the
most-repeated account, the swap dates to Sultan Abu Bakar of Johor, who is said to
have taken a liking to Italian pasta on a trip to Europe in the late 19th century
and asked his royal kitchen to use it; later supplies of imported spaghetti made
the habit stick. The bowl is dressed with bean sprouts, sliced cucumber, pickled
vegetables and chopped herbs, and is often eaten by hand.

Further up the east coast, **laksam** (associated with Kelantan and Terengganu)
is different again: broad, flat sheets of rice-flour noodle rolled up and sliced,
then bathed in a pale, thick gravy of pounded fish and coconut milk. It is mild,
soft and comforting — closer to a rice-noodle porridge than to the sharp Penang
bowl. Kelantan also has its own soupier *laksa* distinct from laksam. These
east-coast and Johor styles show how the same idea travelled and remade itself in
each state's larder — part of Malaysia's broader map of
[regional and ethnic cuisines](/food-lifestyle/regional-and-ethnic-cuisines).

## The common thread

Put the bowls side by side and the pattern is less "one dish, many recipes" than
"one idea, many answers." The table below lines up the major types:

| Type | Region | Broth base | Key ingredients |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Asam laksa | Penang / the north | Sour tamarind fish broth, **no coconut** | Mackerel, torch ginger, laksa leaf, pineapple, prawn paste |
| Curry laksa / laksa lemak | Klang Valley, Melaka (Nyonya) | Rich coconut-milk curry | Tofu puffs, prawns, cockles, fish cake, chilli paste |
| Curry mee | Penang | Coconut-milk curry (same family) | Yellow noodles, cockles, tofu puffs, chilli oil |
| Sarawak laksa | Sarawak (Kuching) | Sambal belacan paste, aromatics | Shredded chicken, prawns, omelette, coriander, vermicelli |
| Johor laksa | Johor | Thick fish-and-coconut gravy | Wolf herring, kerisik, **spaghetti**, herbs |
| Laksam | Kelantan / Terengganu | Pale coconut-and-fish gravy | Rolled flat rice noodles, herbs |

The through-line is unmistakable: fish, rice (or wheat) noodles, a pounded spice
paste, and fresh herbs lifting a deep savoury base. Each region then leans on what
its coast and kitchen offered — tamarind in the north, coconut in the centre and
south, sambal belacan in Borneo — and the Peranakan habit of blending Chinese and
Malay techniques runs underneath nearly all of them. To eat your way across
Malaysia's laksa is to taste its geography.
