That taste comes from the sarsaparilla tradition — historically from the root of plants like Smilax, and in modern soft drinks from flavourings.     a: "They are close cousins. Both belong to the sarsaparilla / root-beer family of herbal-sweet sodas and taste broadly similar. Root beer is the more American name and style; sarsaparilla sodas like Sarsi are the version long popular in Malaysia and the region. If you like one, you will usually like the other."

Not every drink on the Malaysian table is brewed, pulled or pressed. Some come from a
bottle — and few are as fondly remembered as **Sarsi**, the dark, sweet, fizzy
sarsaparilla soda that has been cooling family gatherings and kopitiam meals for
generations. If you have tasted American root beer, you already know roughly what
Sarsi is.

## What Sarsi is

Sarsi is a **carbonated soft drink** flavoured with **sarsaparilla** — a dark, sweet,
faintly herbal-medicinal taste that most people place somewhere very close to **root
beer**. That flavour has deep roots: traditionally it came from the **root of
sarsaparilla plants** (such as *Smilax*), while modern sodas like Sarsi get it largely
from flavourings. The result is a fizzy, sweet, distinctive drink — sweet like a cola,
but with the unmistakable "herbal sweet" character that marks out the sarsaparilla and
root-beer family.

For where Sarsi sits among the country's wider drinks board, see the
[Malaysian drinks](/food-lifestyle/malaysian-drinks) hub. This article is the deep
dive into the sarsaparilla soda itself.

## The F&N brand, and a note on the name

In Malaysia the household name for this drink is **F&N Sarsi**, made by **Fraser &
Neave** — a long-familiar fixture of the chilled shelf. (A word of caution for the
curious: the Malaysian F&N Sarsi is **distinct from an unrelated "Sarsi" brand** used
in some other countries, and precise corporate dates vary between sources, so it is
best to think of F&N as the familiar maker rather than to pin down exact years.) What
matters at the table is the taste, and that has stayed reassuringly constant.

## Sarsi susu: the local float

The most beloved local twist is **Sarsi susu** — Sarsi with **milk**. Pour evaporated,
condensed or fresh milk into the dark soda and it turns creamy and frothy, a homemade
**sarsaparilla float** that softens the sweetness and adds body, a little like an
ice-cream soda. It is a nostalgic treat many Malaysians grew up with, and an easy one
to try: order a Sarsi, add milk, stir. For other sweet, modern favourites it shares
the fridge with, see [Milo dinosaur](/food-lifestyle/milo-dinosaur) and
[bubble tea](/food-lifestyle/bubble-tea).

## A soft drink, not a brew

It is worth placing Sarsi correctly on the drinks board. Unlike **kopi** or **teh
tarik**, it is **not brewed** — it is a bottled or canned fizzy drink, enjoyed chilled
on its own or as Sarsi susu. As a commercial soft drink from a major manufacturer it
**carries halal certification**; as always, the surest check is the **halal mark on
the packaging** itself.
