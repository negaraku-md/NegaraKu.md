Slice into a dark, dense cylinder of gula melaka and the kitchen fills with a
smell somewhere between caramel, toffee and woodsmoke. This is Malaysia's
traditional palm sugar — the sweetener that gives cendol its soul and colours
countless kuih a deep amber. It looks rustic and tastes complex, and for all its
everyday presence in Malaysian cooking, how it is actually made surprises many
people: it comes not from the coconut, but from the coconut palm's *flowers*.

## What gula melaka is

Gula melaka is an unrefined palm sugar — *gula* simply means "sugar" in Bahasa
Melayu. It is produced from the sweet sap of a palm, concentrated by boiling and
set into solid blocks, rather than crystallised and refined like ordinary white
sugar. Because nothing is stripped out, it keeps the minerals, colour and deep
flavour of the raw sap, which is why it ranges from golden brown to almost
black.

Most Malaysian sources identify the source as the sap of the **coconut palm's
flower buds** — the stalk that would otherwise bear the coconuts — not the fruit
itself. This places gula melaka in the broad family of palm sugars that also
includes coconut sugar and sugars tapped from other palms. (Some reference works
instead attribute Malaysian palm sugar to the sugar palm, *Arenga pinnata*, so
the precise species is not universally agreed; what every account shares is that
the sugar comes from palm flower sap.) It is a close relative of what is sold
elsewhere as coconut sugar, though the two are not always identical in form or
processing.

## From coconut flower to sugar block

Making gula melaka is slow, manual work. A tapper climbs the palm and makes a
small incision in the flower-bud stalk, then binds it so the sap drips steadily
into a container hung beneath — a collection that can run for hours. The fresh
sap (*nira*) is sweet and pale, and begins to ferment quickly, so it must be
cooked down soon after it is gathered.

The collected sap is then boiled in large open pans. As the water steams off, the
liquid darkens and thickens into a heavy, glossy syrup. While it is still hot and
pourable, it is ladled into moulds — traditionally lengths of **bamboo**, and
sometimes coconut shells — where it cools and hardens. Tipped out of a bamboo
mould, the sugar takes the short, solid **cylinder** shape that is the most
recognisable form of gula melaka on Malaysian shelves. The result is a sugar that
carries the faint smokiness of the fire it was boiled over.

## The name's link to Malacca

The "melaka" in gula melaka points to the state of Melaka (Malacca), the old
Straits trading port on the peninsula's west coast, where the sugar is widely
said to have originated. That association
is strong enough that the product is known in English as **"Malacca sugar."**

The link is repeated across food writing and is almost certainly rooted in real
regional production, but it is more culinary tradition than firmly documented
history — the exact origins of the name are not settled, and gula melaka is today
produced in several states. It is best treated as a place-name that stuck to a
beloved regional ingredient rather than a precise historical claim.

## Flavour and the forms it comes in

What sets gula melaka apart from plain sugar is its flavour. It is often described
as a smoky, caramelised sweetness with toffee- or butterscotch-like notes —
richer and more rounded than the one-dimensional hit of refined sugar. That depth
is why cooks reach for it when they want a sweet dish to taste of *something*, not
just of sweetness.

It reaches the kitchen in three main forms:

- **Blocks** — the classic hard cylinders or discs, chopped or shaved off as
  needed and melted down for syrups and fillings.
- **Grated or chopped** — the block broken down into granules for easier
  measuring and quicker melting.
- **Syrup** — ready-made liquid gula melaka, often sold bottled, for drizzling
  straight over desserts and drinks.

Because the blocks must be melted (usually with a little water, and often pandan
leaf) before use, many home cooks keep a jar of the syrup on hand as a shortcut.

## How Malaysians cook with it

Gula melaka is overwhelmingly a **dessert** sweetener, and its signature role is
in [cendol](/food-lifestyle/cendol): the dark palm-sugar syrup poured over shaved
ice, green pandan jelly and coconut milk is what makes the dish. It plays a
similar part in other iced sweets in the orbit of
[ais kacang](/food-lifestyle/ais-kacang).

Beyond the ice bowls, it flavours a long list of kuih and puddings. **Sago gula
melaka** — chilled sago pearls with coconut milk and a pour of palm-sugar syrup —
is built around it, as is **onde-onde** (*buah melaka*), the pandan glutinous
rice balls with a molten gula melaka centre that bursts when bitten. It turns up
in steamed and baked kuih, in coconut-based fillings, in ice cream and cakes, and
as a drizzle over everything from waffles to porridge. It appears occasionally in
savoury Malay and Nyonya cooking too, where a little adds roundness and colour.
These uses sit within Malaysia's wider map of [regional and ethnic
cuisines](/food-lifestyle/regional-and-ethnic-cuisines).

## Gula melaka, gula apong and brown sugar

"Palm sugar" is a category, and gula melaka is only one member of it. Malaysians
distinguish it from a few close cousins:

- **Gula apong (gula nipah)** is made from the sap of the **nipah palm** and is
  closely associated with **Sarawak**. It is usually thick, sticky and
  semi-solid rather than set into hard blocks, and often carries a slightly salty
  edge — a staple of Sarawakian treats such as nipah-sugar ice cream.
- **Gula kabung** (also *gula enau* or *gula aren*) is drawn from a different
  palm again and is particularly associated with **Pahang**.

All three differ fundamentally from **brown sugar**, which is refined cane sugar
with molasses added back. Brown sugar can stand in for gula melaka when nothing
else is available, but it lacks the smoky, unrefined palm character — so a cendol
or sago pudding made with it tastes noticeably flatter.

| Sugar | Source palm | Associated region | Typical form |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Gula melaka | Coconut palm sap | Melaka | Hard blocks / cylinders |
| Gula apong | Nipah palm sap | Sarawak | Thick, sticky, semi-solid |
| Gula kabung | Sugar/enau palm sap | Pahang | Blocks / discs |
| Brown sugar | Refined cane + molasses | (not palm) | Loose crystals |

## Regions, quality and authenticity

Gula melaka is made in several parts of Peninsular Malaysia, with Melaka the name
most strongly attached to it and other west-coast states also producing it;
whether Negeri Sembilan and specific districts count as major production centres
is worth confirming against a local source. Smaller, traditional operations still
tap and boil by hand, and their output is prized for depth of flavour.

Quality varies, and so does honesty. Cooks warn that some "gula melaka" on the
market is cut with a high proportion of white cane sugar rather than being pure
palm sap; a common rule of thumb is that genuine gula melaka is comparatively
soft and crumbly and easy to slice, while an adulterated block is harder and
glassier. Malaysia protects certain regional products through the Geographical
Indications Act 2022, but no GI registration specific to gula melaka was found
for this article, so any claim that the name is legally protected should be
checked against the official register before being relied on.
