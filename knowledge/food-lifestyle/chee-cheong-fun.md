Order "chee cheong fun" in George Town and you get cool, silky rolls of rice
noodle smothered in a glossy black sauce and a dark smear of prawn paste. Order
the same words in Kuala Lumpur and a plate arrives alongside stuffed tofu and
fish-paste parcels, dressed in a sweeter sauce. Order it from a dim-sum trolley
and the sheet comes wrapped around barbecued pork, glossed with light soy. One
name, one humble noodle — and a map of very different plates.

## One noodle, many sauces

Chee cheong fun is less a fixed recipe than a canvas. The constant is the
noodle: a thin batter of rice flour (often with a little tapioca or wheat starch)
steamed into a flat sheet, then rolled into soft tubes and cut into bite-sized
lengths. Everything else — the sauces, the garnishes, whether there is a filling
at all — is decided by region and by the stall. That is why a dish beloved in
Penang can look almost unrelated to the one sold a few hours south, and why it
belongs to Malaysia's wider family of
[Malaysian noodles](/food-lifestyle/malaysian-noodles) and
[Chinese-Malaysian cuisine](/food-lifestyle/chinese-malaysian-cuisine).

## The name and the noodle

The Cantonese name, written 猪肠粉 (or 豬腸粉), translates literally as
"pig-intestine noodle." It describes shape, not substance: the rolled, glistening
sheet resembles a length of intestine, and the plain noodle contains no pork at
all. The *fun* (粉) simply means noodle or rice-flour sheet in Cantonese.

The dish is Cantonese in origin. Reference sources trace the rice-noodle roll to
Guangdong province in southern China, where it is a staple of dim-sum culture.
Food writers commonly say the noodle reached Malaya with Chinese immigrants from
Guangdong, who settled in the trading towns and tin-mining districts, though the
usual retellings give no firm date and cite no primary record — so that migration
story is best treated as a well-worn account rather than a documented one.

## Penang and the northern style

Penang's version is the one many Malaysians picture first, and it is defined by
its prawn paste. The warm, sliced rolls are dressed with a trio of sauces: a dark,
thick sweet sauce (Hokkien *thnee cheo*); a black, pungent prawn paste known as
*hae ko* (Hokkien) or *petis udang* in Malay; and a chilli sauce for heat. The
whole plate is finished with a scattering of toasted sesame seeds and a little
oil. By most local accounts the Penang version comes with these condiments and
little else — no filling, no gravy. Some stalls add fried shallots or offer a
curry option, though the shallot garnish is not universal and is better confirmed
locally. The hae ko is the signature note that sets the northern plate apart.

## Kuala Lumpur, Ipoh and the yong-tau-foo plate

Travel south and the dish changes company. In Kuala Lumpur, chee cheong fun is
often served as part of a
[yong tau foo](/food-lifestyle/yong-tau-foo) spread — the rolls sit beside
stuffed tofu, okra, chillies and fish-paste parcels, and the plate is dressed
with a sweeter, bean-based sauce rather than Penang's prawn paste. Many stalls
also offer the rolls doused in gravy: a soupy curry, or, at some shops, a mushroom
gravy with slivers of shiitake.

Ipoh, in Perak, keeps its own habits. The city is known for serving the dish in
both a "dry" form — the rolls with a sweet red sauce — and a "wet" form doused in
curry; the wet, gravy-soaked plate is a familiar comfort there. Whether mushroom
gravy is specifically an Ipoh hallmark or something it shares with Kuala Lumpur is
not firmly settled, and the exact dressing differs from shop to shop. Either way,
chee cheong fun is a fixture of the broader
[Ipoh food](/food-lifestyle/ipoh-food) scene, just as the prawn-paste plate is of
[Penang's](/food-lifestyle/penang-food).

## The Cantonese dim-sum style

Alongside the sauced hawker plates sits the noodle's ancestral form: the dim-sum
roll. Here the freshly steamed sheet is wrapped around a filling before it is
rolled — most often char siu (barbecued pork) or whole prawns, sometimes beef or
a thin omelette — and served soft and warm. Instead of a thick paste, it is
finished with a light, sweetened soy sauce poured over the top. This is the
version found on dim-sum trolleys and in Cantonese restaurants, and it is closest
to how the dish is eaten in Hong Kong and Guangdong, where the sheet is usually
made fresh to order rather than kept warm in a steamer as at many Malaysian
hawker stalls.

## Telling the styles apart

Put the plates side by side and the pattern is clear: the same noodle, dressed by
place. The table below lines up the main Malaysian styles.

| Style | Where | Dressing | Form |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Penang / northern | Penang and the north | Dark sweet sauce, prawn-paste hae ko, chilli, sesame | Plain rolls, no filling |
| Kuala Lumpur | Klang Valley | Sweet bean-based sauce; often with yong tau foo | Plain rolls, sometimes in curry gravy |
| Ipoh | Perak | "Dry" sweet red sauce or "wet" curry/mushroom gravy | Plain rolls, dry or gravy-soaked |
| Cantonese dim sum | Restaurants, dim-sum houses | Light, sweetened soy sauce | Sheet wrapped around char siu or prawn |

The through-line is the noodle itself — soft, slippery, neutral — which is exactly
what lets each region write its own flavour onto it. To eat chee cheong fun
across Malaysia is to taste how one Cantonese sheet learned to speak in several
local accents.
