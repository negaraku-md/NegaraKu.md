Order "laksa" almost anywhere in Peninsular Malaysia and you will get either a
sharp, sour fish broth or a rich coconut curry. Order it in Kuching, across the
South China Sea in Malaysian Borneo, and something else entirely lands in front
of you: a fragrant, faintly creamy bowl, russet with spice paste, piled with
shredded chicken, prawns, ribbons of omelette and a tangle of fine white
noodles. This is Sarawak laksa, and it belongs to a camp of its own.

## What Sarawak laksa is

Sarawak laksa comes from Sarawak, the larger of Malaysia's two Borneo states, and
is most closely tied to its capital, **Kuching**. It is a breakfast staple there,
eaten fresh and hot from coffee shops and hawker stalls as the city wakes up.

The heart of the bowl is its paste — a dense, aromatic blend that some cooks say
runs to more than twenty ingredients. Built around **sambal belacan** (fermented
shrimp paste pounded with chilli), it layers in **galangal, lemongrass, garlic**
and warming spices, then loosens into a broth enriched with **coconut milk** and
sharpened with **tamarind**. The result is savoury and rounded rather than fiery
or sour: the shrimp paste gives it depth, the coconut softens it, and the
tamarind keeps it from turning heavy.

That paste is poured over **fine rice vermicelli** (*bihun*) and dressed with a
familiar Sarawak garnish: poached and shredded **chicken**, blanched **prawns**,
thin strips of **omelette**, crunchy **bean sprouts** and fresh **coriander**. A
wedge of **lime** and an extra spoonful of **sambal belacan** come on the side,
so each diner can dial the brightness and heat up to taste rather than have it
decided in the kitchen.

## The bowl that belongs to no camp

Malaysia's [laksa family](/food-lifestyle/laksa) splits broadly into two
styles, and Sarawak laksa sits outside both. Penang's **asam laksa** is a sour,
tamarind-soured fish broth with **no coconut** and a cool, crunchy garnish of
pineapple, cucumber and torch ginger. **Curry laksa** (also called laksa lemak)
goes the other way — a rich **coconut-and-curry-powder** gravy loaded with tofu
puffs, cockles and prawns.

Sarawak laksa shares a little with each and matches neither. It has coconut, like
curry laksa, but no curry powder; it carries tamarind, like asam laksa, but is
not built around sourness. Its signature is **sambal belacan**, and its toppings
— chicken, prawns and omelette over vermicelli — look nothing like the Peninsular
bowls. Where asam laksa and curry laksa define themselves by one dominant note,
Sarawak laksa is prized for balance.

## "Breakfast of the gods"

Sarawak laksa's best-known champion came from abroad. The late American chef and
travel presenter **Anthony Bourdain** ate the dish at a Kuching coffee shop while
filming, and the phrase **"breakfast of the gods"** has been widely attributed to
him ever since — a line Sarawak now wears with pride. He returned to film the dish
again years later and spoke of it often, giving the bowl a profile well beyond
Borneo.

The origins of the dish itself are hazier. Secondary accounts credit a
mid-20th-century Kuching hawker with popularising it, but sources acknowledge the
true beginnings are uncertain, so the history is best treated with caution. What
is not in doubt is its place today: Sarawak laksa is the state's signature plate
and a fixture of Kuching mornings.

## Where it fits

Sarawak laksa is one of the clearest illustrations of how far a shared name can
travel across Malaysia's
[regional and ethnic cuisines](/food-lifestyle/regional-and-ethnic-cuisines).
Pastes and ready-made broths now carry it to the peninsula and overseas, but the
bowl at its best is still a Kuching breakfast — a spoonful of Borneo in a city
that treats it as a point of pride.
