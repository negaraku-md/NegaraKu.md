Unwrap a parcel of otak-otak and the first thing you meet is the smell: charred
banana leaf, chilli and the sea. Inside is a soft, spiced fish paste — sometimes
thin and smoky off the grill, sometimes thick and almost custard-like from the
steamer. It is a snack, a side dish and, in towns like Muar, a reason for people
to make the drive. The name, oddly, means "brains."

## What otak-otak is

At its simplest, otak-otak is fish made into a paste, seasoned with a pounded
spice mixture (*rempah*) and coconut milk, wrapped in a leaf, and cooked over
fire or steam. The fish is usually mackerel — *ikan tenggiri* (Spanish mackerel)
is the common choice — though other firm white fish are used. The paste is
wrapped in banana, coconut or nipa-palm (*attap*) leaf, which both holds the
mixture in shape and perfumes it as it cooks.

Beyond that shared idea, the bowl — or rather the parcel — varies by region and
community. The leaf, the shape, the colour, whether it is grilled or steamed, and
how soft or firm it finishes all shift from one town to the next. Otak-otak sits
comfortably among Malaysia's
[signature dishes](/food-lifestyle/national-dishes-of-malaysia) and, like
[laksa](/food-lifestyle/laksa), it is less a single recipe than a family of
closely related ones.

## The name: a dish called "brains"

*Otak* means "brains" in Malay and Indonesian, and the name is almost always
explained as a description of how the dish looks. The soft, pale, greyish fish
mixture was said to resemble brain matter, and the name stuck. It is folk
etymology rather than documented linguistics — a comment on texture and colour,
not on any ingredient — and there is no brain in the dish. The explanation
recurs across Malaysian, Indonesian and Singaporean sources, which agree on the
gist even where the details differ.

## Muar and the southern grilled style

In Malaysia, the most famous otak-otak comes from **Muar**, a riverside town in
Johor. The southern style — shared broadly across Johor and Melaka — is thin,
flat and often rectangular, wrapped in coconut or banana leaf and grilled over
charcoal, which leaves it drier and smokier than the steamed versions. Muar
parcels are frequently wrapped in *attap* (nipa-palm) leaf and clipped shut at
both ends.

Sources describe its colour differently: some call the southern grilled style
reddish-orange, from the chillies and spices in the paste, while others emphasise
the greyish, browned look it takes on from the charcoal — and the closely related
Terengganu snack *satar* is the one most reliably described as grey. Whatever the
exact shade, Muar's reputation is secure: it draws visitors from neighbouring
states and from Singapore, and in February 2025 "otak-otak Muar" was served
alongside laksa Johor to ASEAN delegates at a welcome dinner in Desaru as a taste
of the state's cooking.

## Penang and the Nyonya custardy style

Head north and otak-otak changes character. The **Nyonya** (Peranakan) version
associated with Penang is thick rather than thin, shaped like a parcel or
triangular block, and steamed in banana leaf instead of grilled. The result is
much softer — wet, succulent and almost custard-like — and a signature touch is
the wild betel leaf (*daun kaduk*) laid in with the paste. These steamed Nyonya
parcels are commonly coloured yellow to orange by turmeric and chilli, and some
cooks enrich them with prawn, squid or crab.

The Peranakan link is the thread that ties otak-otak to much of the Straits'
hybrid cooking: Straits-born Chinese kitchens married Chinese techniques with
Malay spice pastes, and otak-otak — like several
[regional and ethnic cuisines](/food-lifestyle/regional-and-ethnic-cuisines) of
Malaysia — carries that blend. The contrast between a dry, grilled Johor parcel
and a soft, steamed Penang one is the clearest way to see how far a single idea
can travel within one country.

## Ingredients and preparation

The heart of otak-otak is its *rempah*. A typical Malaysian paste blends dried
and fresh chillies, turmeric, lemongrass, shallots, garlic, galangal and ginger,
loosened with coconut milk; some recipes add candlenut, though not all do. The
spice paste is blended smooth and often fried briefly to deepen its flavour,
then folded into the ground fish along with a little starch, egg, sugar and salt
to bind and season the mixture.

The seasoned paste is spooned onto a softened leaf, folded into a parcel and
secured — stapled, pinned with a toothpick or tied — before cooking. Traditional
methods are charcoal-grilling, which chars the leaf and dries the surface, and
steaming, which keeps the inside moist and soft; home cooks also bake or
pan-cook it. The choice of leaf (banana, coconut or nipa palm) and method is much
of what separates one regional style from another.

## A dish shared across the Straits

Otak-otak is not Malaysia's alone. It is eaten throughout the Straits of Malacca
region — in Indonesia, Malaysia and Singapore, where it is known as *otah* — and
its earliest roots are commonly traced to **Palembang** in South Sumatra, with
the dish often described as a blend of Malay (Palembangese) and Peranakan
cooking. Indonesian versions, including those of Jakarta and Makassar, tend to be
paler and are served with dipping sauces such as *cuko* or peanut sauce, while
the Malaysian and Singaporean styles are usually reddish-orange from chilli and
spice.

Because the dish is genuinely shared, single-country origin claims should be
treated with caution: the precise history is not firmly documented, and more than
one place has a strong claim to it. What is clear is that otak-otak belongs to a
common Straits larder, and that each community — Malay, Peranakan, Indonesian,
Singaporean — has made its own parcel from the same idea.

## Where it is famous and what's next

Within Malaysia, Muar is the name most associated with otak-otak, to the point
that "Muar otak-otak" functions almost as a brand; Penang and Melaka are the
homes of the softer Nyonya style. Across the region, Palembang and Singapore
round out the picture. Wherever it is made, the formula holds: fish, a spice
paste, coconut, and a leaf to carry the whole thing through fire or steam.
