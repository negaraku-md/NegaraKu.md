Few drinks announce themselves as clearly as **three-layer tea**. Set down in a tall
glass, it arrives in three neat bands — dark at the bottom, pale in the middle, amber
on top — a small feat of kitchen physics and one of **Sarawak's** proudest drinks. Its
proper name is **teh C peng special**, and the first rule is: admire it, then stir it.

## The three layers

From the bottom up, the layers are **palm sugar**, **evaporated milk**, and **black
tea**. They stay apart because each differs in density and sugar content, so poured
carefully they settle into bands rather than mixing. The bottom layer is traditionally
**gula apong** — a dark, thick palm sugar made from the **nipah palm**, a distinctly
Sarawakian ingredient — though many stalls now use **gula melaka** (Malacca palm sugar)
as an easier substitute. The effect is striking, but the drink is meant to be **stirred
together** before you drink, so that caramel-dark sugar sweetens the whole glass into a
creamy iced tea.

For where three-layer tea sits among the country's wider drinks board, see the
[Malaysian drinks](/food-lifestyle/malaysian-drinks) hub. This article is the deep dive
into the Sarawak layered tea itself.

## What "teh C peng special" means

The name is coffee-shop code. **"Teh"** is tea; **"peng"** is the Hokkien word for
**iced**; and **"C"** refers to **evaporated milk** — its exact origin is debated, with
one common story tracing it to the **Carnation** brand of evaporated milk and another
simply treating "C" as the local code for evaporated milk. **"Special"** marks the
layered version. So teh C peng special is, literally, an iced evaporated-milk tea —
served, uniquely, in layers. For the wider logic of this ordering language, see
[kopi, teh tarik and coffee-shop drinks](/food-lifestyle/kopi-teh-tarik-and-coffeeshop-drinks).

## A Kuching original

Three-layer tea is **Sarawakian**, and widely traced to the **7th Mile (Batu 7)** area
of **Kuching**. Accounts point to a stall there as its birthplace, though the specific
originator is not firmly documented — so it is best called a Kuching 7th Mile creation
rather than pinned to one name. From there it became a **signature East Malaysian
drink**, and ambitious versions with extra layers — adding pandan syrup or grass jelly —
have followed. Alongside [teh tarik](/food-lifestyle/teh-tarik), it is one of the
country's most recognisable tea drinks.

## Where to find it

Look for three-layer tea across **Sarawak**, especially around **Kuching**, at
kopitiam, food courts and hawker stalls — and increasingly at Sarawakian eateries
elsewhere in the country. It is a point of local pride, and a drink worth ordering just
to watch it arrive.
