Few Malaysian dishes announce themselves as loudly as nasi kerabu. The rice
arrives a startling blue — not food-dye neon, but a soft, natural indigo — heaped
with a thicket of raw herbs and ringed by little piles of grated coconut, fried
fish, stuffed chillies and a dark, pungent sauce. It looks less like a plate of
rice than a painter's palette, and it is one of the most recognisable foods of
Malaysia's east coast.

## What nasi kerabu is

At its simplest, nasi kerabu is rice tossed with a salad of finely sliced raw
herbs and served with an array of condiments and side dishes. The name itself is
the clue: *kerabu* is the Malay term for a salad of shredded vegetables and herbs
dressed with lime, chilli and often toasted coconut, so "nasi kerabu" is, almost
literally, "salad rice." What sets it apart from an ordinary plate of rice and
sides is that the herbs are meant to be mixed *into* the rice, so that every
mouthful carries the grassy, aromatic lift of the *ulam* (raw local greens and
herbs eaten as part of a meal).

That herb-forward assembly, together with its east-coast condiments, places nasi
kerabu firmly within [Malay cuisine](/food-lifestyle/malay-cuisine) and the wider
map of Malaysia's [regional and ethnic cuisines](/food-lifestyle/regional-and-ethnic-cuisines).
It is a composed dish rather than a single cooked item — closer in spirit to a
build-your-own bowl than to a stew — and no two stalls plate it quite the same
way.

## The blue rice

The dish's signature is its colour, and it is entirely natural. The blue comes
from the petals of the butterfly-pea flower — *bunga telang* in Malay,
*Clitoria ternatea* botanically — which are steeped, fresh or dried, in warm
water to release a deep blue pigment. That coloured water then replaces plain
water when the rice is cooked. The flower contributes colour far more than
flavour, and the traditional method uses no artificial dye. The cooking water is
often perfumed further with lemongrass, kaffir lime leaf, pandan and ginger.

Despite the fame of the blue version, nasi kerabu is not always blue. Cooks also
make a yellow version coloured with turmeric, and a plain white one — the rice is
the canvas, and the colour is a choice rather than a rule. Still, it is the blue
rice that has made the dish a visual icon, the detail that puts nasi kerabu on
"must try" lists of Malaysian food.

## The herbs and the kerisik

If the colour is what you notice first, the herbs are what the dish is really
about. Freshly chopped *ulam* are mixed through the rice, and the combination
varies by cook and region — common choices include torch-ginger bud
(*bunga kantan*), long beans, bean sprouts, cucumber, shredded cabbage and
fragrant leaves such as Vietnamese coriander (*daun kesum*). The proportion of
greenery is generous; some accounts describe a near one-to-one ratio of herbs to
rice, which makes the plate as much salad as grain.

Binding it together is *kerisik* — grated coconut toasted until golden and
pounded, sometimes with flaked fish, shallots and lemongrass, into a coarse,
nutty paste. Scattered over the rice, kerisik adds a toasty richness that
balances the brightness of the raw herbs. It is one of the seasonings most
closely identified with the dish.

## Budu, sambal and the condiments

The flavour that most defines nasi kerabu is *budu*. Budu is a fermented-fish
sauce — typically anchovies cured with salt, often with tamarind — and it is a
cornerstone of the Kelantan and Terengganu kitchen. Drizzled over the rice, its
salty, deeply savoury pungency is, for many east-coast Malaysians, the taste of
home. The national culture department's heritage record describes budu as the
dish's main dipping sauce.

Where budu is not used, or alongside it, cooks reach for *sambal* — including a
coconut sambal (*sambal nyior*) that suits the dish well. Between the budu or
sambal, the kerisik and the lime that usually finishes the plate, the seasoning
pulls in several directions at once: salty, sour, toasty and herbal. That layered
contrast is the point.

## The proteins and the sides

Around the rice sits a supporting cast that can turn a modest plate into a feast.
The protein is usually fried or grilled: fried chicken or fish, or — most prized —
*ayam percik*, chicken grilled and basted in a sweet, spiced coconut sauce. By
Tourism Malaysia's account, the Kelantan style of ayam percik runs sweeter than
versions elsewhere.

The classic sides are just as important as the main. *Solok lada* — green chillies
stuffed with grated coconut (and sometimes fish) — is described as one of the
dish's signature accompaniments. A *telur masin* (salted duck egg), halved and
often served in its shell, adds a rich, briny note. *Keropok* (fried crackers)
bring the crunch, crumbled over the top or eaten alongside. The full spread is
best eaten by mixing a little of everything with each spoonful, so that the rice,
herbs, sauce and sides all meet at once.

## Where it comes from

Nasi kerabu is, by broad agreement, a dish of Malaysia's north-eastern coast. The
national culture department's heritage record calls it "closely associated with
the East Coast of Peninsular Malaysia, particularly the state of Kelantan," while
other sources extend its home to neighbouring Terengganu as well — so it is best
thought of as a Kelantanese dish with a wider east-coast family, rather than the
property of a single state.

Its deeper origins are harder to pin down and should be treated with caution. The
same heritage record notes that *some scholars* suggest the dish began among the
Peranakan community of Kelantan around the 15th century — an attractive story, but
one that is only loosely documented. What is better attested is the dish's reach:
it has spread well beyond the east coast, and a close relative, *khao yam*, is
eaten across the border in southern Thailand's Malay-majority Patani region, where
it is sometimes read as a marker of Malay identity.

## Nasi kerabu versus nasi ulam

Visitors often confuse nasi kerabu with *nasi ulam*, and the two are indeed close.
Both are "herb rice" dishes that mix cooked rice with a salad of finely chopped
raw herbs and vegetables, and sources describe them as similar in composition. The
practical distinction is the rice itself: nasi kerabu is defined by its coloured
rice — above all the butterfly-pea blue — and by its east-coast condiments such as
budu and solok lada, whereas nasi ulam is typically served with plain or only
lightly flavoured rice. The boundary is blurry, and plenty of home cooks blur it
further.

Set beside Malaysia's other great rice plates — the coconut-rich
[nasi lemak](/food-lifestyle/nasi-lemak) chief among them — nasi kerabu stakes out
its own corner of the table: cooler, greener, sharper, and unmistakably blue. To
eat it is to taste the herb gardens and fermenting crocks of the east coast on a
single plate.
