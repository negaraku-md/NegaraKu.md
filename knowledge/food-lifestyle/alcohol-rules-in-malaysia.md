Order a beer in a Kuala Lumpur bar and it is entirely routine. Try to buy the same beer in parts of Kelantan and you may find no licensed outlet at all. Malaysia does not have one alcohol rulebook; it has a federal licensing system, a separate set of religious laws that apply only to Muslims, and state governments that each add their own conditions on top. The result is a patchwork that a visitor, a resident and a restaurant owner each experience differently.

This guide sets out the main rules as they stand, without arguing for or against any of them.

## Who governs alcohol in Malaysia?

At the federal level, the sale of alcoholic beverages is licensed under the **Excise Act 1976**. The retail sale of alcohol on premises must be licensed under that Act, and any licence for such sale requires the approval of the **Licensing Board** in accordance with **Section 35** of the Act, according to the Ministry of Finance.

Licensing authority is not exercised directly by the federal government. Through an order gazetted in 1977, the Minister of Finance delegated the power to the Menteri Besar or Chief Minister of each state. That delegation remains in effect, the Ministry of Finance has confirmed, which is a large part of why practice varies from state to state.

In day-to-day terms, businesses interact with several layers:

- The **Royal Malaysian Customs Department (RMCD)** administers excise duty and the Excise Act framework.
- The **Licensing Board** approves licences under Section 35.
- **Local authorities** (city and municipal councils) process the common licence categories for a given business address.

## What licences does a business need?

A business that wants to sell alcohol generally applies for one of a few standard categories. Industry guidance describes three common types:

| Licence type | Typical use |
| --- | --- |
| Retail | Selling packaged alcohol to consumers for off-premise consumption |
| Wholesale | Selling to retailers and other wholesalers |
| Public house | Serving alcohol for on-premise consumption at food-and-beverage outlets |

These are administered through the local authority for the district where the business is located, so specific requirements, fees and conditions differ between councils. This breakdown reflects commercial industry guidance rather than a single official schedule, so a business should confirm the current categories and fees directly with its local authority. Selling without the required licence is an offence and can bring fines, loss of the licence, or other penalties.

## How old must you be to buy or drink?

The nationwide minimum age to buy or consume alcohol is **21**, raised from 18 with effect from **1 December 2017** under the Food (Amendment) Regulations 2016.

The same reforms introduced other controls reported at the time, including:

- A health-warning requirement, so that alcoholic beverages carry a notice that drinking alcohol is harmful to health, also displayed at points of sale.
- Restrictions on **compounded hard liquor (CHL)**, which was reported to be sold only in glass bottles of a minimum 700 ml volume.

Penalties were set for non-compliance. According to reporting on the regulations, breaching the labelling requirements can carry a fine of up to RM10,000 or up to two years' imprisonment, while failing to meet the CHL packaging rules can carry a fine of up to RM5,000 or up to two years' imprisonment. These figures should be confirmed against the gazetted text of the regulations before they are relied on.

## Why do the rules change from state to state?

Two systems operate in parallel, and this is where the topic becomes sensitive.

The first is the **civil, federal** system described above, which licenses sale and sets the buying age for the general public. The second is **state Syariah law**. Malaysia's states each enact Syariah criminal offences legislation that applies **only to Muslims**, and these enactments prohibit the consumption and sale of alcohol by Muslims. Penalties are handled by the Syariah courts and differ between states. This dual structure — civil courts for the general population and Syariah courts for Muslims on personal and religious matters — is a defining feature of Malaysian law.

Because licensing is delegated to state leaders and because states set their own religious-law conditions, the practical picture varies widely:

- **Kelantan and Terengganu**, both governed under strongly Islamic policy platforms, are generally reported to apply the most restrictive local conditions, with few licensed outlets and additional conditions. In December 2024, Malaysian news outlets reported that the Kelantan Islamic Religious Affairs Department (JAHEIK) had notified hotels not to serve alcohol at events, with reporting stating this affected non-Muslim wedding banquets and that some banquets were cancelled as a result. The reporting was based on accounts from hotel operators and local politicians rather than a published official notice, and the directive's formal status and scope were not independently confirmed.
- Most other states permit licensed sale to non-Muslims through the ordinary channels, with alcohol widely available in shops, restaurants and bars.

Views on these arrangements differ. Supporters frame state-level restrictions as upholding Islamic values and local religious sensitivities; critics argue they can affect non-Muslims and businesses that fall outside Syariah jurisdiction. This guide records that both positions exist without endorsing either.

## Where is alcohol duty-free?

Some Malaysian islands hold **duty-free status**, meaning goods are not subject to the usual customs duties. For alcohol, the most prominent are **Langkawi** (Kedah) and **Labuan**, which official customs guidance lists as duty-free zones. Consumer guidance also describes Tioman and Pangkor as duty-free islands, though it reports that tobacco products became taxable there under Budget 2021; these island details rest on secondary guidance and should be confirmed with customs.

For travellers entering Malaysia, the standard duty-free allowance is **wine, spirits or malt liquor not exceeding one litre in total**. This allowance is per person and cannot be pooled between travellers.

Eligibility is generally tied to how long you have been away. The aeronautical information publication of the Civil Aviation Authority of Malaysia lists a general minimum absence of **72 hours**, reduced for the duty-free islands:

| Location | Minimum absence for the allowance |
| --- | --- |
| General entry to Malaysia | 72 hours |
| Langkawi | 48 hours |
| Labuan | 24 hours |

These figures are not fully consistent across published sources: one consumer-law source cited here notes that the customs department's current published allowance tables do not specify a minimum-absence period, and describes the 72/48/24-hour figures as drawn from older materials. Because the allowance is enforced at the point of entry and the rules change periodically, travellers carrying alcohol should confirm the current figures and conditions directly with the Royal Malaysian Customs Department before travelling.

## What's next

- If you run or plan a food-and-beverage or retail business, confirm which licence category applies with the **local authority** for your address, since conditions and fees are set locally.
- If you are travelling with alcohol, check the current customs allowance and any minimum-absence rules before you fly, and remember the one-litre limit is per person.
- If your question touches religious law — for Muslims, or for events in states such as Kelantan and Terengganu — the relevant framework is state Syariah legislation, which is separate from the federal licence.
