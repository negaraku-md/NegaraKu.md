Watch a murtabak being made at a Ramadan bazaar and the theatre is half the
appeal: a ball of dough is slapped and stretched paper-thin on an oiled griddle,
a slick of spiced minced meat, beaten egg and chopped onion is ladled across it,
and the edges are folded inward into a plump square that sizzles until its skin
turns golden and shatter-crisp. Cut into pieces and eaten with a dab of curry and
a few sharp pickled onions, it is one of the most satisfying things to come off a
mamak griddle — and one of the dishes Malaysians most associate with the fasting
month.

## One pancake, a pocketful of filling

Murtabak is, at heart, a stuffed and folded flatbread. A thin sheet of dough is
loaded with a savoury filling — most often minced meat bound with beaten egg and
diced onion — then folded over on itself into a square parcel and pan-fried until
the outside is crisp and the inside set. It is cut into blocks and served hot.

The usual meats are beef, chicken or mutton (goat), seasoned with curry spices
and sometimes garlic. Compared with some neighbouring styles, Malaysian murtabak
is often said to lean more heavily on egg and onion and to carry proportionally
less meat, giving it a softer, more omelette-like interior. It arrives with
accompaniments rather than sauces mixed in: a small dish of curry gravy or
[dhal](/food-lifestyle/indian-malaysian-cuisine) to spoon or pour over, and a
scatter of sliced, lightly pickled onions whose acidity cuts the richness.

## Built on roti canai dough

What gives murtabak its particular texture is its base. It is built on the same
stretched, oil-slicked dough used for [roti canai](/food-lifestyle/roti-canai),
the flaky griddle bread of Malaysia's Indian-Muslim kitchens — which is why the
two so often share a stall and a cook. Where roti canai is tossed, folded and
griddled plain, murtabak takes that same thin sheet and wraps it around a
substantial filling, so the result is thicker, heavier and closer to a meal than
a snack.

The mechanics are simple to watch and hard to master. The dough is stretched
until almost translucent, the filling spread across the centre, and the four
sides folded in to seal everything into a neat square; it then cooks on the flat
iron until both faces are browned and the egg within has just set. Many stall
cooks guard their exact spice blends, and the best are often trained over years
under an established murtabak maker.

## A mamak and nasi kandar staple

Murtabak belongs to the world of Malaysia's Indian-Muslim food — the
[mamak](/food-lifestyle/kopitiam-mamak-culture) stalls and
[nasi kandar](/food-lifestyle/nasi-kandar) shops that are among the country's
most beloved places to eat. In Penang, long a stronghold of the dish, it is a
fixture of the nasi kandar restaurants, usually stuffed with meat and brought to
the table with a curry dip and pickled onion.

Because it sits within this Indian-Muslim tradition, murtabak is part of the
broader story of how South Indian and Tamil-Muslim migrants shaped Malaysian
eating. It shares that lineage with roti canai, teh tarik and the rich, layered
curries of the nasi kandar counter — a cuisine that has become thoroughly
Malaysian while keeping its distinct identity.

## The star of the Ramadan bazaar

Murtabak is available year-round, but it comes into its own during Ramadan.
At the [Ramadan bazaars](/food-lifestyle/ramadan-bazaars-and-fasting-month-food)
that spring up across the country for the fasting month, it is one of the
signature draws — hearty enough to break a fast, portable enough to carry home,
and made to order in full view of the queue.

The scale can be striking. One bazaar trader in Bukit Mertajam told *The Star* in
2022 that he sold around 400 pieces a day, pricing chicken and beef murtabak at a
few ringgit each and a cheese version higher, and said Ramadan was "a much-awaited
time" because sales ran well above normal days. Bazaar cooks also experiment: *The
Star* has reported stalls offering unusual fillings such as rabbit and venison
alongside the traditional meats, a reminder that the dish keeps evolving even as
its core stays the same.

## A name that means "folded"

The word itself points back to the dish's form and its travels. "Murtabak" comes
from the Arabic *mutabbaq*, meaning "folded" — a direct reference to the way the
dough is folded over its filling. Variants of the name follow it across the
region, most notably *martabak* in Indonesia.

Its deeper origins are genuinely contested, and reputable accounts disagree.
Several trace the dish to Yemen, which historically had a sizeable Indian
population, with Indian and Tamil-Muslim traders carrying it eastward into
Southeast Asia; others point further back still, to a folded dough dish recorded
in a 13th-century Abbasid cookbook. No single account is settled, and the honest
summary is that murtabak is a dish of the Indian Ocean trade routes rather than of
any one country — which is exactly why it reads as at home in Yemen, India,
Malaysia, Singapore and Indonesia alike.

## Sweet cousins and regional variants

Most murtabak is savoury, but the folded-pancake idea has sweeter relatives, and
the naming can be confusing. In Malaysia the familiar sweet folded pancake — thick
and filled with sugar, peanuts, sweetcorn or similar — is generally known as apam
balik rather than murtabak, while in Indonesia the sweet version is called
*martabak manis* and the savoury egg-and-meat one *martabak telur* (also labelled
*martabak Mesir*, "Egyptian martabak"). Some Malaysian vendors do sell dessert
versions billed as sweet murtabak, including ones stuffed with banana (*pisang*)
and condensed milk, but whether these count as a distinct, widely recognised style
or simply a stall novelty is less clear, and the "Mesir" label in particular tends
to denote a savoury dish rather than a sweet one.

Savoury variants are easier to pin down. Beyond the standard beef, chicken and
mutton, cheese-filled murtabak — often with mozzarella — has become a popular
upgrade at bazaars and stalls, and cooks vary the spice mix and the amount of egg
to taste. Across the causeway, Singaporean and Johorean versions are frequently
described as meatier than the egg-forward Malaysian norm, a small difference that
regulars notice.
