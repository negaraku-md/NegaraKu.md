The scene is a fixture of Malaysian life: a man at a mamak stall holding a battered
metal mug high above his head and a tin cup low by his waist, a ribbon of milky tea
stretching between them in a long, unbroken arc, cup after cup, until the drink lands
capped with a thick head of foam. This is *teh tarik* — literally "pulled tea" — and
the theatre of the pour is not a gimmick but the thing that makes the drink what it is.
It is, by broad consensus, Malaysia's national drink, and it is poured a hundred thousand
times a day across the country.

## What teh tarik is

At its simplest, teh tarik is a hot milk tea: strong black tea sweetened and enriched
with condensed milk. The tea is usually brewed strong — often from tea dust rather than
whole leaves — and strained, then combined with sweetened condensed milk, sometimes with
extra sugar; evaporated milk is also used. The result is dark amber, sweet and full-bodied,
quite unlike a delicate afternoon tea.

What separates teh tarik from an ordinary cup of milk tea is the pull. Remove the pour
and you simply have *teh susu* — milk tea — a distinction the companion
[kopitiam drinks guide](/food-lifestyle/kopi-teh-tarik-and-coffeeshop-drinks) draws as
well. For where teh tarik sits among the country's wider drinks board — sirap bandung,
cincau, Milo and the rest — see the [Malaysian drinks](/food-lifestyle/malaysian-drinks)
hub. This article is the deep dive into the pulled tea itself.

## The pull, and why it matters

"Tarik" means "to pull" in Malay, and it names the defining move: the hot sweet tea is
poured repeatedly between two containers held far apart, the liquid drawn out into long
arcs. The pour does real, practical work. It cools the tea from brewing heat down to
drinking temperature, it blends the tea thoroughly with the thick condensed milk, and
it aerates the drink into a frothy head. Vendors alternate short and long pulls to build
that foam; too short, and the tea takes in too little air to froth properly.

The craft is exacting. Senthil Kumar, a tea master at the long-running ZamZam restaurant
in Kuala Lumpur, told Arab News that the art lies in alternating short and long pulls and
"slamming" the liquid into the cup below for a good mix and froth, and that too much
condensed milk leaves the tea too dense to foam well. Practitioners favour metal — silver
or stainless-steel vessels rather than glass — for the heat and the throw. Done at speed,
from arm's length, without spilling a drop, the pour becomes a performance: skilled
pullers become local celebrities, and *teh tarik* competitions judging flair and froth
draw crowds. One food writer called it "the world's most athletic tea ritual."

## Mamak roots

Teh tarik's story is bound up with the **mamak** — the Indian-Muslim eateries that are
one of Malaysia's great culinary institutions. The drink is traced to Indian-Muslim
communities who settled in the Malay Peninsula during British colonial rule and ran
roadside tea stalls; over time those stalls grew into the mamak eateries that now dot
every town. Arab News describes teh tarik as coming "from the Mamak community, descendants
of Indian Muslims who settled in the Malay Peninsula centuries ago," most arriving under
British rule, who "built roadside eateries that became Malaysia's iconic Mamak stalls."

The finer timeline is murkier, and worth treating with care. One widely cited account,
attributed to the BBC and repeated on Wikipedia, has black tea introduced to the region
in the 1830s, a South Indian street-cook pulling craft developing after 1850, stalls
serving *masala chai* at rubber-plantation entrances from the 1870s, and — after the
Second World War — a switch to cheaper tea dust, with condensed milk added to mask its
bitterness, producing teh tarik in roughly its modern form. These dates are plausible but
not firmly documented, and are best read as the popular history rather than settled fact.

Two things, though, are not in dispute: the drink is non-alcoholic, and in the mamak
setting it is halal. That combination is a large part of why teh tarik became a genuinely
shared drink — a cup anyone at the table, of any community, could order together. For the
institution itself, see [kopitiam and mamak culture](/food-lifestyle/kopitiam-mamak-culture).

## Teh tarik and the order code

Teh tarik is the sweet default, but it sits inside the same modular ordering grammar that
governs every drink at a mamak or coffee shop. The base word *teh* arrives with milk and
sugar unless you say otherwise, and you modify it with short markers:

| You say | You get |
| --- | --- |
| teh tarik | Pulled sweet milk tea (the default) |
| teh-O | Black tea, no milk, with sugar |
| teh-C | Tea with evaporated milk |
| teh ais / teh peng | Iced tea |
| teh halia | Tea with ginger |
| kurang manis | Less sugar |

So "teh-O ais limau" is iced black tea with lime, and "teh tarik kurang manis" is the
pulled tea made less sweet — a common request, since the standard version is frankly
sugary. The full logic of *O*, *C*, *kosong*, *gao* and *peng* is laid out in the
[kopi, teh tarik and coffee-shop drinks](/food-lifestyle/kopi-teh-tarik-and-coffeeshop-drinks)
guide; this article stays with the pulled tea itself.

## National drink and heritage

Malaysia treats teh tarik as its national drink. The claim is cultural rather than a
single official decree — Arab News notes it is "often referred to as Malaysia's national
drink" without citing a formal designation, and Wikipedia records simply that "Malaysia
considers it its national drink" — but the status is real in everyday life, where teh
tarik stands as a shorthand for the country's multi-ethnic, share-a-table culture,
especially around Malaysia Day.

Formal recognition has come at the level of the shared breakfast tradition. In 2024, at
the 19th session of its Intergovernmental Committee, UNESCO inscribed **"Breakfast culture
in Malaysia: dining experience in a multi-ethnic society"** on the Representative List of
the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity (Reference 02113; Decision 19.COM 7.B.47).
The inscription covers a living tradition of preparing and sharing breakfast, and names
staple foods such as *nasi lemak* and *roti canai* alongside the drink *teh tarik* — so
teh tarik is recognised as part of that heritage, not as a standalone item. Ahead of the
inscription, Malaysia showcased nasi lemak, roti canai and teh tarik at the UN office in
Geneva in June 2024. The drink has also been discussed as a nationally gazetted heritage
food under the National Heritage Act 2005, but reporting on the year is inconsistent, so
that gazettement is noted here rather than dated.

## Variations and its neighbours

The pulled tea has spawned a family of cousins. **Teh halia** laces the tea with ginger;
masala-spiced versions borrow cardamom, cloves and cinnamon from the Indian kitchen;
**teh tarik madu** adds honey; **kopi tarik** applies the same pour to coffee; and the
iced **teh ais** is the hot-weather default. The pull itself crossed borders long ago —
versions of pulled milk tea are sold across Singapore, Brunei, Indonesia and Thailand —
which is exactly why the question of who invented it has no clean answer. Both Malaysia
and Singapore, which separated in 1965, claim it as their own, and no authoritative source
settles the matter.
