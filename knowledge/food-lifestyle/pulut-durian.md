Few desserts are as unapologetically rich as pulut durian: a mound of warm,
coconut-steamed glutinous rice set against the thick, sweet, heady flesh of
durian. The rice is quietly savoury, even faintly salty; the fruit is loud and
custardy. Put the two in the same mouthful and each makes the other taste more
like itself — which is exactly the point.

## What pulut durian is

*Pulut* is the Malay word for glutinous (sticky) rice, and pulut durian is
simply that rice served with durian. The rice is steamed with **coconut milk
(santan)** and a pinch of **salt** until tender, so that on its own it is mild
and savoury rather than sweet. The sweetness comes entirely from the durian.

It is a Malay dessert, and durian is one of Malaysia's most beloved fruits —
eaten with enthusiasm across the country's communities. You can read more about
the fruit, its prized varieties and how its seasons work in our guide to
[durian culture and varieties](/food-lifestyle/durian-culture-and-varieties),
and about the wider tradition it belongs to in our overview of
[Malay cuisine](/food-lifestyle/malay-cuisine).

## What goes into it

The ingredient list is short: glutinous rice, coconut milk, salt, durian, and
usually some sugar or palm sugar for the durian element. Pandan (screwpine)
leaves are commonly added for fragrance.

A typical preparation steams the salted pulut until cooked, then brings the
durian flesh together with coconut milk, sugar and pandan and simmers it until it
thickens into a sauce. The warm rice is served with that durian-coconut mixture
spooned over the top. The salt in the rice is deliberate — it keeps the dish from
becoming cloying and sharpens the perfume of the fruit.

## Versions: fresh fruit or a durian gravy

There is no single correct way to build the dish, and the standard here is
descriptive, not prescriptive. Two forms are common:

- **With fresh durian** — the warm pulut is topped with fresh durian flesh and a
  pour of coconut milk, letting the fruit's own texture and aroma lead.
- **With a cooked gravy** — the durian is cooked down with santan and sugar into
  a smooth, sweet sauce, which coats the rice more evenly.

Beyond that, cooks vary the ratio of coconut milk to durian, how much sugar or
palm sugar they use, and whether pandan is a leaf or a paste. Durian itself sits
among Malaysia's great
[tropical fruits](/food-lifestyle/tropical-fruits-malaysia), and the variety
in the bowl shapes the final taste.

## Pulut durian, pengat and serawa

The sweet durian-and-coconut element has a life of its own. Cooked as a thick,
creamy sweet, it is often called **pengat durian**, **serawa durian** or **kuah
durian** — a soupy durian dessert eaten warm on its own, or mopped up with white
kopitiam bread, or served over rice. When that sauce meets pulut, you have pulut
durian; when it is poured over other things, it keeps its own name. The terms
overlap from region to region, which is part of the charm and part of the
confusion.

## A taste of the season

Above all, pulut durian is seasonal. It is made when durians are at their
cheapest and most plentiful during the harvest, so it carries the feel of a treat
tied to a particular time of year rather than an everyday dessert. It is strongly
associated with home and kampung kitchens, where a good durian and a pot of
glutinous rice are turned into something more than the sum of their parts.
