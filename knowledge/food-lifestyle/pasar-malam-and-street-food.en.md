As dusk falls, a quiet street suddenly transforms into an alley of satay smoke, piles of fruit, and rows of fluorescent lights — this is the pasar malam, one of the most down-to-earth institutions in Malaysia. Together with the pasar tani and the wet market, it forms a network of people's markets where Malaysians shop and eat every day.

Unlike supermarkets that are open all day at fixed prices, people's markets run to the rhythm of the hours, the seasons and haggling. This guide explains the differences between the three types of market, their operating hours, how they are regulated, and what the cleanliness grade displayed at food stalls means.

## What is the difference between a pasar malam, a pasar tani and a wet market?

These three markets are often confused with one another, but each serves a different function.

**A pasar malam** is a mobile market that appears in the evening to night, usually on certain days each week in the same area. It sells everything — ready-to-eat food, fruit, vegetables, clothing, and even household goods. For most people, the pasar malam is a cheap and lively place to have dinner, not just to buy raw ingredients.

**A pasar tani**, on the other hand, is an official programme under FAMA (the Federal Agricultural Marketing Authority). The concept is a "meeting place between producers and consumers" — farmers and small operators sell their farm produce directly to buyers without going through middlemen. It is usually held in the morning to midday.

**A wet market** is a fixed market that operates as early as dawn, selling fresh meat, fish, chicken and vegetables. Its name comes from the floor that is always wet because of ice and water used to wash seafood. The wet market is the lifeblood of home kitchens and of restaurant operators who want fresh ingredients every day.

| Feature | Pasar malam | Pasar tani | Wet market |
|---|---|---|---|
| Operating hours | Evening to night | Morning to midday | As early as dawn to midday |
| Frequency | Certain days each week | Scheduled by location | Every day |
| Main operator | Licensed hawkers | Agricultural producers (FAMA programme) | Permanent traders |
| Sales focus | Ready-to-eat food, fruit, general goods | Farm produce direct from producers | Fresh meat, fish, vegetables |
| Atmosphere | Lively, a place for dinner | Straight from the farm, retail prices | Functional, kitchen shopping |

## How did the pasar tani grow from 12 stalls?

The pasar tani programme is not just an old tradition — it is a policy project whose dates can be traced. According to FAMA, the first pasar tani opened on 31 January 1985 in Johor Bahru. In that starting year, there were only **12 farmers' markets** involving **317 participants** with a sales value of about **RM1.4 million a year**.

Four decades later, the scale is vastly different. According to media reports in early 2024, there were **223 farmers' markets** nationwide involving **8,542 entrepreneurs** and recording a sales value of **RM244.6 million**. FAMA also aimed to open 14 new farmers' markets and upgrade 40 existing ones that year. (These figures are sourced from media reports and still need to be confirmed against official FAMA publications.)

This growth shows that the pasar tani has developed from a small experiment into a national marketing channel connecting smallholder farmers directly with urban consumers.

## Who regulates the stalls and the pasar malam?

Night markets do not appear at random — each one is regulated by the local authority (PBT). In Kuala Lumpur, for example, Kuala Lumpur City Hall (DBKL) lists **97 night-market locations** spread across parliamentary areas such as Batu, Kepong, Segambut, Titiwangsa, Cheras and Lembah Pantai.

The local authority carries out several key functions:

- Issuing **hawker licences** to traders who wish to do business at a pasar malam.
- Setting the **location and operating days** so that markets do not overlap or obstruct traffic.
- Enforcing **cleanliness standards** and conducting inspections.

Because each pasar malam has its own day and time, there is no single uniform schedule for the whole country — visitors need to refer to their respective local authority portal to find out which market operates on a given night.

## How does the A, B and C cleanliness grading system work?

If you notice a "Grade A" sticker or signboard at a stall or restaurant, it is not decoration — it is an official result of a cleanliness inspection by the authorities. This grading system assesses food premises and places them in one of three categories: **Grade A (Very Clean)**, **Grade B (Clean)** and **Grade C (Less Clean)**, as explained by Majlis Bandaraya Johor Bahru.

The score range for each grade is set by the local authority and is **not uniform** across the country. For example, according to the scale in force from 1 May 2024 at Kuantan City Council, the ranges are as follows:

| Grade | Meaning | Score range (example: MBK Kuantan) | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Very Clean | 90-100 | Recognised as the cleanest premises |
| B | Clean | 75-89 | Accepted; can be improved |
| C | Less Clean | 65-74 | Immediate improvement needed |
| (Below 65) | Fail | < 65 | Premises may be ordered to close |

According to Majlis Bandaraya Johor Bahru's list of criteria, the inspection covers the cleanliness level of the premises, workers' typhoid vaccination status, cold-storage temperature of food, grease traps (oil traps) and pest control. This grading scale is intended to raise cleanliness standards, especially in cities and tourist destinations. Note that score ranges and implementation differ between local authorities, so refer to your local council for exact details.

## Is it safe to eat at a pasar malam and pasar tani?

Malaysia's food safety laws apply to roadside stalls just as they do to restaurants. The main framework is the **Food Hygiene Regulations 2009**, enforced under the **Food Act 1983**. Cleanliness offences can be issued notices under **Section 32B** of the act, and enforcement is carried out by the Ministry of Health Malaysia (KKM).

This enforcement is not just on paper. According to an RTM report, during the period from January to June 2024, the Ministry of Health reportedly:

- Inspected **63,784 premises**
- Issued **16,415 notices** for offences under Section 32B
- Ordered **910 premises** to close

Besides grading and enforcement, there are also voluntary recognition programmes such as **BeSS** (Bersih, Selamat, Sihat — Clean, Safe, Healthy), with **11,783 food premises** nationwide reportedly having received the recognition according to the same report.

For visitors, a few simple practices help:

- Choose stalls that display a cleanliness grade of A or B.
- Eat food that is cooked hot and served fresh, not food that has been exposed for a long time.
- Watch whether the operator separates the task of handling money from serving food.
- For fruit and vegetables, the pasar tani offers produce that is usually picked fresher because it comes directly from the producers.

## What's Next

The pasar malam, pasar tani and wet market are more than just places to shop — they are social spaces where communities meet, and economic channels for thousands of small entrepreneurs. Before visiting, check your local authority portal (for example DBKL) for the schedule of nearby night markets, and refer to the FAMA website for the latest list of farmers' markets by state.

If you plan to do business, the first step is to apply for a hawker licence from the local council and understand the requirements of the Food Hygiene Regulations 2009, including the food handling course and premises registration.
