As dusk falls, a quiet street suddenly transforms into an alley of satay smoke, mounds of fruit, and rows of fluorescent lights — this is the pasar malam, one of Malaysia's most down-to-earth institutions. Together with the pasar tani and the wet market, it forms a network of people's markets where Malaysians shop and eat every day.

Unlike supermarkets that are open all day at fixed prices, people's markets run to the rhythm of the hours, the seasons and haggling. This guide explains the differences between these three types of market, their operating hours, how they are regulated, and what the hygiene grades displayed at food stalls mean.

## What is the difference between a pasar malam, a pasar tani and a wet market?

These three markets are often confused, but each serves a different function.

**A pasar malam** is a mobile market that appears in the evening to night, usually on specific days each week in the same area. It sells everything — ready-to-eat food, fruit, vegetables, clothing, right up to household goods. For most people, the pasar malam is a place for a cheap and lively dinner, not merely to buy raw ingredients.

**A pasar tani**, meanwhile, is an official programme under FAMA (the Federal Agricultural Marketing Authority). Its concept is a "meeting place of producers and consumers" — farmers and small operators sell their farm produce directly to buyers without going through middlemen. It is usually held from morning to midday.

**A wet market** is a permanent market that operates from as early as dawn, selling fresh meat, fish, chicken and vegetables. Its name comes from the floor that is always wet from ice and water washing the seafood. The wet market is the lifeblood of household kitchens and restaurant operators who want fresh ingredients every day.

| Feature | Pasar malam | Pasar tani | Wet market |
|---|---|---|---|
| Operating hours | Evening to night | Morning to midday | From dawn to midday |
| Frequency | Specific days each week | Scheduled by location | Every day |
| Main operator | Licensed hawkers | Farm producers (FAMA programme) | Permanent traders |
| Sales focus | Ready-to-eat food, fruit, general goods | Farm produce direct from producers | Fresh meat, fish, vegetables |
| Atmosphere | Lively, a dinner spot | Straight from the farm, retail prices | Functional, kitchen shopping |

## How did the pasar tani grow from 12 stalls?

The pasar tani programme is not merely an old tradition — it is a policy project whose date can be traced. According to FAMA, the first pasar tani opened on 31 January 1985 in Johor Bahru. In that founding year, there were only **12 farmers' markets** involving **317 participants** with a sales value of roughly **RM1.4 million a year**.

Four decades later, the scale is far different. According to media reports in early 2024, there were **223 farmers' markets** nationwide involving **8,542 entrepreneurs** and recording a sales value of **RM244.6 million**. FAMA also targeted opening 14 new farmers' markets and upgrading 40 existing ones that year. (These figures are sourced from media reports and still need to be confirmed against official FAMA publications.)

This growth shows that the pasar tani has evolved from a small experiment into a national marketing channel connecting small farmers directly with urban consumers.

## Who regulates the stalls and the night markets?

Night markets do not appear at random — each one is regulated by a local authority (PBT). In Kuala Lumpur, for example, Kuala Lumpur City Hall (DBKL) lists **97 night market locations** spread across parliamentary constituencies such as Batu, Kepong, Segambut, Titiwangsa, Cheras and Lembah Pantai.

The local authority carries out several key functions:

- Issuing **hawker licences** to traders who wish to do business at a night market.
- Setting the **location and operating days** so that markets do not overlap or obstruct traffic.
- Enforcing **hygiene standards** and conducting inspections.

Because each night market has its own days and hours, there is no single uniform schedule for the whole country — visitors need to refer to their respective local authority's portal to find out which market operates on a given night.

## How does the A, B and C hygiene grading system work?

If you notice a "Grade A" sticker or signboard at a stall or restaurant, it is not decoration — it is an official result of a hygiene inspection by the authorities. This grading system assesses food premises and places them in three categories: **Grade A (Very Clean)**, **Grade B (Clean)** and **Grade C (Less Clean)**, as explained by Johor Bahru City Council.

The score range for each grade is set by the local authority and is **not uniform** nationwide. For example, under the scale effective from 1 May 2024 at Kuantan City Council, the ranges are as follows:

| Grade | Meaning | Score range (example: MBK Kuantan) | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Very Clean | 90-100 | Recognised as the cleanest premises |
| B | Clean | 75-89 | Accepted; can be improved |
| C | Less Clean | 65-74 | Requires immediate improvement |
| (Below 65) | Fail | < 65 | Premises may be ordered to close |

According to Johor Bahru City Council's list of criteria, an inspection covers the cleanliness of the premises, workers' typhoid vaccination status, the temperature of food cold storage, grease traps (oil traps) and pest control. This grading scale aims to raise hygiene standards, especially in cities and tourist destinations. Note that the score ranges and implementation differ between local authorities, so refer to your local council for exact details.

## Is it safe to eat at a pasar malam or pasar tani?

Malaysia's food safety laws apply to roadside stalls just as they do to restaurants. The main framework is the **Food Hygiene Regulations 2009**, which are enforced under the **Food Act 1983**. Hygiene offences may be issued a notice under **Section 32B** of the Act, and enforcement is carried out by the Ministry of Health Malaysia (KKM).

This enforcement is not merely on paper. According to an official KKM statement (reported by BERNAMA), from January to June 2024, KKM:

- Inspected **63,784 premises**
- Issued **16,415 notices** of offence under Section 32B
- Ordered **910 premises** to close

Beyond grading and enforcement, there is also a voluntary recognition programme such as **BeSS** (Bersih, Selamat, Sihat — Clean, Safe, Healthy), with **11,783 food premises** nationwide having received the recognition according to KKM.

For visitors, a few simple practices help:

- Choose stalls that display a hygiene grade of A or B.
- Eat food that is cooked hot and served fresh, not left exposed for a long time.
- Watch whether the operator separates the tasks of handling money and serving food.
- For fruit and vegetables, the pasar tani offers produce that is usually picked fresher because it comes directly from the producer.

## What's Next

The pasar malam, pasar tani and wet market are more than just places to shop — they are social spaces where communities meet, and economic channels for thousands of small entrepreneurs. Before visiting, check your local authority's portal (for example DBKL) for the schedule of nearby night markets, and refer to the FAMA website for the latest list of farmers' markets by state.

If you plan to trade, the first step is to apply for a hawker licence from the local council and to understand the requirements of the Food Hygiene Regulations 2009, including the food handling course and premises registration.
