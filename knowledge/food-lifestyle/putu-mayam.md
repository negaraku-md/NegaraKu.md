Few Malaysian snacks are as quietly beloved as putu mayam: a little nest of
thread-thin rice noodles, pale and soft, scattered with freshly grated coconut and
a spoonful of sugar. It is barely sweet, faintly fragrant with pandan, and light
enough to eat two or three nests without noticing — the kind of unhurried treat
people reach for at breakfast or over afternoon tea.

## What putu mayam is

At its simplest, putu mayam is steamed rice-flour vermicelli. Rice flour is mixed
with water — and sometimes a little coconut milk — into a soft dough, which is then
pressed through a perforated mould so it emerges as fine strands. The maker coils
those strands into small flat nests and steams them briefly, often over or with
pandan leaf, which lends the noodles their gentle green fragrance. The result is
pliant and faintly chewy, with almost no flavour of its own, which is exactly the
point: it is a canvas for what goes on top.

That topping is nearly always the same two things. First, a generous handful of
**freshly grated coconut**, soft and milky. Then **sugar** — most commonly brown
sugar or palm sugar (*gula melaka*), though some stalls offer a coloured sugar
instead. Older accounts describe palm sugar served in small blocks before granulated
sugar became common. Unlike many hawker dishes, putu mayam is usually eaten cool
rather than piping hot, and traditionally by hand, sometimes off a square of banana
leaf that saves you a spoon and fork.

## The idiyappam connection

Putu mayam did not begin in Malaysia. It is the Malaysian and Singaporean name for
**idiyappam**, the dish known in English as **string hoppers** and in Tamil as
*nool puttu* ("string" *puttu*). Idiyappam has deep roots in South India —
especially Tamil Nadu and Kerala — and in Sri Lanka, where the same pressed-and-
steamed rice noodles are a staple. The name itself points to the method: in
Malayalam, *idi* means "press."

The biggest difference between home and here is what the noodles are eaten with. In
their South Asian setting, string hoppers are typically a savoury base, paired with
curry, dhal or sweetened coconut milk. In Malaysia and Singapore, the same nests
took a sweet turn, settling into the coconut-and-sugar snack sold on the street
today. It is one small thread in the larger weave of
[Indian Malaysian cuisine](/food-lifestyle/indian-malaysian-cuisine), and a neat
example of how a migrant dish adapts to a new home — a pattern explored across our
guide to Malaysia's
[regional and ethnic cuisines](/food-lifestyle/regional-and-ethnic-cuisines).

## A travelling-vendor food

Part of putu mayam's charm is how it has long reached its eaters: on the move. It is
a classic **roving-vendor** snack, historically carried door to door from pushcarts
and, in more recent decades, bicycles and motorbikes, as well as being sold at
markets and night-market stalls. Regulars learn to recognise a passing seller by a
distinctive horn or call, and by the aluminium boxes that keep the nests warm and
soft. Freshly pressed putu mayam has become less common as fewer makers take on the
labour-intensive work of steaming it from scratch, but it remains a widely
recognised street food enjoyed by Malaysians of every background.

Because it is nothing more than rice flour, coconut and sugar, putu mayam is a
naturally meat-free, vegetarian snack, which helps explain its broad appeal across
communities. For many it is a small daily pleasure rather than a special-occasion
dish — light, cheap and easy to share.

## Where to try it

Look for putu mayam at morning markets, *pasar malam* (night markets) and roadside
stalls, and from the travelling vendors who still work residential streets. Ask for
extra coconut if you like it rich, and try it with palm sugar at least once — its
caramel depth is what lifts an otherwise plain nest of noodles into something worth
stopping for.
