In a state crowded with good noodles, one plate is claimed above the rest: Tuaran
mee, named for the quiet west-coast town that gave it to Sabah. The pull is not a
sauce or a secret broth but the strand itself — a yellow egg noodle with a bounce
you feel in the first bite, finished over fierce heat until its underside turns
crisp. Some call it what char kway teow is to Penang: the dish an entire place is
known for.

## What Tuaran mee is

The noodle comes first. It is made from a batter of egg yolk and flour, and
traditionally shaped by hand, which gives it a firm, springy bite quite unlike a
limp wheat noodle. That texture is the whole point of Tuaran mee — before any
topping is added, the strand already carries the dish.

The town lends both the name and the recipe. Tuaran sits about 34 kilometres north
of Kota Kinabalu, and the dish is attributed to the area's Hakka Chinese community;
its Chinese name, 鬥亞蘭面, means simply "Tuaran noodles." It belongs to the broader
story of [Chinese Malaysian cuisine](/food-lifestyle/chinese-malaysian-cuisine),
the Borneo chapter of a tradition usually told through the peninsula.

## The fried plate

The form most people mean by Tuaran mee is the fried one. The noodles go into a
very hot wok and are stir-fried for several minutes — long enough that the layer
against the metal catches and crisps while the rest stays springy, a contrast of
crunch and bounce in the same mouthful. Local greens are tossed in toward the end.

Then the toppings, both unmistakably Chinese: slices of sweetened roast pork
(char siew, 叉燒) laid over the top, and fried egg rolls (chun kien, 春捲), a Hakka
touch, cut into coins. The egg rolls are often filled with minced pork, which — with
the char siew — means the classic plate is usually not halal; cooks also make
versions with chicken in place of pork, so it is worth asking at the stall. This
kind of community and regional variation runs right through Malaysia's
[regional and ethnic cuisines](/food-lifestyle/regional-and-ethnic-cuisines).

## Soup, dry and other versions

Fried is famous, but it is not the only way the noodle is served. The same springy
strand turns up in a soup version and in a dry-tossed one, where the noodles are
warmed through and dressed in a dark sauce rather than fried. Around Kota Kinabalu
you will also find local twists, including a rendition made with Lihing, Sabah's
rice wine. What stays constant is the noodle; the method and the sauce are what
change.

## Where to try it

Tuaran mee is easiest to find around Tuaran town itself and across the Kota Kinabalu
area, where dedicated noodle houses serve it from morning through early afternoon. A
good plate is judged first on the noodle — it should hold its spring and show a
little crisp at the base — and then on the balance of sweet char siew, savoury egg
roll and fresh greens over the top.
