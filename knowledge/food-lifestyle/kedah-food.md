Drive north up the Peninsula and the land flattens into an almost unbroken sheet
of green and gold: wet paddy to the horizon, broken only by limestone outcrops
and the odd kampung. This is Kedah, the state Malaysians call *jelapang padi* —
the rice barn — and its food grows straight out of that landscape. Rice is not
just the staple here; it is the economy, the scenery and the starting point for
a kitchen that is unmistakably northern, and quietly shaped by the Thai border
just up the road.

## The rice bowl of Malaysia

Kedah's nickname is earned. The state sits on a broad alluvial plain and, with
neighbouring Perlis, forms the country's single most important rice-growing
region — the Muda area, managed by the Muda Agricultural Development Authority
(MADA). By most accounts this granary supplies a very large share of Malaysia's
home-grown rice, though the figure cited shifts from source to source: the *New
Straits Times*, reporting on Kedah's paddy fields, describes the state as
Malaysia's rice bowl and notes output estimates ranging from around 40% to close
to half of national production. The precise percentage depends on the year and
on how the growing area is defined, so it is best treated as "a large share"
rather than a fixed number (see the open questions below).

What matters for the table is abundance of rice itself. Where rice is cheap,
fresh and everywhere, a cuisine builds outward from it — plain steamed rice to
carry gulai and sambal, glutinous *pulut* for sweets, leftover rice tossed with
herbs, and fermented and grilled sides to make a little protein go far. Nearly
every Kedah dish below is, at root, a way to eat more rice well. Rice is also the
foundation of the wider [Malay cuisine](/food-lifestyle/malay-cuisine) that
Kedah belongs to.

## A northern, Thai-tinged table

Kedah's cooking reads as northern Malay first. The MICHELIN Guide, surveying
Malaysia's regional flavours, calls Kedah "the oldest civilization in Malaysia"
and says its "native Malay cuisine also has strong Sumatran and Southern Thai
influences" — a legacy of centuries of traders and settlers along the old
maritime route. Exactly how those two strands balance out is not settled, and
is better heard as a general observation than a precise recipe-by-recipe claim.

The Thai thread is the one a visitor notices first. Kedah shares a long border
with southern Thailand, and Thai-style dishes — above all *tom yam*, the hot-sour
soup — are woven into ordinary eating rather than treated as foreign. The
exchange runs both ways across a frontier where Malay and Thai communities have
long lived side by side. One caution worth flagging: *budu*, the pungent
fermented fish sauce sometimes mentioned in the same breath as northern cooking,
is most strongly associated with the east-coast states of Kelantan and
Terengganu; in Kedah the defining fermented note comes instead from *pekasam*
(below). Treat any "budu in Kedah" claim with care.

## Laksa Kedah: the northern bowl

If one dish anchors Kedah's reputation, it is its laksa. **Laksa Kedah**, widely
called **laksa utara** (northern laksa), belongs to the sour, fish-based branch
of the [laksa](/food-lifestyle/laksa) family — the same branch as Penang's
famous asam laksa, not the coconut-curry kind. It is built on a broth of boiled,
flaked fish, typically *ikan kembung* (mackerel), pounded or blended into a
thick gravy and soured with tamarind, then served over rice noodles.

How much it differs from the Penang version is genuinely contested. Some accounts
treat the two as near-identical and point only to garnish — a boiled egg added in
Kedah, say. Others, including *Free Malaysia Today*'s round-up of Malaysian laksa
styles and *The Star*'s guide to laksa types, describe the Kedah/northern bowl as
noticeably thicker and usually milder and less sour than Penang's sharp, pungent
asam laksa. The honest answer is that recipes vary from stall to stall and town
to town across the north, so the "right" laksa Kedah is a moving target; the
differences are flagged for verification below. Either way it is finished the
northern way — shredded cucumber, onion, torch-ginger bud, mint or laksa leaf,
and a dark smear of sweet prawn paste.

## Nasi ulam and the herb-forward kitchen

Kedah's love of fresh greenery shows clearly in **nasi ulam**, a rice dish that
is closer to a herb salad than a hot plate. Cooled cooked rice is tossed with a
large assortment of finely shredded herbs and leaves — Wikipedia's entry notes
the northern Malay Peninsula as one of its homes and lists ingredients such as
*daun kaduk* (wild pepper leaf) and *pucuk gajus* (cashew-leaf shoots), often
with toasted grated coconut (*kerisik*), dried shrimp or flaked fish and shallots
worked through. Older accounts claim dozens of herbs in a single bowl, though in
practice only a handful are constant. The herbs are stirred in off the heat so
they stay fresh and fragrant rather than wilting.

That instinct — eat your greens raw, pounded or lightly dressed — runs through
the northern table more broadly, where a plate of *ulam* (raw herbs and
vegetables) with sambal belacan is a standard companion to rice. Kedah also keeps
regional specialities such as *nasi lemuni*, rice cooked with the juice of the
lemuni plant, whose leaves lend a faintly bitter note softened by coconut milk,
and which the MICHELIN Guide notes is traditionally served to women in post-natal
care.

## Gulai, pekasam and the savoury staples

The everyday core of a Kedah meal is rice with **gulai** — the broad family of
Malay spiced, often coconut-enriched gravies and curries built on a pounded
*rempah* (spice paste) — ladled over it, alongside grilled or fried fish and a
dish of sambal. This is the shared grammar of
[Malay cuisine](/food-lifestyle/malay-cuisine) statewide, and Kedah cooks it with
a northern accent.

Its most distinctive savoury signature, though, is **pekasam**: fish fermented
with salt, toasted ground rice and palm sugar until sour and deeply savoury,
then fried or cooked with sambal. Wikipedia's entry notes that pekasam is
consumed mostly in Kedah, Perlis, Perak and sometimes Kelantan, and that in
Malaysia it is the fermented fish product made from *freshwater* fish — species
such as *sepat*, *lampam* and *puyu* drawn from the very paddy and irrigation
waters that define the state. In a rice-growing region, a preserved freshwater
fish is exactly the kind of larder staple the land makes easy.

## Pulut and the glutinous-rice sweets

Where there is rice, there are rice sweets — and Kedah leans hard on **pulut**,
glutinous rice. It turns up as *pulut* steamed with coconut milk and eaten with
sweet or savoury toppings; as *pulut panggang*, parcels of coconut-and-herb or
dried-shrimp filling wrapped in banana leaf and grilled; and in the sticky,
slow-cooked sweets of the broader Malay repertoire such as *dodol* and
*wajik* that appear at festivals and markets. These are not unique to Kedah, but
a granary state naturally keeps a deep bench of glutinous-rice *kuih*, and they
are a fixture of Ramadan bazaars and *kenduri* (feasts) across the north.

## Char koay teow and the street-food layer

Kedah's table is not only Malay. Generations of Chinese and Indian-Muslim
(*mamak*) communities have added their own layer, and the clearest example is
**char koay teow**, the smoky wok-fried flat rice noodle most famously linked to
Penang next door. Kedah towns such as Alor Setar and Sungai Petani cook their own
versions, and more than one northern town claims its char koay teow is a cut
above — part of a friendly, unresolved regional rivalry rather than a settled
fact. The dish itself — flat rice noodles seared hard over flame with prawns,
cockles, egg, bean sprouts and chives — is covered in full under
[char kway teow](/food-lifestyle/char-kway-teow). Alongside it sit mamak staples,
Thai-Malay stir-fries and the ubiquitous bowl of tom yam, all of which make
Kedah's street food feel of a piece with the wider north.

## The common thread

Line up Kedah's dishes and the logic is the same one you can see from any trunk
road through the state: it all comes back to rice. The paddy that earns Kedah its
*jelapang padi* name is also the pantry — grain for the staple, glutinous *pulut*
for sweets, freshwater fish from the irrigation channels turned into pekasam,
and herbs from the kampung edge folded into nasi ulam. Layered on top is a
northern, border-country openness that lets tom yam and a milder, thicker laksa
sit comfortably beside gulai and sambal. To eat in Kedah is to taste a cuisine
that knows exactly where its rice comes from — and this is only one corner of
Malaysia's wider map of
[regional and ethnic cuisines](/food-lifestyle/regional-and-ethnic-cuisines).
