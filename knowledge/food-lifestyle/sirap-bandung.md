Of all the drinks on a Malaysian coffee-shop board, sirap bandung is the one you
notice first. It arrives a vivid, opaque pink, usually over ice and often capped
with a little froth, and it tastes of roses and sweet milk. It is a drink of
celebration as much as refreshment — poured at Malay weddings, broken out at the
breaking of the Ramadan fast, and sold the rest of the year at mamak stalls and
night-market carts. This article is the deep dive on that pink glass; for where it
sits among the country's wider drink culture, see the
[Malaysian drinks](/food-lifestyle/malaysian-drinks) hub.

## What sirap bandung is

At its simplest, sirap bandung is rose-flavoured syrup mixed with milk and water
and served cold. The syrup is a rose cordial — a sweet, deep-red rose base — and
the milk is usually condensed or evaporated milk, the same tinned milk that
sweetens Malaysian [kopi and teh](/food-lifestyle/kopi-teh-tarik-and-coffeeshop-drinks).
Stirred together and poured over ice, they make a drink that is sweet, floral and
creamy at once. It is also known as *air bandung* ("bandung water").

The drink belongs to a small family of syrup coolers. Take the milk away and you
have **air sirap** — plain coloured syrup, often rose, let down with water, the
no-frills cousin that turns up at *kenduri* and roadside stalls. Add a squeeze of
lime and you edge toward *sirap limau*. Sirap bandung is the richest of the set,
and the only pink one, because the milk is doing something specific to the colour.

## The pink colour and the ingredients

The pink is not food colour added for show — it is what happens when you pour milk
into a strong red rose syrup. The rose cordial on its own is a deep, translucent
red; fold in opaque white condensed or evaporated milk and the red lightens and
clouds into the drink's signature rose-pink. Recipe writers describe starting with
a "deep red syrup" precisely because it "gives a lovely pink once milk is added."

The balance of ingredients is a matter of taste and some disagreement. The core is
rose syrup, milk and water in roughly cordial proportions — on the order of one
part syrup to three or four parts liquid — adjusted sweeter or milkier to
preference. Cooks differ on the milk: some reach for condensed milk for richness,
while others prefer evaporated milk and warn that condensed milk can make the drink
"way too sweet." Many stall versions are pulled or shaken until frothy, so a good
one can arrive with a head of foam a little like [teh tarik](/food-lifestyle/kopi-teh-tarik-and-coffeeshop-drinks).
What is consistent is the flavour signature: rose first, then sweet milk.

## The contested name

Here is where care is needed, because the name invites a tidy story that may not be
true. Many people assume "bandung" points to the Indonesian city of Bandung in West
Java. It is an easy guess — but the sources do not support it. Malay-language
dictionary references and Malaysian and Singaporean recipe writers instead gloss
*bandung* as meaning "paired," "mixed" or "coming together," describing anything
combined from two things. The same word turns up in *rumah berbandung* (a
semi-detached house) and *mee bandung* (a mixed noodle dish). On this reading, the
name describes the drink itself — rose syrup *married* to milk — and, as more than
one source states plainly, it "has no connection to the city of Bandung in
Indonesia." Ask for *air bandung* in the city of Bandung, writers note, and you are
likely to get a puzzled look.

That is the better-supported explanation, but it should still be held loosely.
Accounts of the drink's deeper history disagree: English Wikipedia traces it to the
colonial port of Singapore in the early twentieth century, while other writers place
it more broadly in the Malay Peninsula or across maritime Southeast Asia, and some
simply concede that the exact origin may never be known. Whether the milk was part
of the drink from the beginning or added later, perhaps under British-colonial or
Indian influence, is likewise speculation rather than documented fact. The honest
summary is that sirap bandung is a Malay-world drink of uncertain birthplace with a
contested name — and the "named after the city" story is the least likely of the
explanations, not the default.

## Weddings, iftar and mamak stalls

Whatever its origins, sirap bandung's place in Malaysian life is not in doubt. It is
closely tied to Malay celebration. At wedding receptions and *kenduri* it is a
default drink, poured by the jugful for guests alongside rich festive dishes such as
*nasi beriani* and [rendang](/food-lifestyle/rendang). During Ramadan it is one of
the classic drinks for *iftar*, the breaking of the fast, its sweetness and cold a
welcome reward at dusk. As the bartender who built a competition drink around it put
it, "sirap bandung is enjoyed by Malaysians during festive seasons or when eating
out."

It is an everyday drink too. You find it at the 24-hour
[mamak stalls](/food-lifestyle/kopitiam-mamak-culture) and hawker centres where so
much of Malaysian drinking life happens, and at
[pasar malam](/food-lifestyle/pasar-malam-and-street-food) night-market carts. It
rides on the same modular ordering code as the rest of the coffee-shop board: say
*sirap bandung peng* or *sirap bandung ais* and you get it over ice. Because it is a
non-alcoholic drink of syrup, milk and water, it sits comfortably at Muslim
gatherings and on halal menus; the usual sensible caveat applies that strict
observers may wish to check the particular brands of syrup and milk a kitchen uses.

## Variants: bandung soda, bandung cincau and more

Sirap bandung is a base as much as a finished drink, and vendors stack things onto
it. The common variants include:

- **Bandung soda** — sirap bandung lengthened with a carbonated soft drink for fizz.
  The soda gives the drink, in one description, "some sizzle and pop." A similar
  rose-milk-and-soda mix in Indonesia goes by the name *soda gembira* ("happy
  soda").
- **Bandung cincau** — sirap bandung served with
  [cincau](/food-lifestyle/malaysian-drinks) (grass jelly), soft dark strips of
  herbal jelly dropped into the pink drink so it becomes half-drink, half-dessert.
- **Bandung cincau laici** — the grass-jelly version with lychee, and **bandung
  selasih**, which adds basil or chia seeds for a little texture.

The drink has also been reinvented upmarket. In 2025 a clarified, modernised take
called the **bandung pandan spritz** — built on a clarified sirap bandung with
coconut-lychee cordial, pandan and citrus — won the Signature Mocktail Championship
staged for Visit Malaysia 2026, organised by the Malaysian Association of Hotels and
the Mixology Alliance of the MFBEA with support from [Tourism Malaysia](/tourism/tourism-malaysia-and-tourism-agencies), and was
promoted in the press as a national mocktail. (Headlines call it "official" or
"national," while the reports' own detail describes it as the championship winner, so
the championship framing is the safer one.) The creator chose to serve it with *kuih
lapis*, whose many layers he offered as a nod to Malaysia's layered society — a neat
reminder of how much cultural weight a pink glass of rose milk can carry.
