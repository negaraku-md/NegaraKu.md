Ask for a spring roll in Malaysia and you may get one of two very different things.
One is deep-fried, golden and crackling. The other — *popiah* — is soft, pale and
cool, a thin skin wrapped fresh around a warm, savoury-sweet stew and eaten the
moment it is rolled. It is the fresh one that most people mean by the name, and it
is one of the country's most sociable foods: often not bought at all, but built by
hand around a crowded table.

## What popiah is

Popiah is a fresh, *unfried* spring roll. Its defining feature is the skin — a
soft, thin, almost translucent crêpe made from a very wet wheat-flour dough that
cooks swipe across a hot steel plate and peel away in paper-like rounds. Unlike
the crisp wrapper of a fried spring roll, the popiah skin stays pliable, because
the roll is assembled and served straight away rather than cooked a second time.

The name itself points to that thinness. In Hokkien and Teochew, the dish is
written 薄餅 and is usually glossed as "thin cake", "thin wafer" or "thin
flatbread" — in Penang it is often spelled and pronounced *poh piah*. The exact
sense of the word, like much else about the dish, is reported slightly
differently from source to source, so the gloss is best treated as approximate.

Popiah belongs to the wider world of Malaysian hawker and
[pasar malam street food](/food-lifestyle/pasar-malam-and-street-food), though it
is just as much a home dish — and that double life, stall and kitchen, shapes
everything that follows.

## A contested Hokkien-Teochew heritage

Popiah came to Malaysia with southern Chinese migrants, and the fresh roll is
eaten by Hokkien and Teochew communities across Southeast Asia and Taiwan. Beyond
that broad lineage, the details are contested. Some accounts place its origin
specifically in Fujian (the Minnan region); others locate it in the neighbouring
Teochew-speaking Chaoshan area, and it is sometimes dated to around the 17th
century — a date and geography that should be read as tradition rather than
settled fact. It is widely described as having grown out of a spring vegetable
roll eaten when fresh produce was abundant, which is one explanation for the
English label "spring roll".

Running alongside the migrant Hokkien version is a well-known **Nyonya popiah**,
part of the [Nyonya (Peranakan) culinary
tradition](/food-lifestyle/nyonya-peranakan-cuisine) of the Straits-born Chinese.
A 2025 Malay Mail report on a Peranakan cooking demonstration described the Nyonya
roll as seasoned with fermented soybean paste (*taucu*) and garlic and filled with
jicama, cucumber, bean sprouts, omelette, fried tofu and prawns — framing it as
Peranakan heritage passed down at home. Whether this counts as a distinct regional
dish or simply the same Hokkien roll adopted into Nyonya kitchens is not something
the sources settle, and it is flagged below for verification.

## The filling: stewed sengkuang at the heart

Whatever the regional label, the heart of a popiah is the same: finely grated
**sengkuang** — the jicama, or yam bean — stewed until soft and sweet. The grated
root is cooked down, often with grated carrot, diced fried bean curd and sometimes
French beans, and seasoned simply with salt, pepper and a little sugar. The mixture
is deliberately juicy; one Malaysian recipe warns that the filling "might be
slightly watery", which is part of its character rather than a fault.

Onto that base go the proteins and aromatics that distinguish one stall or
household from another. Prawns or small dried shrimp are common, garlic is standard
in many versions, and richer rolls may include crab meat, Chinese sausage or
shiitake mushrooms. (In some non-halal hawker settings, fried pork lard is added
for extra savour — a reminder that popiah recipes vary widely by community and are
not uniformly halal.) The through-line is the soft, sweet sengkuang; everything
else is negotiable.

## Sauces, herbs and the finishing touches

A popiah is as much assembled as cooked, and the finishing layers are where it
comes alive. Before the filling goes in, the skin is painted with two things: a
dark, thick **sweet sauce** — in the Penang version built from soy sauce (*tau
you*), a fermented bean sauce (*tau ciau*) and sweet prawn paste (*hae ko*) — and
a smear of **chilli paste** for heat. Elsewhere a sweet flour sauce or a hoisin
blend plays the same role.

On top of the sengkuang come the fresh and crunchy elements: a leaf of **lettuce**,
blanched **bean sprouts**, shredded **omelette**, crisp **fried shallots** and a
generous scatter of **ground roasted peanuts**. Fresh cucumber appears in some
versions, coriander in others. The result is a single mouthful that runs soft to
crunchy and sweet to spicy at once — the same balancing act, across different
ingredients, that defines so much Malaysian cooking.

## How a roll is built — and the popiah party

Assembly follows a simple order. Lay the skin flat; streak on the sweet sauce and
chilli; add the lettuce as a bed; spoon on "a spoonful or two" of the warm
sengkuang; then crown it with egg, sprouts, peanuts and shallots. To close it,
fold the bottom edge up over the filling, fold the two sides inward, and roll it
away from you into a plump cylinder, which is then sliced into bite-sized pieces.

That hands-on quality is why popiah is so often a shared event. Across China,
Taiwan, Singapore and Malaysia, families hold a **popiah party**: the skins and
every topping are set out in bowls down the table, and each person wraps a roll to
their own taste — more chilli here, extra peanuts there. Eating popiah this way
turns a dish into an activity, and it is one of the reasons the roll is so tied to
gatherings and reunions rather than to a single recipe.

## Popiah goreng: the fried cousin

Confusingly, there is also a fried member of the family. **Popiah goreng** (also
called *chun pniah* or simply fried popiah) takes a filled roll and deep-fries it
until the wrapper turns crisp and golden — a popular hawker snack in Malaysia,
Singapore and Brunei, sometimes served alongside *lor bak* and dipping sauces, and
often coated in a heavy, spicy chilli sauce in its Malaysian guise. It shares a
name and a basic shape with the fresh roll but is a distinct snack: crunchy rather
than soft, bought ready-made rather than rolled at the table. When Malaysians want
to be precise, "popiah" on its own usually means the fresh version and "popiah
goreng" the fried one.

## Penang and Melaka styles

Like many Malaysian dishes, popiah shifts from town to town. In **Penang**, where
it is widely called *poh piah* and typically sold by Chinese hawkers, the roll
leans sweet and juicy — the sweet sauce of soy, bean sauce and *hae ko* is
pronounced, and the sengkuang is often kept wet so the roll stays moist. In
**Melaka**, long a Peranakan heartland, the fresh roll is strongly associated with
Nyonya cooking and is sometimes finished with a darker, palm-sugar-tinged sweet
sauce; particular stalls there are well enough known that regulars book their rolls
in advance. These are differences of emphasis — sauce, sweetness, wetness — rather
than of kind, and the specifics rest largely on local press and food writing, so
they are flagged below for firmer sourcing. Popiah thus sits comfortably within
Malaysia's broader map of [regional and ethnic
cuisines](/food-lifestyle/regional-and-ethnic-cuisines), a single idea reworked by
each community that adopted it.
