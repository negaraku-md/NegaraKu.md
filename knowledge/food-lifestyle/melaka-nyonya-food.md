Few Malaysian towns put their history on a plate as plainly as Melaka. A spoon of
sour-sweet tamarind here, a Chinese noodle there, a Portuguese curry down the
lane, a dessert crowned with dark palm sugar named after the state itself — the
old port's menu reads like a ledger of everyone who ever traded, settled or ruled
here. To eat in Melaka is to taste six centuries of arrivals.

## A melting pot on the Straits

Melaka (spelled Malacca in English and historical texts) grew from a 15th-century
sultanate into one of the great entrepôts of the Malacca Strait, passing in turn
through Portuguese, Dutch and British hands. Each wave left cooks, ingredients and
techniques behind, and the kitchens of the Chinese, Malay, Indian and
Portuguese-Eurasian communities borrowed freely from one another. That layered
townscape is why Melaka — together with George Town in Penang — was inscribed as a
UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2008, listed as the "Historic Cities of the Straits
of Malacca" for a built and cultural landscape shaped by more than 500 years of
trading and cultural exchange between East and West. The food is the edible half of
that heritage, and it spans the full map of Malaysia's
[regional and ethnic cuisines](/food-lifestyle/regional-and-ethnic-cuisines).

## Baba-Nyonya: the Peranakan heart

Melaka is widely regarded as a cradle of Peranakan — or Baba-Nyonya — culture, the
community descended from early Chinese traders who settled and intermarried with
local Malays. Their kitchens produced one of the region's most distinctive
cuisines: Chinese ingredients cooked with Malay spice pastes (*rempah*), coconut
milk, tamarind, *belacan* (fermented shrimp paste) and aromatic herbs. The term
*nyonya* refers to Peranakan women, whose home cooking defined the style.

Classic Melaka Nyonya dishes include *ayam pongteh* (a braised chicken-and-potato
stew sweetened with fermented soybean paste), *itik tim* (a tangy duck-and-salted-
vegetable soup) and the pungent, herb-packed *otak-otak*. The repertoire leans on
long pounding and slow simmering, and it blurs any neat line between "Chinese" and
"Malay" food. The full story of this cuisine — its history, dishes and the Melaka-
Penang differences — is covered in
[Nyonya and Peranakan cuisine](/food-lifestyle/nyonya-peranakan-cuisine).

## Chicken rice balls

The dish most associated with Melaka by visitors is *nasi ayam ketul*, or chicken
rice balls: the same poached chicken and fragrant, stock-cooked rice found in
[Hainanese chicken rice](/food-lifestyle/hainanese-chicken-rice) across Malaysia,
but with the rice pressed into warm, golf-ball-sized spheres instead of served
loose. The balls are eaten alongside sliced chicken, chilli sauce and a light
broth, and some of the city's long-running shops draw weekend queues that spill
onto the street, with the balls sold for only a few cents each.

The origin is a folk story rather than documented history. The most repeated
account holds that rice was shaped into portable balls so that labourers — or, in
another version, sailors — could carry lunch easily; some diners outside Melaka
even dispute that the balls began here at all. What is clear is the dish's
Hainanese Chinese lineage and its strong, lasting identification with Melaka.

## Satay celup: the communal satay hotpot

Satay celup (*celup* means "to dip") is Melaka's best-loved interactive meal and,
by most accounts, a local invention. Diners sit around a bubbling communal pot of
thick, satay-style gravy and cook their own skewers — raw and semi-cooked seafood,
meat, vegetables, tofu and more — by dunking them in until done, paying by the
number of sticks. Think of it as a satay-flavoured steamboat: Wikipedia describes
it as skewered ingredients dipped into "a hot boiling pot of satay gravy," with
stalls offering upwards of eighty types of ingredient.

The gravy is commonly described as a thinner, peanut-based satay sauce, though
descriptions of its exact make-up vary. Its origins are not firmly documented;
one popular account credits the owners of a Melaka stall from around the
mid-20th century, and others trace the dipping technique to southern Chinese
hotpot adapted to a Malay peanut sauce — treat both as plausible folk history
rather than settled fact.

## Cendol and gula Melaka

For dessert, Melaka lends its name to one of Malaysia's defining sweet
ingredients. [Gula Melaka](/food-lifestyle/gula-melaka) is a dark, caramel-toned
palm sugar made from the sap of coconut-palm flower buds; the name pairs *gula*
("sugar" in Malay) with the state, which is generally held to be where it
originated — an attribution that is widely repeated but only loosely documented.

That sugar is the soul of [cendol](/food-lifestyle/cendol), the shaved-ice
dessert of green rice-flour jelly strands, coconut milk and a generous pour of
melted gula Melaka, often with red beans. Melaka's version is a point of local
pride, and open-air cendol stalls are a fixture of the old town. Cendol's own
birthplace is unsettled — sources variously propose Java and the Peranakan
communities of Melaka and Penang — so the dessert is best described as a shared
Southeast Asian treat that Melaka has made especially its own.

## The Kristang table

Melaka also preserves a cuisine found almost nowhere else: the food of the
Kristang, the Portuguese-Eurasian community descended from 16th-century Portuguese
settlers and locals, many still living in the city's Portuguese Settlement. Their
best-known dish is *kari debal*, or devil's curry — a sharp, fiery stew of meat
(often chicken or pork) built on a spice paste with vinegar and mustard, by
tradition cooked from festive leftovers at Christmas, Easter and weddings. Free
Malaysia Today describes it as a heritage dish of Melaka, and several sources link
its vinegar-and-chilli profile to Portugal's Goan influence. The Kristang word
*debal* is usually glossed as "leftovers," with the "devil" nickname attributed to
the heat — a neat story that is worth treating as folk etymology. This distinctive
table is explored in
[Eurasian and Kristang cuisine](/food-lifestyle/eurasian-kristang-cuisine).

## Jonker Street and pineapple tarts

The liveliest way to graze through all of the above is the Jonker Street (Jonker
Walk) night market, which fills the heart of Melaka's Chinatown on weekend
evenings with stalls selling cendol, satay, grilled seafood, *putu piring*,
Nyonya *kuih* and more — Melaka's contribution to Malaysia's wider culture of the
[pasar malam and street food](/food-lifestyle/pasar-malam-and-street-food).

A favourite thing to carry home is the pineapple tart (*kuih tart*): a buttery,
melt-in-the-mouth pastry topped or filled with slow-cooked pineapple jam.
Commonly credited to Peranakan Nyonya cooks — who are said to have married
European, Portuguese-influenced pastry methods to local fruit — the tart is a
staple of Chinese New Year, helped along by wordplay (pineapple is *ong lai* in
Hokkien, suggesting "prosperity comes"), though its precise origin and first maker
are not documented. Today it is baked and eaten across Malaysia's festivals,
Melaka included.
