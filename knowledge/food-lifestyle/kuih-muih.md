Walk past a kuih stall in the morning and the display looks like a paint box:
emerald-green pandan jelly, blushing pink layers, deep-brown palm-sugar
custards, snowy coconut coatings. Pick one up and it is rarely the fluffy sponge
a Western eye expects — more often it is soft, chewy, faintly sweet and
perfumed with coconut and pandan. This is *kuih-muih*, and it is one of the most
recognisable corners of the Malaysian table.

## What counts as a kuih

"Kuih" (plural *kuih-muih*) is a Malay umbrella term for bite-sized traditional
cakes, snacks and sweets. It is a deliberately broad word: a single kuih might
be called a cake, a dumpling, a pudding, a pastry or a cookie in English. What
unites them is scale and spirit rather than a fixed recipe — small, handmade,
often made through "age-old, often laborious techniques," as the MICHELIN Guide
puts it.

The first thing that surprises newcomers is how the kuih are cooked. Unlike
Western baking, the dominant method across Peninsular Malaysia, Singapore and
Brunei is **steaming**, which gives many kuih their characteristic soft,
slightly bouncy texture. **Boiling** is common for dumpling-style kuih,
**frying** is widespread (especially, by some accounts, in Sabah and Sarawak,
where crunchy kuih are popular), and some are **baked** or cooked on a griddle.
Kuih can be **sweet or savoury** — the sugary ones are the most famous abroad,
but savoury kuih are an everyday part of the repertoire too.

By many popular accounts there are more than a hundred distinct types, varying
from state to state, though a firm count is hard to pin down and the figure is
best treated as indicative. The word itself is usually traced to the Southern
Min (Hokkien and Teochew) *koé* (粿), a sign of how far back the Chinese and
Malay culinary exchange runs, though the precise etymology is a matter for
linguists rather than food writers.

## The kuih pantry

Most kuih are built from starches other than wheat. **Rice flour**,
**glutinous-rice flour** (and whole glutinous rice) and **tapioca** form the
backbone; mung-bean (green-pea) flour and sago appear in specific recipes to
lend a firmer, more pudding-like set. Wheat flour is comparatively rare.

Three ingredients give kuih their signature character:

- **Coconut** — grated for coatings, or pressed into *santan* (coconut milk) and
  coconut cream for richness. It appears in a huge share of kuih, sweet and
  savoury alike.
- **Pandan** (screwpine) leaf — the fragrant, grassy aroma and natural green
  colour behind countless kuih, from jellies to custards.
- **Gula melaka** — Malaysian palm sugar, tapped from coconut or other palm
  flower sap and boiled down into firm, dark, smoky-caramel discs. It is the
  defining sweetener of the local kitchen, used fresh or aged, and is the same
  syrup that flavours [cendol](/food-lifestyle/cendol) and
  [ais kacang](/food-lifestyle/ais-kacang).

Sweet potato, mung bean and banana round out the common fillings and
flavourings, and banana leaf is a frequent wrapper. Peranakan kitchens also
reach beyond gula melaka to cane sugar, brown sugar and honey.

## Steamed and boiled classics

The steamed and boiled kuih are the ones most people picture first.

- **Onde-onde** (also sold as **buah melaka** in parts of Malaysia) are small
  glutinous-rice-flour balls, tinted and scented with pandan, hiding a pool of
  liquid gula melaka that bursts in the mouth, then rolled in grated coconut.
  Whether "onde-onde" and "buah melaka" name exactly the same thing or close
  cousins is a point on which usage varies.
- **Kuih lapis** ("layer cake") is a steamed cake of rice flour, coconut milk
  and sugar built up in many thin, usually brightly coloured layers that are
  peeled apart and eaten one at a time.
- **Seri muka** ("pretty face") pairs a base of steamed glutinous rice with a
  smooth green pandan custard set on top — two textures, two layers, one of the
  best-loved kuih of all.
- **Kuih talam** is likewise two-layered: a savoury-sweet white coconut top over
  a green pandan base, served in little moulds.
- **Dodol** is a dense, sticky, toffee-like confection of glutinous-rice flour,
  coconut milk and gula melaka, stirred for hours over a fire until it turns
  dark and chewy — a festive specialty in several states. A close relative,
  *wajik* (or *kuih wajid*), is a compressed glutinous-rice sweet cooked the
  same way.

## Baked, griddled and fried kuih

Not every kuih is soft and steamed. A whole family is crisp, caramelised or
golden with frying oil.

- **Kuih kapit**, nicknamed **"love letters,"** are wafer-thin coconut biscuits
  pressed between hot moulds and folded into fans or rolls while still pliable —
  a laborious, much-loved treat especially at Chinese New Year.
- **Bahulu** are tiny, crusty sponge cakes baked in metal moulds, often shaped
  like buttons or goldfish, and a fixture of festive tables.
- **Kuih bakar** is a baked pandan (or occasionally gula-melaka) custard-cake
  with a lightly browned, slightly firm top, cousin to the baked cassava cake
  *kuih bingka ubi*.
- **Karipap** — the Malaysian **curry puff** — is a small crimped pie of curried
  potato, sometimes with chicken or egg, in a pastry shell that may be fried or
  baked. It is one of the most popular savoury kuih.
- **Pisang goreng** (banana fritters) and related fritters such as *cucur* are
  deep-fried snacks that sit comfortably within the kuih idea. (The thin folded
  pancake *apam balik*, with its peanut-and-sweetcorn filling, is a close relative
  often sold alongside.)

## Many kitchens, many kuih

Kuih are not the property of any one community; several culinary streams feed
the same shared category.

- **Malay** kuih are the broadest group. Wikipedia notes a rough tendency for
  kuih in the northern states to lean sweet and for savoury kuih to be more
  common in the central and southern peninsula, though this is a general pattern
  rather than a hard rule.
- **Peranakan (Nyonya)** kuih, from the Straits-born Chinese communities, are
  commercially prominent, especially in the south. Many are essentially Malay
  kuih adjusted to Peranakan taste, often jewel-bright; *kuih pie tee* (crisp
  little filled cups) is a signature. These belong to the wider world of
  [regional and ethnic cuisines](/food-lifestyle/regional-and-ethnic-cuisines)
  that make Malaysian food so layered.
- **Chinese** kuih, mainly in Hokkien and Teochew styles, include **ang ku kueh**
  (ang koo) — a small cake with a soft, red, tortoise-patterned glutinous skin
  around a sweet mung-bean or peanut filling, traditionally made for birthdays
  and the full-month celebration of a baby.
- **Indian** Malaysian sweets and snacks — from syrup-soaked and milk-based
  sweets to crisp savouries — overlap with the kuih table at markets and during
  festivals, part of the same tea-time culture even when they carry their own
  names.

## When Malaysians eat kuih

Kuih are, above all, **tea-time** food. The late-afternoon *minum petang*
(afternoon tea) is their natural home, bought by the piece from kuih shops,
market stalls and roadside vendors to eat with coffee or tea — much as
[kaya toast](/food-lifestyle/kaya-toast) anchors the breakfast and coffee-shop
ritual.

They are also woven into celebration. Kuih are a staple of the
[pasar malam](/food-lifestyle/pasar-malam-and-street-food) night markets, where
trays of them are sold fresh each evening, and of the
[Ramadan bazaars](/food-lifestyle/ramadan-bazaars-and-fasting-month-food) that
appear during the fasting month, prized for breaking the fast. At
[festivals across Malaysia's communities](/food-lifestyle/festive-foods-across-malaysian-festivals)
— Hari Raya open houses above all, but also Chinese New Year and other
occasions — specific kuih mark the season: bahulu and dodol for Hari Raya, kuih
kapit for Chinese New Year, and festive layers and sweets passed around to
guests.

## The common thread

For all their variety — steamed and fried, sweet and savoury, Malay, Nyonya,
Chinese and Indian — kuih-muih share an unmistakable grammar: small handmade
cakes built on rice and coconut, scented with pandan and sweetened with gula
melaka, made to be eaten by the piece. To graze a kuih tray is to taste the
same cultural blending that runs through the rest of Malaysian cooking, in
bite-sized form.
