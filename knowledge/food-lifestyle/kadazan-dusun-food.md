Order a plate of *hinava* in Kota Kinabalu and you get something that startles
visitors expecting curry or sambal: cubes of raw fish turned opaque and firm by
nothing more than lime juice, scattered with shallots, chilli, ginger and a dusty
grating of wild-mango seed. It is the clearest signpost to a cuisine that works by
a different logic from the peninsula's — one built on acid, forage and
fermentation rather than long-simmered spice pastes. This is the food of the
Kadazan-Dusun, the indigenous community most associated with the kitchens of
Sabah.

## One people, one larder

"Kadazan-Dusun" is an umbrella for closely related indigenous groups of Sabah,
widely described as the state's largest native community; the Kadazan and Dusun
labels are sometimes treated as one people and sometimes as distinct, and the
exact relationship is contested. What their cooking shares is a dependence on what
the land, rivers and forests of North Borneo actually provide: freshwater fish,
foraged shoots and ferns, wild fruit, home-grown glutinous rice, and the time and
jars to ferment what cannot be eaten at once.

The result sits apart from the chilli-and-coconut register of much Malaysian food.
Kadazan-Dusun dishes tend to be sour, clean and pungent rather than rich, and
preservation is a central craft rather than an afterthought. It is a distinctive
corner of the broader
[Sabah food](/food-lifestyle/sabah-food) scene and of Malaysia's wider map of
[regional and ethnic cuisines](/food-lifestyle/regional-and-ethnic-cuisines).

## Hinava: the citrus-cured raw fish

*Hinava* is the cuisine's most famous dish, and the one most often served to
outsiders. Wikipedia's survey of Sabahan cuisine calls it "a traditional
Kadazan-Dusun dish of raw fish cured in lime juice," typically a firm-fleshed
white fish such as mackerel, marinated with lime, sliced shallots, chopped chilli
and julienned ginger. Prawns are sometimes used in place of fish, and recipes vary
on whether sliced bitter gourd joins the mix. It is frequently likened to
ceviche, since the citric acid firms and whitens the fish without heat.

The ingredient that sets hinava apart is the grated dried seed of the
**bambangan**, a wild mango of Borneo, which lends an earthy, faintly bitter note
that no squeeze of lime alone would give. Because the fish is cured rather than
cooked, hinava is a dish of freshness: it is dressed and eaten quickly, and is a
fixture at celebrations and at the Kaamatan harvest festival.

## Bosou, noonsom and the fermented preserves

If hinava is the cuisine at its freshest, **bosou** is it at its most patient.
My Sabah describes bosou as a "popular traditional preserved dish of the
Kadazandusun people" — raw freshwater fish such as *pelian* (mahseer) or tilapia
mixed with cooked rice, salted, and pickled with *pangi* (the seed of
*Pangium edule*), then sealed in a jar and left to ferment for around two weeks.
The same preserve is known as **noonsom** or **tonsom** in different Dusun
dialects, and it can be made not only with fish but with pork, pig fat or
vegetables.

The point of bosou was practical before it was a delicacy: My Sabah notes that,
before refrigerators, it let a household keep meat for months. The flavour it
develops is strong and tangy — an acquired taste, as Sabah Tourism's Kaamatan
guide gently puts it — and it is typically eaten in small amounts alongside rice.
Accounts differ on the preserving agent (some sources name the smoked, pulverised
*buah keluak* / pangi seed, which is the kernel of the same *Pangium edule*
fruit), so exact recipes are best taken from a local cook.

## Pinasakan and the bambangan relish

**Pinasakan** is the everyday home dish of the three. Wikipedia describes it as a
Kadazan-Dusun dish of fish simmered with turmeric leaf and rhizome and a souring
agent — unripe bambangan or the sour fruit *takob-akob* — while Sabah Tourism's
guide frames it more broadly as fish slow-cooked with turmeric, salt and a sour
element such as tamarind or wild fruit. The turmeric gives it colour and a gentle
earthiness; the souring fruit keeps it bright. It is the kind of dish cooked at
home rather than performed for guests.

The **bambangan** itself earns a second life as a relish. Beyond flavouring
hinava and pinasakan, the half-ripe fruit and its dried seed are pickled into a
tangy, slightly fermented condiment (sometimes called *noonsom bambangan*) eaten
as a side. Its sharp, resinous tang is one of the defining tastes of the Sabahan
table.

## Tuhau, sayur manis and the forage

The forest is a pantry here. **Tuhau** — the stems of a wild ginger
(*Etlingera*) — is, in Wikipedia's words, "popularly served as a relish by the
Kadazan-Dusun community," finely sliced and dressed with lime, chilli and onion,
or fried. Its aroma is powerful and polarising: pungent and sharply herbal,
closer to a strong citronella than to culinary ginger. For many Sabahans it is a
taste of home; for first-timers it can be a shock.

Alongside it come the green things gathered rather than grown: fiddlehead jungle
ferns and *sayur manis* (the sweet-leaf shrub *Sauropus androgynus*, also called
*cangkuk manis*), usually stir-fried simply, often with a little dried shrimp or
garlic. These greens are eaten widely across Sabah, and it is worth noting that
reputable references describe them as Sabahan vegetables without always tying them
specifically to Kadazan-Dusun kitchens, so the ethnic attribution should be read
loosely.

## Rice, sago and sup manuk

Rice is the backbone of the meal. A characteristic serving is **linopot** — rice,
sometimes mixing hill rice with tapioca or sweet potato, wrapped and steamed in a
leaf bundle — which Wikipedia lists among the foods shared by the Kadazan-Dusun,
Murut and Rungus communities. Sago starch, worked into a glutinous paste in the
manner of Borneo's **ambuyat**, appears in the wider East Malaysian repertoire,
though ambuyat is more strongly tied to Brunei and other communities than to the
Kadazan-Dusun specifically, and its place in this cuisine is best treated with
caution.

One dish bridges the kitchen and the brewing jar: **sup manuk lihing**, a chicken
soup enriched with rice wine. Wikipedia notes that lihing "can be used to make
chicken soup (Sup Manuk Lihing)," and The Flying Dusun records a "Drunken Chicken
Soup" as "particularly famous amongst the Kadazandusun." The wine deepens the
broth and is held, by custom, to be nourishing — a dish often cooked for new
mothers and for guests.

## Tapai, lihing and the Kaamatan table

Home-brewed rice wine is inseparable from Kadazan-Dusun culture, and from the
**Kaamatan** (Tadau Kaamatan) harvest festival celebrated across Sabah — widely
held through the month of May and culminating in a state holiday at its end — in
thanksgiving for the rice harvest. **Tapai** is the general term for these
glutinous-rice brews. **Lihing**, the most refined of them, is made, according to
The Flying Dusun, from glutinous rice (*pulut*) and a natural rice-based yeast
called *sasad*: the rice is cooked, cooled on a mat, mixed with the pounded yeast,
and sealed in a jar — traditionally scrubbed with guava leaves and closed with
banana or *tarap* leaves — to ferment. It is especially associated with the
Kadazan of Penampang, and is both drunk and cooked with.

A stronger drink sits above lihing, but its identity is genuinely contested.
Sabah Tourism's guide describes **montoku** as a stronger tapai made from cassava
rather than rice; The Flying Dusun and Wikipedia instead describe *montoku* (also
*montaku*, or *talak*) as a schnapps-like spirit distilled from the spent lihing
mash. Both can be true of different drinks sharing a name, and the distinction is
one a Kadazan-Dusun source should settle. What is not in doubt is the social role:
at Kaamatan, clay jars of home-brewed wine — drunk, by tradition, through long
straws — stand at the centre of the celebration, and the cuisine's quiet craft of
fermentation becomes, for a season, its loudest pleasure.
