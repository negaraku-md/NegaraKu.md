Malaysia has eaten brilliantly for generations — but mostly at hawker stalls,
coffee shops and home tables, not in white-tablecloth rooms with degustation
menus. That has changed quickly. Over roughly the past decade a confident
fine-dining scene has taken shape, built less on imported French or Japanese
templates than on a distinctly local idea: that Malaysia's own ingredients and
flavours deserve the full apparatus of contemporary high-end cooking. This is the
story of that scene — who leads it, how it is served, and how it reinterprets the
food Malaysians already know. For the formal rating systems that now track it,
see the separate guide to the [MICHELIN Guide in Malaysia](/food-lifestyle/michelin-guide-malaysia).

## A scene comes of age

For most of its history, Malaysian dining prestige lived in hotels and in
European, Chinese and Japanese fine-dining imports. The shift toward an
ambitious, locally rooted scene is relatively recent, gathering pace from the
mid-2010s and accelerating sharply after the MICHELIN Guide arrived in late 2022.
The international director of the MICHELIN Guide, Gwendal Poullennec, described
Malaysia at the 2026 selection as "a country where culinary heritage and modern
imagination come together in perfect balance," per the MICHELIN Guide — a useful
summary of what the scene is reaching for.

The numbers give a rough sense of scale. The MICHELIN Guide Kuala Lumpur &
Penang 2026 edition — the fourth, announced on 11 November 2025 — listed around
151 establishments, including eight one-Star restaurants, 58 Bib Gourmand picks
and 84 on the MICHELIN Selected list, according to the MICHELIN Guide. These
figures are refreshed annually and should be treated as a snapshot rather than a
fixed roster.

## Dewakan and the names at the top

If the scene has a single anchor, it is **Dewakan** in Kuala Lumpur, led by chef
Darren Teoh. It is Malaysia's only two-MICHELIN-Star restaurant, a distinction it
retained in the 2026 edition for what the MICHELIN Guide called a third
consecutive year, and the Guide praises its "innovative use of indigenous
ingredients." Dewakan is also frequently credited in the food press as a pioneer
of the modern Malaysian movement, though "pioneer" is an interpretive label
rather than an official title.

Several other names round out the top tier, and the picture differs by guide. On
the **Asia's 50 Best Restaurants 2026** list, announced in March 2026, Penang's
**Au Jardin** was ranked No. 39 — described by The Star as the first Malaysian
restaurant to enter the top 40 and "officially recognised as the best restaurant
in Malaysia" for that year. Led by chef Su Kim Hock and open since 2018, it
reportedly builds its menu around a high share of Malaysian produce. On the same
year's extended 51–100 list, Dewakan placed No. 62 and Penang's **Gen**, chef
Johnson Wong's "ingredient-first" restaurant, placed No. 89, per The Star. These
standings move from year to year — Dewakan, for instance, rose from No. 84 the
previous year — so no single number should be read as permanent.

## The degustation format

The scene's signature format is the **degustation**, or multi-course tasting
menu, in which the kitchen rather than the diner chooses a sequence of small,
composed dishes. Dewakan is built around a "seasonal tasting menu" that, in the
words of Going Places by Malaysia Airlines, "highlights Malaysia's bounty of
native ingredients"; other KL rooms such as ATAS at The RuMa Hotel have offered
multi-course degustation options in the same mould.

The format matters because it is how the modern Malaysian idea is expressed: a
long menu lets a kitchen walk diners through regions, seasons and techniques in a
way a single à la carte plate cannot. It also sets expectations — tasting menus
are typically booked ahead, served at a fixed sitting, and priced well above
everyday dining. Exact course counts, prices and formats vary by restaurant and
change over time, so it is worth confirming current details directly.

## The modern Malaysian and local-ingredient movement

The thread running through the most-discussed restaurants is a deliberate turn
toward Malaysian ingredients and flavours. The MICHELIN Guide singled out Terra
Dining, promoted to one Star in 2026 under chef Yu Cheng Chong, for its
"ingredient-driven approach to contemporary Malaysian cuisine," while Au Jardin
and Gen are both described in the press as sourcing heavily from local farmers
and fishermen.

This local-ingredient emphasis shades into sustainability. In the 2025 edition,
Dewakan became the first restaurant in Malaysia to receive a **MICHELIN Green
Star**, the Guide's recognition for sustainable gastronomy; the MICHELIN Guide
described a nose-to-tail approach and the fermenting of food scraps into
house-made sauces, and said the restaurant "exemplifies how fine dining and
sustainability can go hand in hand." Foraged and wild ingredients are often
associated with this movement in media coverage, though the precise degree of
foraging at any given restaurant is best confirmed from the kitchen itself. The
broader point is that modern Malaysian fine dining treats the country's own
larder — not an imported one — as its starting material, drawing on the same
deep well as Malaysia's [regional and ethnic cuisines](/food-lifestyle/regional-and-ethnic-cuisines).

## Kuala Lumpur and Penang: two scenes

Fine dining in Malaysia is concentrated in two cities, which is also the current
reach of the MICHELIN Guide here. **Kuala Lumpur** holds the largest cluster —
Dewakan, Beta, DC by Darren Chin, Nadodi and others — spanning modern Malaysian,
modern French and progressive South Asian cooking. **Penang**, long celebrated
for its street food, has become a serious fine-dining destination in its own
right, with Au Jardin and Gen leading its international profile.

The two cities pull in slightly different directions. Kuala Lumpur's scene is
larger, more international and more hotel-linked; Penang's leans on its intense
sense of place and its heritage larder. It is worth stressing that the MICHELIN
Guide's focus on these two cities says nothing about the quality of cooking in
Melaka, Ipoh, Johor Bahru, Kuching or Kota Kinabalu — it simply has not yet
extended there.

## Hotel dining, wine and cocktail pairing

A good deal of the high-end scene still sits inside hotels and towers, a legacy
of where fine dining first took root in Malaysia. Going Places by Malaysia
Airlines, for example, highlighted Taka by Sushi Saito within The St. Regis Kuala
Lumpur and the modern Malaysian ATAS at The RuMa Hotel, alongside high-floor
destinations such as the Marble 8 steakhouse and skyline-view rooms. Hotel
settings often bring the service polish, cellars and bar programmes that
free-standing restaurants may build more gradually.

Drink pairing has grown alongside the food. Tasting menus are increasingly
offered with a matching **wine or cocktail flight** — Going Places noted the
wine- or cocktail-pairing option at the progressive South Asian restaurant
Nadodi, and pre-dinner cocktails at DC by Darren Chin. Because Malaysia levies
substantial duties on alcohol, pairings can add significantly to the bill, and
some restaurants also design non-alcoholic pairings; confirm what is offered when
booking.

## Reinterpreting hawker and heritage flavours

Perhaps the most characteristic move of modern Malaysian fine dining is turning
humble, familiar flavours into refined courses. Au Jardin's menu, for instance,
has featured a lacto-fermented bread with tomato chutney described by The Star as
a tribute to thosai — a fine-dining nod to an everyday Indian-Malaysian
breakfast. Restaurants such as Beta are likewise described as reinterpreting
familiar Malaysian dishes through a tasting-menu lens.

This is a two-way relationship rather than a hierarchy. The same MICHELIN Guide
that stars these kitchens also honours hawker stalls and
[hawker centres and food courts](/food-lifestyle/hawker-centres-and-food-courts)
with Bib Gourmand and Selected listings, and the plated courses frequently
reference the country's [national dishes](/food-lifestyle/national-dishes-of-malaysia)
and the ritual of the local [coffee shop](/food-lifestyle/coffee-culture-malaysia).
Fine dining here does not replace the hawker table; it converses with it.
