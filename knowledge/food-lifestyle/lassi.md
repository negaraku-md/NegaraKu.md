Sit down to a fiery plate of banana-leaf rice or a rich North Indian curry, and the
drink that most often arrives to put out the fire is a tall, frothy glass of
something cool and creamy and faintly sour. That is **lassi** — the yogurt drink of
the Indian subcontinent — and in Malaysia it is the natural companion to a spicy
meal.

## What lassi is

Lassi is made by blending or whisking **yogurt** (*dahi*) with water or milk until
smooth and a little frothy, then serving it cold. That simple base is a cooler in its
own right — thick, tangy and soothing — and it is what makes lassi such a good foil
for heat and spice. From there it divides by style, and the styles matter.

For where lassi sits among the country's wider drinks board, see the
[Malaysian drinks](/food-lifestyle/malaysian-drinks) hub. This article is the deep
dive into the yogurt drink itself.

## Sweet, salted, and mango

Three broad versions cover most of what you will meet:

| You order | You get |
| --- | --- |
| sweet lassi | Yogurt sweetened with sugar — the popular, simple version |
| salted / savoury lassi | Yogurt with salt, often roasted cumin — the older, traditional style |
| mango lassi | Yogurt blended with sweetened mango pulp, rich and dessert-like |

The **salted** version, sometimes spiced with ground roasted cumin, is the older,
savoury original. **Sweet** lassi is the everyday favourite. And **mango lassi** —
yogurt blended with sweet mango pulp, sometimes enriched with cream or ice cream — is
the one most people fall for, so popular it has become almost a dessert. Malaysian
menus also carry banana and other fruit lassis.

## A South Asian drink, at home in Malaysia

Lassi is **of Indian origin**, generally traced to the **Punjab** region of the
subcontinent — a region split between India and Pakistan, so the drink is shared
across both. Its roots are ancient and not precisely documented, which is reason to
describe it as a long-established Punjabi drink rather than to fix it to a date. In
Malaysia it arrived with the country's Indian communities and settled into the
Indian-restaurant and **mamak** drinks repertoire, where it has been cooling spicy
meals ever since — including at the lassi stalls that draw their own followings, like
the well-known stand in Ipoh. For the broader eating-out world it belongs to, see
[kopitiam and mamak culture](/food-lifestyle/kopitiam-mamak-culture).

## A vegetarian, halal cooler

Part of lassi's reach is that it fits almost everyone's table. It is **yogurt-based
and meat-free** — vegetarian, and in itself halal — which makes it a natural match
for a vegetarian [banana-leaf-rice](/food-lifestyle/banana-leaf-rice) meal and an
easy order across communities. As with any drink, halal assurance attaches to the
premises, so diners who need certification should check the individual restaurant.
