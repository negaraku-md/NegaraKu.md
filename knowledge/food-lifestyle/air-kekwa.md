Among the sweet, milky drinks of the Malaysian coffee shop, **air kekwa** stands a
little apart: clear, pale gold, lightly floral, and reaching back into a long tradition
of Chinese herbal teas. Chrysanthemum tea — for that is what *kekwa* means — is the
gentle, "cooling" cup people turn to after a heavy, fried or spicy meal.

## What air kekwa is

Air kekwa is made by steeping or gently boiling **dried chrysanthemum flowers** in hot
water and sweetening the result, traditionally with **rock sugar**. Some brews add
**wolfberries** (goji), **dried longan** or **monk fruit** (*luo han guo*) for extra
body and sweetness. What you get is a clear, honey-gold tea with a mild, floral,
faintly honeyed flavour — soothing, and not at all bitter. The name itself is simply
the Malay-and-Hokkien word *kekwa*, meaning chrysanthemum.

For where air kekwa sits among the country's wider drinks board, see the
[Malaysian drinks](/food-lifestyle/malaysian-drinks) hub. This article is the deep
dive into the chrysanthemum cup itself.

## The "cooling" tradition

Air kekwa belongs to a family of **cooling herbal teas** — *liang cha* in Mandarin,
*leong cha* in Cantonese — that Chinese communities brought to Malaysia. In Chinese
food culture, foods and drinks are often sorted into **"heaty"** and **"cooling"**,
and chrysanthemum tea sits firmly on the cooling side. That is why people order it
after oily, spicy or rich "heaty" food, or simply on a hot day. It is important to be
clear about what this means: it is a **long-standing cultural belief about balance**,
part of the herbal-tea tradition — not a proven medical effect. Enjoy it as the
refreshing, soothing drink it is, and the folklore as the colour it adds.

## Fresh or from the bottle

You will meet air kekwa in two forms. At **kopitiam** and **herbal-tea (liang cha)
stalls** it is brewed fresh and poured hot or over ice, and you can ask for it **less
sweet**. In shops and supermarkets it comes **bottled and canned** — the ready-to-drink
chrysanthemum tea sold by brands across Malaysia and Singapore, fixed in sweetness but
endlessly convenient. Both are the same honeyed, floral drink. Alongside other
cooling drinks like [cincau](/food-lifestyle/cincau) and
[barley](/food-lifestyle/barley), it rounds out the country's gentler, non-milky side.

## Where to find it

Look for air kekwa at **kopitiam**, dedicated **herbal-tea stalls**, and on the
chilled shelf of any shop. It is an everyday drink — a soft, floral counterpoint to
the robust kopi and teh tarik poured at the same counter. For the institution that
pours it, see [kopitiam and mamak culture](/food-lifestyle/kopitiam-mamak-culture).
