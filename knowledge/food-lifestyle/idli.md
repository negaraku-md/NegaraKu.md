Before the griddle at a South Indian eatery starts hissing with thosai, a stack
of pale, pillowy discs is already waiting under a steamer lid: idli, the soft
steamed cake that may be the gentlest way Malaysia starts the day. Where so much
breakfast here is fried, griddled or stir-fried over roaring heat, idli is
quietly steamed — mild, spongy and made to soak up whatever you serve it with.

## What idli is

Idli is a savoury steamed cake made from a fermented batter of rice and urad dal,
the split black gram that Tamil cooks call *ulundu*. It originates in South India,
where — along with neighbouring Sri Lanka — it is one of the most common breakfast
foods. In Malaysia it belongs squarely to
[Indian-Malaysian cuisine](/food-lifestyle/indian-malaysian-cuisine), most of whose
community traces its roots to South India.

Idli shares its batter family with the better-known
[thosai](/food-lifestyle/thosai), but the two part ways at the stove. Thosai is
spread thin and griddled crisp; idli is steamed soft — and that single difference
gives idli its cloud-like texture and its place as the lighter member of the South
Indian breakfast table.

## The fermented batter

What makes idli idli is fermentation. Rice and urad dal are soaked, ground to a
batter and left to stand — usually overnight — so that natural fermentation can do
its work. One commonly cited approach uses roughly four parts rice to one part
urad dal, though proportions vary from kitchen to kitchen.

That resting period is where both the flavour and the lift come from: the urad dal
helps the batter trap air, and the fermentation produces idli's characteristic
mild sourness and its airy, spongy crumb. A well-fermented batter rises, while a
flat one yields dense cakes. Compared with thosai, idli batter is kept thicker, so
it holds its shape in the mould rather than spreading thin.

## Steamed, not griddled

To cook idli, the batter is spooned into shallow rounded depressions in a stacked
steaming tray — an *idli* mould — and set over boiling water for a few minutes
until the cakes puff and set. They come out white, domed and springy. Nothing is
browned or crisped; the appeal is entirely in the soft, faintly tangy sponge.

## Sambar, chutney and podi

An idli is rarely eaten alone. The classic partners are **sambar** — a spiced
lentil-and-vegetable broth soured with tamarind — and **coconut chutney**, into
which the soft cake is dipped. The third companion is **idli podi** (sometimes
called *milagai podi*), a dry powder of ground lentils, dried chilli and spices
mixed with a little oil or ghee. A plain idli leans entirely on these for its
flavour, which is exactly the point: it is a vehicle built to carry sambar and
chutney.

## A family of idli

Beyond the plain cake, menus and home cooks offer several forms:

- **Mini idli** — small, bite-sized cakes, often served tossed in podi and ghee or
  bathed in sambar.
- **Podi idli** — idli dusted or coated with idli podi for a spicier, drier bite.
- **Rava idli** — made with *rava* (semolina) instead of (or alongside) rice; it is
  typically a quicker, less-fermented version.
- **Sambar idli** — idli soaked in sambar until soft and savoury throughout.

The exact line-up varies by eatery, and some specialist cafes — including at least
one in Kuala Lumpur devoted to the dish — carry a longer roster of variations.

## Where and how to eat it

Idli turns up in two familiar settings. The first is the South Indian vegetarian
and [banana-leaf restaurant](/food-lifestyle/banana-leaf-rice), where it is a
morning and tea-time fixture alongside thosai and vadai. The second is the mamak
stall — the Indian-Muslim eateries where it shares the menu with roti canai and
thosai. In both, idli belongs to *tiffin*: the South Indian tradition of light
steamed and griddled snacks eaten in the morning or late afternoon, and it sits
comfortably on any list of
[Malaysian breakfast](/food-lifestyle/malaysian-breakfast) staples.

A good idli is judged simply: soft and springy, never dense or rubbery, with a
clean, faintly sour taste that comes alive the moment it meets sambar and chutney.
