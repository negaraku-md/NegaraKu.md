answer: "Asam boi is dried, preserved sour plum — a small, wrinkled, intensely sweet-salty-sour snack and flavouring of Chinese origin (known in Chinese as suan mui / 酸梅, and related to the Cantonese li hing mui). In Malaysia its most famous role is in drinks: steeped into a syrup or dropped into a glass, it turns a plain drink sweet-salty-sour. The classic is limau asam boi (calamansi lime with sour plum), and it is also added to soft drinks like Sprite and to Ribena. It is eaten as a snack too, and is a traditional travel companion said to ease motion sickness, though that use is folk rather than clinically established. Made of preserved fruit, asam boi drinks are non-alcoholic and halal, with assurance attaching to the premises or packaging."

Some flavours are hard to forget, and **asam boi** is one of them: a small, wrinkled,
dried sour plum that is sweet, salty and mouth-puckeringly sour all at once. On its own
it is a snack; dropped into a glass, it is the secret behind some of Malaysia's
best-loved drinks.

## What asam boi is

Asam boi is a **dried, preserved sour plum** — plums cured with **sugar and salt**,
sometimes with liquorice and other flavourings, until they turn chewy, wrinkled and
intensely **sweet-salty-sour**. It is **Chinese in origin**, known in Chinese as *suan
mui* (酸梅) and related to the Cantonese *li hing mui*. Some versions are strong enough
to eat straight as a snack; others are made especially to flavour drinks.

For where asam boi sits among the country's wider drinks board, see the
[Malaysian drinks](/food-lifestyle/malaysian-drinks) hub. This article is the deep dive
into the sour-plum twist itself.

## How it transforms a drink

In the glass, asam boi works by **steeping**: the plum is simmered or soaked with sugar
and water to draw out a **sweet-salty-sour syrup**, which is then stirred into the
drink. The most famous result is **limau asam boi** — fresh [lime](/food-lifestyle/air-limau-ais)
with sour plum, served over ice with a few pieces of plum and sometimes a pinch of salt.
But Malaysians add it far more widely: to **Sprite** (Sprite asam boi), to **Ribena**,
to green-apple juice, anywhere a plain sweet drink could use a savoury, moreish depth.
That one preserved plum turns sweetness into something far more interesting.

## Chinese roots, and the "travelling plum"

Asam boi comes from the **Chinese tradition** of preserved sour plums, brought to
Malaysia and Singapore by Chinese communities. Its Cantonese name is sometimes glossed
as **"travelling plum"**, reflecting an old habit of carrying it on journeys — people
have long sucked on a sour plum to settle the stomach on a bumpy road. It is worth
noting that this is a **traditional, folk use** rather than a clinically proven remedy;
enjoy the plum for its flavour, and take the folklore as the colour it adds.

## A halal everyday favourite

Asam boi is **preserved fruit**, and asam boi drinks are fruit, sugar and water with no
alcohol, so they are **halal** in themselves. As always, halal assurance attaches to
the **premises** for a stall-made drink, and to the **certification mark** on packaged
products. For the coffee-shop world these drinks belong to, see
[kopitiam and mamak culture](/food-lifestyle/kopitiam-mamak-culture).
