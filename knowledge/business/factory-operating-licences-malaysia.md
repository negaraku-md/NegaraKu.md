Getting a manufacturing licence feels like the finish line. It is closer to the starting line. The licence answers one question — *may this company make this product at this scale?* — but it says nothing about the building the machines sit in, the machines themselves, the fire risk, the council that governs the address, or the effluent going down the drain. Those are the approvals that let you actually *operate the premises*, and a factory can hold a perfectly valid manufacturing licence while being unlawful to occupy or run.

Think of it as two stacks. One stack authorises the **activity** of manufacturing. The other stack authorises the **premises** — the physical factory as a place people work in, machines run in, and pollution could come out of. This article is about the second stack: the operating layer.

## Which approvals actually let a factory operate?

The operating layer is assembled from separate authorities, each with its own statute and its own document. None of them substitutes for another.

| Approval | Authority | Legal basis | What it does |
|---|---|---|---|
| Manufacturing licence / ICA exemption | MITI / MIDA | Industrial Co-ordination Act 1975 | Authorises the manufacturing **activity** (see the dedicated guide) |
| Certificate of Completion and Compliance (CCC) | Architect/engineer (PSP) + local authority | Street, Drainage and Building Act 1974 + Uniform Building By-Laws | Makes the **building** legally fit to occupy |
| Certificate of fitness for plant | DOSH | Occupational Safety and Health Act 1994 + OSH (Plant Requiring CF) Regulations 2024 | Lets regulated **machinery** be operated |
| Fire certificate | BOMBA | Fire Services Act 1988 | Ongoing fire-safety clearance for **designated premises** |
| Business-premise + signboard licence | Local council (PBT) | Local Government Act 1976 + council by-laws | Licenses the **business at that address** |
| EIA / prescribed-premises approval | DOE | Environmental Quality Act 1974 | Approves the **activity's environmental impact** where prescribed |

The order roughly follows the life of the project: approve the impact, build the building, certify the building and its plant, clear the fire risk, then license the trade at the address.

## The federal manufacturing licence — start here, then look past it

The first stack is the **manufacturing licence** under the Industrial Co-ordination Act 1975, administered by MITI through MIDA, together with the ICA exemption route for smaller manufacturers. That is a distinct topic with its own thresholds and its own confirmation letter, covered in full in the [manufacturing licence guide](/business/manufacturing-licence-malaysia). The single most useful thing to carry over from it is this: **the manufacturing licence is not an operating permit for the building.** Once you hold it (or an ICA 10 exemption confirmation), everything below still has to be done separately.

## Is the building legally fit to occupy? The CCC

A factory cannot be lawfully occupied just because it is built. In Peninsular Malaysia the gatekeeping document is the **Certificate of Completion and Compliance (CCC)**, which replaced the old local-authority Certificate of Fitness for Occupation (CFO) in April 2007.

The CCC is issued not by the council but by the project's **Principal Submitting Person** — the registered architect or professional engineer who submitted the building plans — certifying that the building was completed in accordance with the approved plans and complies with the **Street, Drainage and Building Act 1974** and the Uniform Building By-Laws (Street, Drainage and Building Act 1974, Attorney General's Chambers). The professional carries personal responsibility for that certification. Before the CCC can be issued, the various technical agencies (including BOMBA, water, sewerage and the local authority) must clear their respective portions.

Occupying a factory without a CCC is unlawful, and it has a knock-on effect: the downstream operating licences that ask for proof of lawful occupation cannot be completed. In Sabah and Sarawak the terminology and the certificate differ — the older occupation-permit style approval under the state ordinances can still apply — so check the position with the relevant state authority rather than assuming the peninsular CCC applies.

## Registering the factory and its machinery with DOSH

This is the layer most changed by recent law. For decades a factory notified the Department of Occupational Safety and Health (DOSH) and certified its machinery under the **Factories and Machinery Act 1967**. That Act was **repealed on 1 June 2024** by the Factories and Machinery (Repeal) Act 2022, and its machinery-safety regime was consolidated into the **Occupational Safety and Health Act 1994** (Occupational Safety and Health Act 1994, DOSH).

The practical requirement survives the repeal. Regulated **plant** — steam boilers, unfired pressure vessels, and lifting equipment such as cranes, hoists, passenger lifts and gondolas — must hold a valid **certificate of fitness** issued by DOSH before it may be operated, now under the **Occupational Safety and Health (Plant Requiring Certificate of Fitness) Regulations 2024** (DOSH regulations index). The certificate is granted after inspection and must be renewed on a periodic cycle; operating regulated plant without one is an offence, and an inspector can order the machinery stopped.

On top of the plant certificates, OSHA 1994 imposes general duties on the **occupier** of every workplace — to provide a safe system of work, and, above the relevant employee threshold, to appoint safety personnel and take out the required registrations. So DOSH engagement is not a one-off certificate; it is an ongoing operating relationship for the life of the factory.

## The BOMBA fire certificate

Fire safety splits into two moments. During construction, the Fire and Rescue Department (**BOMBA**) reviews the fire-protection systems and clears its portion of the CCC. Then, for the operating phase, comes the **fire certificate**.

Section 28 of the **Fire Services Act 1988** requires every **designated premises** to hold a fire certificate, and to renew it **annually** (Fire Services Act 1988, Attorney General's Chambers). Whether your factory is "designated" turns on the Fire Services (Designated Premises) Order, which lists categories of building by use, size and location — a net that commonly catches larger industrial premises, warehouses and factories above a certain scale. Purely private dwellings and places of worship are outside it.

The fire certificate is the ongoing operating document, distinct from the one-time construction clearance. Running a designated premises without a current fire certificate is an offence carrying a substantial fine and possible imprisonment, so this is a live annual obligation, not a set-and-forget approval.

## The local-council business-premise and signboard licence

Even a fully built, plant-certified, fire-cleared factory still needs the **local council (PBT)** to license the business at that address. This is the **business-premise licence** — and, if there is any signage, the **signboard licence**, often bundled with it as a composite licence under the Local Government Act 1976.

Because this licence lives in each council's own by-laws, the documents, fees, validity period and whether the signboard licence can stand alone all differ from one council to the next — a point covered in detail in the [business-premise licence guide](/business/premise-licence-malaysia). The council will typically want to see that the premises are lawfully built and occupied (the CCC) and, where relevant, the fire clearance before it issues the licence, which is another reason the CCC sits early in the sequence.

## Does DOE need to approve your factory?

The environmental layer is the one most often assumed away, and it is entirely activity-dependent under the **Environmental Quality Act 1974** administered by the Department of Environment (DOE).

Three situations to check:

- **Prescribed activities needing an EIA.** Large or heavy industrial projects listed in the **Environmental Quality (Prescribed Activities) (Environmental Impact Assessment) Order 2015** must obtain an **approved EIA before the project is implemented** (EIA Order 2015, DOE). No approval, no project.
- **Prescribed premises.** Certain industries — crude palm oil mills, raw natural rubber factories, and scheduled-waste treatment or disposal facilities — are **prescribed premises** that need DOE **written permission to construct** and a **licence to operate**, and the DOE controls changes to their effluent and emissions (Environmental Requirements: A Guide For Investors, DOE).
- **Ordinary factories with emissions or effluent.** Even where no EIA or prescribed-premises licence is triggered, a factory that discharges industrial effluent or emits air pollutants must comply with the DOE's clean-air and effluent regulations and, in many cases, notify a new source of pollution.

The rule of thumb: the bigger or dirtier the process, the earlier and heavier the DOE involvement — and for prescribed activities the environmental approval comes *before* you build, not after.

## What's next

Sequence the operating stack the way it is actually consumed. If the activity is environmentally prescribed, DOE approval and the EIA come first, before ground is broken. Build to the approved plans, then let the Principal Submitting Person certify the **CCC** so the building can be occupied. Certify the regulated **plant** with DOSH and keep those certificates of fitness renewed. Obtain and annually renew the **BOMBA fire certificate** if the premises are designated. Then get the **local council's** business-premise and signboard licence for the address. Alongside all of it, hold the federal **manufacturing licence** (or ICA exemption) for the activity itself.

Two of these layers changed recently — the machinery regime moved from the repealed Factories and Machinery Act 1967 into OSHA 1994 in June 2024, and the environmental thresholds are periodically revised — so verify the current position with DOSH and the DOE, and confirm the Sabah/Sarawak equivalents if your factory is in East Malaysia, before you commit to a site. For the two stacks this guide points to, see the companion articles on the [manufacturing licence](/business/manufacturing-licence-malaysia) and the [business-premise licence](/business/premise-licence-malaysia).
