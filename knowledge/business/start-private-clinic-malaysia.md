Opening your own clinic feels like the natural next step after years of training and practice — but a clinic is not an ordinary shop you can incorporate on Monday and open on Friday. Malaysia regulates private healthcare tightly, and the premises themselves must be registered with the Ministry of Health before a single patient is seen. Skip that step and you are not merely unlicensed; you are committing an offence.

This is an operator's guide to doing it in the right order: who is allowed to own a clinic, how the entity and the premises are approved, and the supporting certificates — the doctor's practising certificate, the fire and local-council approvals, and the poisons licence — that a working clinic needs.

## Who can own a private clinic

A private medical clinic must be under the professional charge of a **registered medical practitioner**, and a private dental clinic under a **registered dental practitioner**. That practitioner must hold a current **Annual Practising Certificate (APC)** — more on that below. Under the **Private Healthcare Facilities and Services Act 1998 (Act 586)**, the certificate of registration for the clinic is granted in respect of that responsible person, so the clinical control cannot be delegated to a non-clinician.

The *business* wrapper around the clinic is more flexible. Doctors have long practised as sole proprietors or in partnerships, and it is now common to hold the practice through a private limited company (Sdn Bhd). Whatever the form, the professional-control requirement under Act 586 does not go away: the person answerable to the Ministry of Health for the clinic must be a properly qualified, APC-holding practitioner.

## First, register the business entity

Before you approach the Ministry of Health, set up the entity that will run the clinic and sign the lease. This is registered with the **Companies Commission of Malaysia (SSM)** — a sole proprietorship or partnership under the Registration of Businesses Act 1956, or a company under the Companies Act 2016. This gives you the legal person that holds the tenancy, employs staff, opens the bank account and, later, appears on the MOH application. It is the ordinary business layer; the healthcare-specific approvals sit on top of it.

## Registration vs licensing under Act 586

This is the distinction people most often get wrong. Act 586 treats a small clinic and a hospital differently:

| Facility | Act 586 route | Approving authority |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Private medical clinic / private dental clinic | **Registration** — apply for a certificate of registration | Director General of Health |
| Private hospital, maternity home, ambulatory care centre and other larger facilities | **Licensing** — approval to establish, then a licence to operate | Director General of Health |

A typical first practice is a clinic, so it follows the lighter **registration** path. The point to internalise is that both are mandatory: no person may lawfully establish, maintain, operate or provide a private healthcare facility or service unless it is registered or licensed under Act 586. Registration is granted by the **Director General of Health**, and the certificate must be displayed at the clinic where patients can see it.

## How the application and inspection work

Registration is handled by the Ministry of Health's private medical practice control division — **CKAPS** (Cawangan Kawalan Amalan Perubatan Swasta) — through its online **MedPCS** system. In outline, the process runs like this:

- You submit the application for the premises, including the responsible practitioner's details and APC, the entity's documents, floor plans of the clinic, and photographs of the premises.
- CKAPS reviews the proposed layout; a clinic must meet MOH standards on room sizes, treatment and sterilisation areas, and the like.
- Once the premises are ready, MOH conducts a **physical inspection** to confirm the clinic matches the approved plans and meets the standards.
- If everything is in order, the Director General issues the **certificate of registration**, and the clinic may open.

Because there is an inspection and a plan-approval stage, you cannot compress this into the final week before opening. Lodge the application well ahead of your intended opening date, and do not fit out the premises in a way that has not been approved.

## The doctor's Annual Practising Certificate

The APC is the practitioner's licence to practise, and it underpins the clinic's registration. Under the **Medical Act 1971**, any fully registered medical practitioner who wishes to practise medicine in Malaysia in a given year must hold a valid **Annual Practising Certificate** issued by the **Malaysian Medical Council (MMC)**. It is applied for online, and the practitioner must show **professional indemnity cover** and the required **continuing professional development (CPD)**.

Dentists have the equivalent from the **Malaysian Dental Council (MDC)**: a dental APC, renewed each year (applications for the following year are due by 1 November of the current year), with a fee that scales with the number of practice addresses — RM100 for one address, RM200 for two, and RM300 for three or more. Keep the APC current: if it lapses, the practitioner is not lawfully practising, which puts the clinic's registration in question.

## Premises: CCC, BOMBA and local council

The building itself has to be lawful and safe before it can host a clinic:

- **Certificate of Completion and Compliance (CCC)** — the premises must be a legally completed, fit-for-occupation building (a CCC, or an older Certificate of Fitness), and the use must be permitted for a clinic.
- **BOMBA (Fire and Rescue Department)** — the premises must meet fire-safety requirements; a successful fire inspection yields a fire-safety support letter that the local authority relies on when issuing the business licence.
- **Local council (PBT)** — you need the local authority's **business premises licence** and a **signboard/advertisement licence** before displaying the clinic's name.

Clinics also generate clinical waste, so a **licensed clinical-waste disposal arrangement** is expected as part of running the premises to standard.

## Dispensing medicines: the Poisons Act 1952

Most clinics keep and hand out medicines, and that brings in the **Poisons Act 1952 (Act 366)**, enforced by the **Pharmacy Enforcement Division** of the Ministry of Health at **state level**. Registered medical practitioners have standing under the Act to supply medicines to their own patients in the course of treatment, but a clinic that stores and dispenses **scheduled poisons** generally needs the appropriate **poison licence**, issued at the state level. Poison licences are graded by type — a Type A licence, for example, covers dealing generally in poisons and is issued to a pharmacist. Because the exact requirement turns on what your clinic will keep and dispense, confirm it with your **state Pharmacy Enforcement Division** before you order stock, rather than assuming your practitioner status covers everything.

## What's next

Sequence the work so nothing blocks anything else: register the entity with SSM, secure premises that already have a CCC and are permitted for clinic use, get the practitioner's APC current, then run the two premises tracks in parallel — the BOMBA fire support letter and the local-council business and signboard licences on one side, and the MOH/CKAPS registration and inspection on the other. Sort out the Poisons Act position with your state Pharmacy Enforcement Division before you stock medicines. Standards, forms and fees change, so verify the current requirements directly with CKAPS, the MMC or MDC, BOMBA, your local council and your state Pharmacy Enforcement Division before you commit to a lease or a fit-out.
