faq:
  - q: "Do I need an APAD licence to carry my own company's goods?"
    a: "If you carry only goods that belong to your own business, you apply for a Carrier Licence C (private carrier), which has no paid-up capital or equity condition. You only need a Carrier Licence A (public carrier) if you carry goods belonging to third parties for hire or reward. Separately, since 1 April 2026 a 'de-controlled' goods vehicle with a laden weight not exceeding 7,500 kg used purely to carry the owner's own goods is exempt from an APAD operator permit — but hire-and-reward carriage always needs a Carrier Licence A. Verify your vehicle class and the current exemption with APAD."
  - q: "Who issues the operator licence in Sabah and Sarawak?"
    a: "Not APAD. The Land Public Transport Act 2010 applies to Peninsular Malaysia; in Sabah and Sarawak commercial vehicle licensing is handled by the Commercial Vehicle Licensing Board (Lembaga Pelesenan Kenderaan Perdagangan, CVLB/LPKP) under the Commercial Vehicles Licensing Board Act 1987 (Act 334). The Carrier A and Carrier C concept is the same on both sides, but the applications, forms and portals are separate, and moving goods across the Peninsula-Borneo line can need permits on both sides."
  - q: "What licence does a freight forwarder need to clear customs?"
    a: "A customs agent licence under section 90 of the Customs Act 1967, issued by the Royal Malaysian Customs Department (RMCD). It lets a forwarding or shipping agent act on behalf of importers and exporters to declare and release goods from customs control. In practice a company first obtains International Integrated Logistics Services (IILS) status from MIDA, then applies to RMCD. Under section 90(2), an approved customs agent must also register under the Service Tax Act 2018, and the industry guidance requires that registration within 14 days of approval."
  - q: "What driving licence do my lorry drivers need?"
    a: "On top of the correct class of competent driving licence (Class D for lighter commercial vehicles up to the heavier Class E), a driver who drives a goods vehicle commercially needs a Goods Driving Licence (GDL) — a vocational licence issued by the Road Transport Department (JPJ). Applicants must hold a valid CDL for more than a year, pass a JPJ medical examination and the vocational tests. The GDL fee is RM20 per year."
  - q: "Can a fully foreign-owned company run a haulage business in Malaysia?"
    a: "Not straightforwardly. A Carrier Licence A (hire and reward) requires majority Malaysian equity with a Bumiputera component and a minimum paid-up capital, so a haulier serving third parties is effectively reserved for majority-local companies. Foreign-linked groups usually enter through IILS status and a customs/forwarding licence, or by carrying their own goods under a Carrier Licence C. Confirm the exact equity split and capital floor with APAD and MIDA before you structure the shareholding."

Starting a logistics company in Malaysia is not one decision but a stack of them. The Sdn Bhd is the easy part; the value — and the delay — is in the licences. A lorry that carries someone else's cargo, a forwarder that lodges a customs declaration, and a driver behind the wheel each answer to a different regulator, and the biggest one — the operator licence — is also where foreign ownership hits a wall. This is the operator's set-up path, in the order you actually need it.

(This is the "how do I start the business" guide. For the sector as a whole — ports, the national freight picture, where the industry is heading — see the macro logistics-and-ports article.)

## First, what kind of logistics are you actually doing?

Before any form, pin down the activity, because it decides which regulator you face:

- **Haulage / trucking for others** — moving third-party goods by road for a fee. This is the **Carrier Licence A** world (APAD or CVLB).
- **In-house transport** — moving only your own company's goods. This is **Carrier Licence C**, a much lighter gate.
- **Freight forwarding / customs clearance** — arranging shipments and clearing them through customs. This needs a **customs agent licence** under the Customs Act, and usually **MIDA IILS status** behind it.
- **Warehousing / bonded storage** — if you store goods on which duty is suspended, that warehouse needs its own **customs licence**.

Most real operators do more than one of these, and each layer is a separate application. Map them before you incorporate, because they drive your shareholding and paid-up capital.

## Step 1 — Incorporate the company with SSM

Everything sits on a legal entity. The standard vehicle is a private limited company (**Sdn Bhd**) registered with the **Companies Commission of Malaysia (SSM)** under the Companies Act 2016 — at least one resident director, a company secretary, a registered office. A logistics company is otherwise an ordinary trading company at this stage; the sector-specific gates come next.

Two things to decide *now*, not later, because they are hard to unwind:

- **Shareholding.** If you intend to hold a **Carrier Licence A**, the company must meet a majority-Malaysian, part-Bumiputera equity condition (see Step 2). Structure the cap table for that from day one.
- **Paid-up capital.** Both the Carrier A licence and the customs-agent route carry paid-up capital expectations well above the RM1 you can incorporate with, so budget the capital injection into your incorporation plan.

## Step 2 — The operator licence: Carrier A vs Carrier C

This is the licence that defines a logistics business, and it turns on one question: **whose goods are you carrying?**

In **Peninsular Malaysia** it is issued by the **Land Public Transport Agency (APAD)** under the **Land Public Transport Act 2010 (Act 715)**; APAD's Commercial Licensing Division licenses public transport, tourist and **goods vehicles**. In **Sabah and Sarawak** the same function is performed by the **Commercial Vehicle Licensing Board (CVLB / LPKP)** under the **Commercial Vehicles Licensing Board Act 1987 (Act 334)**. Different laws, different portals — same A-versus-C logic.

| | **Carrier Licence A** (public carrier) | **Carrier Licence C** (private carrier) |
|---|---|---|
| **What it covers** | Carrying goods belonging to third parties **for hire or reward** | Carrying **your own** company's goods only |
| **Who needs it** | Hauliers, trucking firms, 3PLs, courier fleets | Manufacturers, retailers, distributors moving own stock |
| **Equity condition** | Majority Malaysian equity, with a Bumiputera portion | None |
| **Paid-up capital** | A minimum floor applies (higher for container carriage) | None specified |
| **Issued by** | APAD (Peninsula) / CVLB (Sabah, Sarawak) | APAD (Peninsula) / CVLB (Sabah, Sarawak) |

The equity and capital conditions on **Carrier A** are the real barrier. The figures commonly applied for a non-container Carrier A are a minimum paid-up capital in the low-hundreds-of-thousands of ringgit and at least 51% Malaysian equity including a 30% Bumiputera share, with a higher capital floor for container haulage (which has at times been frozen to new entrants). **Treat those numbers as indicative and confirm the current thresholds directly with APAD** — they are set by policy and change. A pure own-account operator taking **Carrier C** sidesteps all of it.

One recent wrinkle: from **1 April 2026** the government clarified that a **"de-controlled" goods vehicle** not exceeding **7,500 kg** laden weight, used only to carry the owner's **own** goods, does not need an APAD operator permit. Hire-and-reward carriage still always needs a Carrier A. If you sit near that boundary, check your vehicle's classification with APAD before assuming you are exempt.

## Step 3 — If you clear customs: the section 90 forwarding-agent licence

Moving a box is one licence; **declaring it to customs** is another. A freight forwarder, shipping agent or customs broker that acts for importers and exporters — lodging declarations and releasing goods from customs control — must hold a **customs agent licence under section 90 of the Customs Act 1967**, issued by the **Royal Malaysian Customs Department (RMCD)**.

In practice the doorway is **MIDA**: a company is generally expected to obtain **International Integrated Logistics Services (IILS) status** from the Malaysian Investment Development Authority before RMCD will approve it as a customs agent. IILS is the status for firms offering integrated logistics — freight forwarding, warehousing, transportation and customs clearance under one roof.

Two conditions catch new forwarders out:

- **A real office.** RMCD wants proof of a physical, operational premises; shell companies are refused.
- **Service tax.** Under **section 90(2)**, an approved customs agent must be a registered person under the **Service Tax Act 2018**, and must register for service tax **within 14 days** of being approved as an agent.

## Step 4 — Put compliant drivers behind the wheel

A licensed fleet still needs licensed drivers. Anyone who drives a **goods vehicle commercially** must hold a **Goods Driving Licence (GDL)** — a **vocational licence from the Road Transport Department (JPJ)** — on top of the correct class of competent driving licence (Class D through the heavier Class E). To qualify, a driver must have held a valid CDL for more than a year, pass a JPJ medical examination and the vocational theory and practical tests. The **GDL fee is RM20 per year**. Driving a commercial lorry without a valid GDL is an offence and voids much of your operating position, so build the licence check into hiring.

## Step 5 — Warehousing, if you store duty-suspended goods

If your model includes **bonded storage** — holding imported goods before duty is paid — the warehouse itself needs a **licence under section 65 of the Customs Act 1967** (ordinary, public bonded, or private bonded warehouse), and bonded warehouses must lodge a **bank guarantee** pegged to the duties at risk. Ordinary dry warehousing that only stores duty-paid or local goods does not need a customs warehouse licence — but check local-authority premises and fire approvals, which every warehouse needs regardless.

## What's next

Sequence the set-up in the order the gates actually bind: incorporate the Sdn Bhd with the **right shareholding and capital** for the licence you want; secure the **operator (Carrier) licence** — A if you carry for others, C if you carry your own — from **APAD** or the **CVLB**; add the **section 90 customs-agent licence** (via **MIDA IILS**) only if you clear customs; make sure every driver holds a **GDL**; and licence any **bonded warehouse** separately. The Carrier A equity and capital conditions are the decision that shapes everything upstream, so settle your ownership structure before you spend on trucks.

Because the paid-up capital, equity and exemption thresholds are set by policy and were being adjusted through 2026, treat the specific figures here as a starting point and **verify the current requirements with APAD (or the CVLB), MIDA and RMCD** before you commit.
