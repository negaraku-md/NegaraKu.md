Opening a tuition centre or a private school looks, from the outside, like renting a shoplot, hiring good teachers and printing a banner. In Malaysia it is not. Education is a regulated activity, and registering a company with SSM — the step most first-time operators think of as "starting the business" — only gets you a legal entity. It does not give you the right to teach a single paying student.

The right to operate comes from a second, separate layer: registering the *institution* with the education regulator. Which regulator, and which route, depends on what you are running. Here is how to set up a private education business legally, from the entity to the classroom door.

## Which regulator are you under?

The first question decides everything else. Malaysia splits private education into two regimes:

- **Schools, tuition centres, enrichment/skills centres and kindergartens** fall under the **Education Act 1996 (Act 550)**, regulated by the **Ministry of Education (MOE)** and its **State Education Departments** (Jabatan Pendidikan Negeri, JPN).
- **Colleges, university colleges and universities** fall under the **Private Higher Educational Institutions Act 1996 (Act 555)**, regulated by the **Ministry of Higher Education (MOHE)**.

Section 71 of the Education Act 1996 deliberately carves higher education out of its own scope, pushing it to Act 555. So a tuition centre and a private university are not two sizes of the same licence — they are two different laws with two different regulators. Most of this guide covers the Education Act route; the higher-education route is summarised at the end.

## Step one: register the business entity with SSM

Whatever you are building, you first need a legal vehicle. Register it with the **Companies Commission of Malaysia (SSM)**:

- a **private limited company (Sdn Bhd)** — the usual choice for anything you intend to scale, franchise or take on partners; or
- a **sole proprietorship or partnership** — simpler and cheaper for a small one-location tuition centre, but with unlimited personal liability.

This is the same entity-registration step every Malaysian business takes, and it is necessary but *not sufficient*. Having a registered Sdn Bhd named "Bright Minds Tuition Sdn Bhd" does not entitle it to enrol students. That entitlement is the next layer.

## Why you must register the institution before enrolling a student

Under **section 79 of the Education Act 1996**, *all* private educational institutions must be registered — the Ministry's own guidance states plainly that "all types of private education institutions are required to register under section 79." A tuition centre, an enrichment centre, a kindergarten and a private school are each a "private educational institution" and each must be registered in its own right.

Crucially, registration is a two-stage gate. Before an institution can be registered, you must first obtain **establishment approval** (*kelulusan penubuhan*): the Ministry requires that "before a private educational institution is allowed to register, establishment approval must be obtained first." The establishment application is uploaded through the Ministry's **MOEIS** online system and submitted to the State Education Department for approval; only after that does the institution proceed to registration.

The penalty for skipping this is real. The Private Education Division enforces the Act, and operating an unregistered institution can attract **a fine of up to RM30,000, or up to two years' imprisonment, or both**. Registration is the licence to operate — not a formality to catch up on later.

## Tuition or enrichment centre vs private school: which door?

Both routes register under section 79, but through different offices:

| | **Tuition / enrichment / skills centre, kindergarten** | **Private school (academic, religious, international, expatriate)** |
|---|---|---|
| **Registering body** | State Education Department (JPN) | Ministry's Private Education Division (Bahagian Pendidikan Swasta) |
| **Governing law** | Education Act 1996, s.79 | Education Act 1996, s.79 |
| **Establishment approval** | Required first, via MOEIS + JPN | Required first, via MOEIS, per school-type guidelines |
| **Curriculum control** | Lighter — course/subject content declared | National Curriculum for primary/secondary (s.75); Ministry guidelines per school type |

The practical takeaway: a **tuition or enrichment centre** is registered at **State (JPN) level**, which makes it the lighter-touch, faster route for most first-time operators. A **full private school** — one offering primary or secondary education, whether academic, religious, international or expatriate — goes through the **Private Education Division** and follows the Ministry's establishment guidelines for that specific school type, carrying heavier curriculum and facility obligations.

## Premises, fire safety and the council licence

Approval of your premises is part of the establishment stage, not an afterthought. Per the Ministry's private-education guidance, the premises must be cleared by three bodies:

- the **Local Authority** (the municipal or city council) — for the business/premise licence and permitted land use;
- the **Fire and Rescue Department (Bomba)** — for fire-safety compliance; and
- the **District Health Office** — for health and sanitation.

The premises must also meet the Ministry's specifications for classrooms and facilities. Because these clearances gate the registration, the sensible order is to identify a compliant premises — correct zoning, adequate fire exits, suitable layout — *before* you sign a long lease. Operators routinely underestimate how much a shoplot needs to be modified to pass inspection.

## Teachers and curriculum

Your establishment application must include the **names of the administrators (pengelola), teachers and staff** of the institution — the regulator vets who is running and teaching at the centre, not just the premises. For a **private school**, curriculum is also controlled: under **section 75**, institutions providing primary or secondary education must follow the **National Curriculum** and prepare pupils for prescribed examinations, while post-secondary institutions may be required to teach the national language, Malaysian studies and related subjects. A tuition centre's curriculum is lighter-touch, but the teaching staff and course content are still declared and subject to approval.

## Private colleges and universities: a different Act entirely

If your ambition is a **college, university college or university**, none of the above applies — you are under **Act 555** and **MOHE**. The regime is heavier:

- you first obtain a **Letter of Authorization** to establish the institution (the Ministry targets issuing this within 45 working days of a complete application);
- you then **register** with the **Registrar General of Private Higher Educational Institutions** and receive a **Certificate of Registration**, which must be displayed on the premises (targeted within 30 working days of a complete application, after the required agency approvals); and
- each **programme** needs course approval, and qualifications are **accredited by the Malaysian Qualifications Agency (MQA)**. Compulsory general subjects (Mata Pelajaran Umum) also apply.

In short: same country, different law, different ministry, and a materially longer runway. Do not assume a "private education" licence is one thing.

## What's next

Sequence the two layers deliberately. **Register the business with SSM** to get your entity, then treat **institution registration under section 79** as the real licence — and remember it is a two-stage gate: **establishment approval first** (premises cleared by council, Bomba and Health; administrators, teachers and curriculum declared), then registration itself, through the **JPN for a tuition or enrichment centre** or the **Private Education Division for a private school**. Do not enrol students until the institution is registered; the RM30,000 / two-year penalty is not theoretical.

If you are aiming higher — a college or university — start instead from **Act 555 and MOHE**, where the Letter of Authorization, Certificate of Registration, course approval and MQA accreditation define the path. Because forms, guidelines and fee schedules are updated from time to time, confirm the current requirements directly with your State Education Department or the Ministry's Private Education Division before you commit capital.
