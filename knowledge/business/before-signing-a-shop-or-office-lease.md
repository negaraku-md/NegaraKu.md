First, suitability: is the premises approved for your activity? faq:
  - q: "The landlord is happy to rent to me — isn't that enough?"
    a: "No. A landlord's willingness to lease does not mean your business may lawfully operate there. Two separate things still have to be true: the premises must be approved and suitable for your activity (the right land use/zoning, a valid Certificate of Completion and Compliance, and — in a strata building — management by-laws that permit your use), and the licences your activity needs must actually be obtainable at that address. These depend on the approved use and building status, not on the lease. Confirm them with the local authority (PBT) before you sign, because fixing a suitability or licensing problem afterwards is far harder."
  - q: "Do I need a licence to put up my shop signboard?"
    a: "Generally yes. Most businesses need both a business premises licence (lesen premis perniagaan) and a separate signboard or advertisement licence (lesen papan tanda / lesen iklan) from the local council (PBT), issued under the Local Government Act 1976 and the council's by-laws. Signboard approval typically depends on the design, size and whether it is illuminated, and most councils require Bahasa Malaysia to appear clearly. Fees and conditions vary council by council, so check your specific PBT — and confirm the signboard you plan can actually be approved for that premises before signing the lease."
  - q: "If my business fails, can the landlord just lock me out or seize my stock?"
    a: "Not by self-help. Under the Specific Relief Act 1950, a landlord cannot evict you or retake possession without a court order — changing the locks or forcibly removing you is unlawful. For unpaid rent, the landlord's remedy is a court-issued warrant of distress under the Distress Act 1951, where a court bailiff (not the landlord) may seize and auction movable goods on the premises; but it is capped at the arrears for the preceding 12 months, it is not an eviction tool, and goods actually used in your trade or business are generally exempt. These are general protections; the lease still governs most of your position, so read it carefully."
  - q: "Is the shoplot being 'commercial' enough to run any business there?"
    a: "Not necessarily. 'Commercial' premises can still be restricted to particular activities by the land-use category, the local authority's zoning, and (in a strata building) the management by-laws — so the same unit may be fine for one trade and not another. Some councils confine specific activities to specific zones. And certain trades carry extra premises conditions: food and beverage premises need food-premises/health clearance under the Food Act 1983 and may face a state-variable halal requirement, and other trades have their own rules. Check your activity against the premises with the PBT (and the management body, for strata) before committing."

Signing a commercial lease feels like the moment the business becomes real — keys,
an address, a place to fit out. It is also the moment a founder most often skips
the checks that decide whether the business can legally open there at all. The
trap is a natural assumption: *the landlord is renting it to me, so I can run my
business here.* A willing landlord and a signed lease grant neither the right use
of the premises nor the licences your trade needs.

There are two separate gates to clear **before** you sign, and the lease itself is
a third thing you have to get right because — unlike housing — there is no special
law to fall back on.

## Gate 1: Is the premises suitable for your activity?

**Permitted use comes first.** Premises have an approved use set by the **land-title
category and conditions** and the **local authority's (PBT — Pihak Berkuasa
Tempatan) zoning** under the **Local Government Act 1976 (Act 171)** and the **Town
and Country Planning Act 1976 (Act 172)**. Running a business from premises not
approved for that activity — or from a *residential* unit — can breach both the
council's rules and the land-title conditions. If the premises need a **change of
use**, that requires approval (from the State Authority for the land title, often
with planning permission from the PBT) and it **can be refused** — so find out
before you are committed to the rent.

"Commercial" is not a blanket permission, either: the land-use category, zoning and
(for strata) by-laws can restrict a unit to particular activities, so the same
shoplot may be fine for one trade and not another.

**Building fitness** is the second half of suitability. Confirm the building has a
valid **Certificate of Completion and Compliance (CCC)** — the document (which
replaced the old Certificate of Fitness for Occupation) certifying it was completed
to the approved plan and is fit for occupation under the **Street, Drainage and
Building Act 1974 (Act 133)**. A unit **without a valid CCC may not be lawfully
occupied.** And if you plan to fit out or renovate, that usually needs **building-plan
approval first** — even knocking down a partition or combining lots — and
unapproved works can later block a licence renewal.

**Strata adds a third layer.** If the unit is in a strata building, the **Strata
Management Act 2013 (Act 757)** and the scheme's **by-laws** (enforced by the
management corporation or JMB) can independently restrict your **use, renovation
and signage** — over and above the lease and the PBT. Get the by-laws and confirm
your activity, fit-out and signboard are allowed.

## Gate 2: Can you get the licences — at this address?

Most businesses need, from the local council (PBT) under the Local Government Act
1976, a **business premises licence (lesen premis perniagaan)** and a separate
**signboard / advertisement licence (lesen papan tanda / lesen iklan)**. The key
point for a tenant: **these conditions attach to the premises.** Whether the
licences can be *issued* depends on the approved use, the building status and the
PBT's conditions — not on the landlord's willingness to lease. So confirm, ideally
with the PBT before signing, that the licences your activity needs can actually be
granted for **that** address.

Some trades carry extra premises conditions. **Food and beverage** premises need a
food-premises or health clearance under the **Food Act 1983**, with inspection, and
may face a **halal requirement** that is **state-variable and moving** (some states
have introduced halal certification as a condition of an F&B licence; there has
been federal pushback) — so check your state, your PBT and **JAKIM** rather than
assuming a national rule. Fire-safety (Bomba) and other sector approvals may also
apply. Everything here — fees, conditions, zoning — **varies council by council**,
so your own PBT is the authority.

## The third thing: the lease is your main protection

Here is the part that surprises many tenants: **Malaysia has no dedicated
commercial-tenancy protection statute.** Housing has the Housing Development
(Control and Licensing) Act 1966, but commercial leases are largely left to the
**Contracts Act 1950** — meaning **the lease document itself is the main, often
only, determinant of your rights.** There is no statutory safety net to catch an
unfair term.

That does not mean no law applies. General statutes still bite: a landlord **cannot
evict you by self-help** — a court order is required under the **Specific Relief Act
1950**; a landlord's remedy for **rent arrears** is a court-issued **warrant of
distress** under the **Distress Act 1951**, executed by a bailiff, capped at the
preceding 12 months and with goods used in your trade generally exempt; and a
deposit forfeiture that operates as a **penalty** rather than genuine loss can be
challengeable under the Contracts Act. But these are backstops, not a substitute
for a well-drafted lease.

So, before you sign, read and negotiate into the document:

- **rent revision** terms (how and when rent can rise);
- **break and termination** rights, and notice;
- **reinstatement / make-good** obligations at the end;
- **who bears licensing, renovation and sector-approval costs** (tenant or landlord);
- and a **contingency if a licence or approval is refused** — ideally a right to
  terminate and recover your deposit.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating a signable lease as permission to operate.** Suitability and licensing
  are separate gates the lease does not clear.
- **Assuming 'commercial' means any business.** Use category, zoning and strata
  by-laws can restrict the activity.
- **Skipping the CCC and renovation-approval check.** An un-fit unit or unapproved
  fit-out can stop you occupying or licensing it.
- **Relying on a statute to protect a commercial tenant.** There isn't a dedicated
  one — the lease is your protection.
- **Using the Peninsular rules in Sabah or Sarawak**, which run under separate
  legislation.
