Seven friends who want to run a residents' association, an alumni group, a sports club, a religious body or a welfare NGO all reach for the same legal vehicle in Malaysia: the **society**, or *persatuan*. It is the country's default non-profit membership structure — cheap to set up and lightly capitalised — but it lives under its own statute, the Societies Act 1966, and its own regulator, and getting the paperwork wrong can leave you running an *unlawful* body without realising it.

Here is how registering a society actually works, and when a company limited by guarantee or a trust is the better tool instead.

## What counts as a "society" under the Act?

A society is defined very broadly. Under **section 2 of the Societies Act 1966**, a *society* "includes any club, company, partnership, or association of seven or more persons whatever its nature or object, whether temporary or permanent." The key threshold is **seven or more persons** associating for a common purpose.

Just as important is what a society is **not**. The same section carves out bodies that belong under other laws — a company registered under the Companies Act, a co-operative, a trade union, a school body, and, crucially, **any company, association or partnership formed for the sole purpose of carrying on a lawful business that has for its object the acquisition of gain**. In plain terms: a society is a *non-profit* membership association. If your aim is to earn and distribute profit, you need a company, not a society.

The regulator is the **Registrar of Societies (ROS)** — the *Jabatan Pendaftaran Pertubuhan Malaysia* (JPPM) — which sits under the Ministry of Home Affairs, a different ministry from the one that oversees companies.

## Why you must register — and register on time

Registration is not optional. **Section 6(1)** requires every local society to apply to the Registrar for registration in the manner prescribed. Two consequences follow if you skip it:

- Under **section 6(2)**, until the society is registered, no one may organise or take part in any activity of the society — other than corresponding with the Registrar — **without the Registrar's written permission**.
- Under **section 41(1)(b)**, a society that is not registered under section 7 is an **unlawful society**, and its office-bearers commit an offence under section 42.

So the sequence matters: you form the pro-tem committee, then apply promptly, and you hold back from running events, opening accounts in the society's name, or collecting subscriptions until the certificate is issued. (The Societies Regulations 1984 prescribe the application form and a short window for lodging it after the society is established.)

## The pro-tem committee and the three office-bearers

The founding step is a **pro-tem (provisional) meeting** of at least seven people. That meeting does the substantive work: it agrees the society's name, adopts the **constitution**, and elects the committee — including the three main office-bearers the system revolves around, the **President, Secretary and Treasurer** (*Yang Dipertua, Setiausaha, Bendahari*).

The application itself is filed online through **eROSES** (`eroses.gov.my`), JPPM's electronic registration system. The **Secretary** registers as an eROSES user and lodges the application, and the three main office-bearers verify their identities in the system. A nominal registration fee applies (JPPM sets it at **RM30** for a new society), paid through eROSES. Note that the Registrar may require the office-bearers to be Malaysian citizens — the First Schedule, paragraph 1(gg), lets the Registrar demand that office-bearers and executives be citizens.

## The constitution the law requires

A society cannot be registered on a name and a wish — it must adopt a constitution (rules) covering a fixed checklist. The **First Schedule** to the Act (referenced by **section 7(3)(e)**) requires the constitution to provide for, among other things:

- the **name** and registered place of business or postal address;
- the **aims or objects** for which the society is formed and its funds may be used;
- the **qualifications for membership** and how members are admitted;
- the **composition and appointment of the committee** and the powers of the office-bearers;
- the **sources of income**, entrance fees and subscriptions;
- who may **authorise expenditure**, the keeping of **accounts** and their annual publication to members, and the appointment of **auditors**;
- the **quorum** for meetings; and
- how the **constitution may be amended**.

If any required matter is missing, **section 7(3)(e)** allows the Registrar to refuse registration. To make this easy, JPPM publishes **model constitutions** (for non-branch, branch and mutual-benefit societies) that a new society can adopt — using the template is the quickest path to approval.

## Name approval and grounds for refusal

The name is checked at registration. Under **section 7(3)(d)** the Registrar **must refuse** a name that is misleading as to the society's character, that so nearly resembles another society's name as to deceive, that is **identical** to an existing local society's name, or that is, in the Registrar's opinion, undesirable. JPPM also expects the name in **Bahasa Melayu** (with an English version in brackets if used). Beyond the name, section 7(3) lets the Registrar refuse a society that appears **unlawful or prejudicial to peace, security, public order or morality**, or that does not genuinely exist.

## After registration: annual returns

Registration brings an ongoing duty. Under **section 14**, every registered society must, **within sixty days after its annual general meeting** — or within sixty days after the end of the calendar year if no AGM is held — furnish the Registrar with an updated list of office-bearers and member numbers, any amendments to its rules, its address, and its **accounts and balance sheet** for the last financial year. Neglecting these filings is a common reason registrations are cancelled.

## Society vs company limited by guarantee vs trust

The society is one of three non-profit routes in Malaysia, and they are governed by different laws and regulators:

| Feature | Society / *persatuan* (ROS) | Company limited by guarantee (SSM) | Charitable trust / foundation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Governing law | Societies Act 1966 | Companies Act 2016, section 45 | Trust law; Trustees (Incorporation) Act 1952 for a body corporate |
| Regulator | Registrar of Societies (JPPM), Home Ministry | Companies Commission (SSM) | Legal Affairs Division / the courts |
| Founders | 7+ members | Members (a public company) | Settlor + trustees; no members |
| Legal personality | Registered body under the Act | Full separate legal entity (a company) | Only if trustees are incorporated |
| Cost & formality | Lowest; small fee, model constitution | Higher; company compliance, fit-and-proper vetting | Moderate; a formal trust deed |
| Typical use | Clubs, associations, community & religious NGOs | Foundations, professional bodies, chambers of commerce | Endowments, family or grant-making foundations |

The **[company limited by guarantee](/en/business/company-limited-by-guarantee-malaysia)** is the more formal, company-law route — a public company with no share capital, subject to director vetting and (to drop "Berhad") a RM1 million fund. A **trust** suits an endowment run by trustees rather than a membership: the trustees can be incorporated under the Trustees (Incorporation) Act 1952 to give the fund perpetual succession. For most grassroots groups, the **society is the natural fit** because it is member-based, inexpensive and purpose-built for associations.

## Tax exemption is a separate step

Registering a society does **not** by itself make it tax-exempt or make donations to it tax-deductible. That status comes from a **separate application to LHDN** for approval as an institution or organisation under **subsection 44(6) of the Income Tax Act 1967**. Approval exempts the body's income and lets donors claim a deduction for gifts — but it is granted by the tax authority on its own criteria, quite apart from your ROS registration. Plan for it as a second, later step once the society is running and can show its objects and accounts.

## What's next

If a society fits your purpose, the practical path is short: gather your seven founders, hold the pro-tem meeting to agree a name and adopt JPPM's model constitution, elect your President, Secretary and Treasurer, and have the Secretary lodge the application through eROSES — and hold off on activities and fundraising until the certificate is issued, because an unregistered society is unlawful. Once registered, calendar the annual return under section 14 so the registration is never at risk, and treat LHDN's subsection 44(6) tax exemption as a deliberate follow-on rather than an automatic benefit. If your body will hold significant assets, run commercial-style activities, or needs the credibility of company status, compare this against the [company limited by guarantee](/en/business/company-limited-by-guarantee-malaysia) before you commit.
