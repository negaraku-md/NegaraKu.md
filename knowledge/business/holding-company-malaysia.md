Once a business owns more than one company — a trading arm and a property arm, two brands, an operating company and the family's investments — the question stops being "how do I run my company?" and becomes "how do I structure the group?" A holding company sits on top, owning the others, and it can bring cleaner ownership, asset protection and a smoother exit. It also brings consolidated accounts, its own tax regime, and charges you might not expect when you move the pieces around.

Here is how holding companies and groups work in Malaysia, in law and in tax.

## What counts as a holding company

The definitions are set precisely by the **Companies Act 2016**, and they are more nuanced than the "owns more than 50%" shorthand most people use.

Under **section 4**, a corporation is a **subsidiary** of another — making that other its **holding company** — if the holding company does any *one* of three things:

- **controls the composition of the subsidiary's board of directors**;
- **controls more than half of its voting power**; or
- **holds more than half of its issued shares** (excluding preference shares).

Any one is enough — control of the board can make a company a subsidiary even without a majority shareholding. A company is also the holding company of any subsidiary of its own subsidiary, which is how control cascades down a multi-tier group.

Two related terms complete the picture:

- an **ultimate holding company** (section 5) has subsidiaries but is not itself a subsidiary of anyone — the entity at the very top;
- a **wholly-owned subsidiary** (section 6) has no members except its holding company or that company's nominees.

## The first hidden cost: consolidated accounts

A group cannot report as if the companies were unrelated. Under **sections 248 and 250** of the Companies Act 2016, the directors must prepare annual **consolidated financial statements** that include **all** the company's subsidiaries and present a true and fair view of the group as a whole. *How* to consolidate — the control principle, eliminating intra-group transactions — is governed by the accounting standard **MFRS 10**.

This is the compliance cost owners most often underestimate. Consolidation means a group-level audit, more accounting work, and more disclosure than running the same businesses as separate, unlinked companies. It is worth budgeting for before you build the structure.

## The tax question: are you an investment holding company?

Not every holding company is an "investment holding company" in the tax sense — and the distinction matters a great deal.

An **investment holding company (IHC)** is defined by LHDN as a company whose activities consist mainly of holding investments, where **at least 80% of its gross income** (other than from a business of holding investments) comes from those investments. If your holding company mainly collects dividends, interest and rent, it is likely an IHC.

The consequence, under **section 60F** of the Income Tax Act 1967, is restrictive for an **unlisted** IHC. Because its investment income is treated as a **non-business source**, it *cannot* claim ordinary business deductions. It may only deduct **permitted expenses** — director's fees, salaries, management, secretarial, audit and accounting fees, rent and office costs — and even those are limited by a formula and **capped at 5% of the company's gross dividend, interest and rent income**.

A **Bursa-listed** IHC is treated more generously under **section 60FA**: its investment income is a **business source**, assessed source by source, which allows fuller deductions (subject to its own restrictions). The practical takeaway: if you are building a pure investment holding company, understand that its expenses are largely trapped unless it is listed — this is not the same as an ordinary trading company.

## The tax advantages of a group

Structured well, a group offers genuine tax efficiencies:

**Dividends flow up tax-free.** Malaysia runs a **single-tier system**: dividends paid by a resident company are exempt in the shareholder's hands, and a corporate shareholder can pass exempt single-tier dividends on. So profits distributed from an operating subsidiary up to the holding company are **not taxed again**. (Note a 2025 change: a 2% dividend tax now applies to *resident individuals* on dividend income above RM100,000 — but it does **not** touch inter-company dividends, which remain exempt.)

**Losses can be shared.** Under **group relief** in **section 44A** of the Income Tax Act 1967, a company can surrender **up to 70% of its adjusted loss** for a year to one or more related companies in the same group, letting a profitable subsidiary absorb a loss-making one's losses. The conditions are strict: both companies Malaysian-resident and incorporated, each with paid-up ordinary capital above RM2.5 million, the same 12-month accounting period, and at least 70% common ownership. Companies enjoying certain incentives (such as pioneer status) are excluded.

## The charges when you move the pieces

Restructuring into a group is not free, and two charges catch people out.

**Stamp duty on share transfers.** Moving shares — for example injecting your operating company's shares into a new holding company — attracts **ad valorem stamp duty of 0.3%** (RM3 per RM1,000, or part) on the higher of the consideration or the market value of the shares. Relief may be available for transfers between **associated companies** (broadly, 90%-common ownership), so this can sometimes be mitigated — confirm the current conditions before you act.

**Capital gains tax on unlisted shares.** This is a recent and important change. Since **capital gains tax took effect for companies from 2024**, disposing of **unlisted shares of Malaysian companies** can be taxed — broadly at **10% of the net gain** (with an election to 2% of the gross price for shares acquired before 2024). Related to this, gains on **real property company shares** disposed of by companies moved from the RPGT regime into CGT. The old assumption that "Malaysia doesn't tax capital gains on shares" is no longer true for companies, and it directly affects how and when you reorganise a group.

## What's next

If you are considering a holding-company structure, weigh the benefits — clean ownership, asset ring-fencing, tax-free upstream dividends, loss sharing, a tidier exit — against the real costs: consolidated accounts and audit, the restrictive IHC deduction rules if the top company is a pure investment vehicle, and the stamp duty and capital gains tax triggered by moving shares in.

Sequence it with advice: the *company-law* steps (incorporating the holding company, transferring shares) are the easy part; the *tax* consequences — IHC status, group-relief eligibility, CGT on the reorganisation itself — are where the value is won or lost. Because the CGT and stamp-duty rules changed in 2024 and are still settling, verify the current position with a tax adviser before you restructure.
