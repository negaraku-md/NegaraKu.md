Opening a pharmacy in Malaysia is not like opening a normal shop, and the reason is a single design choice in the law: the licence that lets a pharmacy sell medicines is tied to a *person* — a registered pharmacist — not to a company or a storefront. You can have the capital, the location and the fit-out ready, but until a qualified pharmacist stands behind the licence, you have a health-product shop, not a pharmacy.

Here is how a community (retail) pharmacy is set up legally in Malaysia, and where the line sits between a real pharmacy and an ordinary health retailer.

## Who is actually allowed to run a pharmacy?

The starting point is the person, not the business. Under the **Registration of Pharmacists Act 1951 (Act 371)**, only someone registered with the **Pharmacy Board of Malaysia** may practise pharmacy, and the Board itself exists to keep that register and enforce the Act (*Pharmacy Board Malaysia, Pharmaceutical Services Programme*).

Two things about that pharmacist matter for a pharmacy business:

- **They must be a fully registered pharmacist** — meaning they qualified, completed the required provisional/registration steps, and are on the Board's register.
- **They must hold a valid Annual Retention Certificate.** Registration is not "once and forever": under Act 371 a pharmacist who wants to remain on the register must apply each year to be retained, and the Registrar issues an annual certificate that runs to the end of the year (*Registration of Pharmacists Act 1951*). A lapsed certificate means the pharmacist is no longer in good standing — and that directly undermines the pharmacy's licence.

This is why the pharmacist is the anchor of the whole set-up: the operating licence is issued *to* them.

## What is the licence a pharmacy actually runs on?

The licence that makes a pharmacy a pharmacy is the **Type A Poisons Licence** under **section 26 of the Poisons Act 1952 (Act 366)**.

The Pharmaceutical Services Programme describes the **Type A Licence** as one "issued to a pharmacist to import, store and deal generally all poisons by wholesale and retail or by wholesale only or by retail only" (*Issuance of Poison Licence Type A/B/E*). For a community pharmacy, you are relying on the **retail** scope — it is what legally authorises selling scheduled poisons (prescription and pharmacy-only medicines) over the counter.

Two features follow from this:

- **Only a registered pharmacist can hold it.** A "licensed pharmacist" in the Act's language is a registered pharmacist who holds a Type A Licence. So the licence and the person are inseparable.
- **Retail sale must be personally supervised.** The sale of poisons by retail must be carried out by, or under the **immediate personal supervision** of, the licensed pharmacist. You cannot leave scheduled medicines to be sold by unsupervised staff.

## Who issues the licence, and how do you apply?

Poisons licences are **issued by the Pharmacy Enforcement branches at state level**, under the Pharmaceutical Services Programme (*Issuance of Poison Licence Type A/B/E*). The **Pharmacy Enforcement Division** processes licences covering "the activity of sale, use, import and export of poisons" and **inspects the premises licensed under the Acts** (*Pharmacy Enforcement Division, Pharmaceutical Services Programme*).

In practice:

- New applications, renewals and amendments for the Type A Licence are made **online through the My.Pharma-C system** (mypharmac.pharmacy.gov.my), with the required supporting documents uploaded.
- A **processing fee applies** and is paid through the online system; confirm the current amount on the official portal before applying, as fee schedules change.
- The **State Pharmacy Enforcement Division inspects the premises** before and during the life of the licence.

## What do the premises have to look like?

The licence is for a *specified premises*, so the shop itself is part of what is approved. In broad terms:

| Requirement | What it means in practice |
| --- | --- |
| A proper, fixed pharmacy premises | The licence names the premises; you cannot sell poisons from an unapproved or mobile location. |
| Pharmacist supervision on site | Retail sale of poisons must be under the licensed pharmacist's immediate personal supervision. |
| Proper storage and record-keeping | Poisons must be stored and recorded as the Poisons Act and its regulations require; enforcement officers inspect this. |
| Passes enforcement inspection | The State Pharmacy Enforcement Division inspects premises before and after the licence is granted. |

Because the Pharmacy Enforcement Division sets premises conditions and inspects, treat the fit-out, storage and layout as something to design *around the requirements*, not to retrofit after signing a lease. Check the current premises conditions with your State Pharmacy Enforcement office before committing to a site.

## Do you still register a business with SSM?

Yes. The **licensing** side (pharmacist registration + Type A Licence) is separate from the **business-registration** side. Like any business in Malaysia, the pharmacy's legal entity is registered with the **Companies Commission of Malaysia (SSM)** — as a sole proprietorship/partnership under the business-registration regime, or as a private limited company (Sdn Bhd) (*SSM, Companies Commission of Malaysia*).

So a typical sequence is: register the entity with SSM, secure the premises, then have the registered pharmacist apply for the Type A Poisons Licence tied to that pharmacist and that premises. A non-pharmacist can own or invest in the *entity*, but the *pharmacy operation* still depends on a registered pharmacist holding the licence and supervising the store.

## What can a pharmacy sell that an ordinary shop cannot?

This is the heart of the distinction:

- **A licensed pharmacy (with a Type A Licence)** can sell **scheduled poisons** — the prescription-only and pharmacy-only medicines controlled under the Poisons Act 1952 — alongside general OTC and health products, with poisons sold under the pharmacist's personal supervision.
- **A general health-product retailer (no poisons licence)** may sell only **non-poison, general-sale items** — supplements, toiletries, and OTC products not classified as poisons — and **must not** sell scheduled medicines or present itself as a pharmacy.

The **Sale of Drugs Act 1952** sits alongside the Poisons Act to control the sale and quality of drugs generally, and both are among the reference laws the Pharmaceutical Services Programme points prospective community pharmacists to (*Reference documents for private community pharmacists, FAQ*). Selling scheduled medicines without the licence, or holding out a shop as a pharmacy when it is not, is exactly what the enforcement regime is built to catch.

## What's next

The realistic order of work is: confirm the **pharmacist** (registered, with a current Annual Retention Certificate) who will hold the licence; **register the business** with SSM; line up **premises** that will pass Pharmacy Enforcement inspection; then apply for the **Type A Poisons Licence** through My.Pharma-C at state level. The person and the licence are the non-negotiable core — the entity and the shopfit are the easier part.

Because fees, premises conditions and the exact document checklist are set by the Pharmacy Enforcement Division and can change, verify the current requirements directly with your **State Pharmacy Enforcement office** and the official Pharmaceutical Services Programme portal before you spend on a lease or fit-out. If you are weighing the entity choice underneath the pharmacy, see the related guides on sole proprietorship versus Sdn Bhd and on business licensing in Malaysia.
