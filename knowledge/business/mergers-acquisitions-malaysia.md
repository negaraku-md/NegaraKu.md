Most people picture a merger or acquisition as a single dramatic signing. In practice a Malaysian deal is a sequence of smaller decisions, and the first one — *how* you buy, not whether you buy — quietly shapes everything that follows: your tax bill, the liabilities you inherit, the approvals you need, and how much the lawyers end up charging. Get the structure right and due diligence becomes a pricing exercise; get it wrong and you can buy a lawsuit, a tax liability, or a licence you were never entitled to hold. This guide walks the whole path, from choosing between a share sale and an asset sale to the regulatory touchpoints that decide whether a deal closes cleanly.

Whether you are a founder selling the company you built, an investor rolling up competitors, or an SME owner buying a rival's book of business, the same framework applies. The mechanics differ for a listed company — those follow a separate take-over regime — but the logic of structure, diligence and allocation of risk is constant.

## Share sale or asset sale — the decision that frames everything

There are two fundamental ways to acquire a Malaysian business, and they are not interchangeable.

In a **share sale**, the buyer purchases the shares of the target company from its shareholders. Ownership of the company changes hands, but the company itself — its assets, contracts, employees, licences, bank accounts *and* its liabilities — stays exactly where it is. Nothing inside the company moves; only the names in the register of members change. That makes the transfer clean and continuous, which is why contracts and licences usually survive without needing a counterparty's consent. The catch is total inheritance: the buyer takes on every liability the company carries, including tax arrears, pending litigation and obligations nobody disclosed. This is why a share sale leans so heavily on due diligence, warranties and indemnities.

In an **asset sale** (often called a business sale), the buyer instead purchases selected assets — plant, equipment, stock, intellectual property, goodwill, the customer book — and typically the business as a going concern. Liabilities stay with the seller unless the buyer expressly agrees to assume them, so the buyer can leave the skeletons behind. The price of that clean slate is friction: each asset transfers on its own terms. Real property needs a separate memorandum of transfer, key contracts need to be assigned or novated (which usually means getting the counterparty's consent), licences are often non-transferable and must be re-applied for, and — importantly in Malaysia — employees do **not** transfer automatically. There is no automatic transfer-of-undertakings rule, so staff must be transferred by agreement, or terminated and re-hired, which can raise retrenchment and continuity-of-service questions.

| Dimension | Share sale | Asset (business) sale |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What transfers | The whole company, via its shares | Selected assets + the business as a going concern |
| Liabilities | Buyer inherits all of them (known and unknown) | Stay with seller unless expressly assumed |
| Contracts & licences | Usually continue (watch change-of-control clauses) | Often need consent, novation or re-application |
| Employees | Stay with the company (continuous) | Do not transfer automatically; by agreement or re-hire |
| Transfer mechanics | Single transfer of shares | Many separate transfers, asset by asset |
| Stamp duty | 0.3% on the share transfer instrument | Ad valorem on property; varies by asset |
| Seller's tax | Possible RPGT if a Real Property Company | RPGT on property gains; balancing charges |
| Typically preferred by | Sellers (simpler, cleaner exit) | Buyers (cherry-pick assets, shed liabilities) |

The practical rule of thumb: buyers instinctively prefer asset deals for the clean slate, sellers prefer share deals for the simpler exit and often better tax treatment, and the final structure is a negotiated compromise usually driven by tax, by how many consents an asset deal would require, and by how comfortable the buyer is with the target's liability profile.

## What does the deal process look like?

Most private M&A in Malaysia follows the same arc, and knowing the sequence helps you see where leverage sits and where deals stall.

- **Non-disclosure agreement (NDA).** Before a seller opens its books, the parties sign a confidentiality agreement so commercially sensitive information shared during diligence cannot be used elsewhere. This is the first binding document in almost every deal.
- **Term sheet / letter of intent (LOI).** The parties set out the headline terms — price (or a valuation mechanism), structure, key conditions and a timetable. An LOI is usually **non-binding** on the commercial terms, with a few deliberate exceptions that *are* binding: confidentiality, exclusivity (a "lock-out" period where the seller agrees not to talk to other buyers), and who bears costs.
- **Due diligence.** The buyer investigates the target in depth (see the next section). This is where most surprises surface and where the price and the warranties get renegotiated.
- **Sale and purchase agreement (SPA).** The definitive contract — a share sale agreement (SSA) or a business/asset purchase agreement — is drafted and negotiated. It sets the price and any adjustment, the warranties and indemnities, the **conditions precedent** (things that must happen before completion, such as regulatory approvals or third-party consents), and what each side must do at completion.
- **Completion (closing).** Once the conditions precedent are satisfied, the parties complete: the buyer pays the consideration, the seller delivers the shares or assets, board and shareholder resolutions are passed, directors may resign and be replaced, and the company's registers are updated.
- **Post-completion.** The share transfer is stamped with LHDN, SSM lodgements are made, and integration begins. Some of the price may sit in an escrow or retention account for a period to back the warranties.

A straightforward private deal can run a few weeks to a few months; a complex or regulated one, considerably longer, usually because of due diligence findings or the time needed to obtain approvals and consents.

## What does due diligence actually cover?

Due diligence is the engine of the deal. Its purpose is not to produce a tidy report but to find the liabilities, price the risk, and decide what must be fixed before completion, covered by a specific indemnity, or reflected in the price. It runs on several tracks.

- **Legal due diligence** examines the corporate record (constitution, statutory registers, SSM filings, share capital and ownership chain), material contracts (and crucially their **change-of-control clauses**, which can let a counterparty terminate on a share sale), litigation and disputes, intellectual property, property titles and leases, employment arrangements, and regulatory and licensing compliance.
- **Financial due diligence** tests the numbers behind the price — audited and management accounts, revenue quality, debt and off-balance-sheet obligations, working capital, receivables and their collectability — so the buyer is not paying for earnings that will not recur.
- **Tax due diligence** checks the target's income tax, SST, and withholding-tax position, the status of filings and any arrears or disputes with LHDN, and the transaction's own tax exposure — including stamp duty, RPGT and whether the target is a Real Property Company. Unresolved tax exposure in a share sale becomes the buyer's problem.
- **Operational and commercial due diligence** looks at the things that make the business actually work: customer and supplier concentration, dependence on key staff or founders, systems and assets, and the durability of the goodwill being bought.

The findings feed straight back into the SPA. A clean item becomes a warranty; a known, quantifiable risk becomes a specific indemnity or a price reduction; an unfixable deal-breaker ends the negotiation.

## Which approvals and regulators matter?

Beyond the private contract, several regulatory touchpoints can apply. Not all apply to every deal — the trick is to identify which ones bite *your* transaction early, because consents and approvals sit on the critical path.

**SSM filings and the Companies Act 2016.** A transfer of shares is given effect under the Companies Act 2016: the transfer instrument is executed and stamped, the company updates its register of members, and the change is reflected in SSM records. Changes in directors, company secretary and (where relevant) the constitution are lodged with SSM. In an asset deal, any existing charges over the assets must be dealt with.

**Competition Act 2010 and MyCC.** This is the point most commonly overstated, so state it precisely. As of 2026 Malaysia has **no general, economy-wide merger-control regime** — there is no obligation to notify the Malaysia Competition Commission (MyCC) of an ordinary acquisition, and no economy-wide merger filing. The **Competition (Amendment) Bill 2026**, passed by the Dewan Rakyat and the Senate in July 2026, strengthened MyCC's enforcement powers but **deliberately did not introduce merger control**; the government indicated a merger regime would be introduced later, under the 13th Malaysia Plan (2026-2030). Two caveats remain important. First, the Competition Act 2010's existing prohibitions — on anti-competitive agreements and on abuse of a dominant position — still apply to how the merged business behaves. Second, **sector-specific merger regimes do exist**, notably in aviation, in communications and multimedia, and in energy, so a deal in those sectors may still need clearance. Treat the general "no merger filing" position as the default, but verify the sector.

**Sector and foreign-ownership approvals.** Some industries carry their own equity conditions or licensing gates that a change of ownership can trigger — for example financial services (Bank Negara Malaysia), telecommunications and broadcasting (MCMC), oil and gas services (Petronas licensing), and distributive trade for foreign-owned entities (KPDN's wholesale and retail trade conditions). If the target holds a regulated licence, confirm whether the licence survives a change of control and whether regulator consent is required.

**Listed companies — the Securities Commission and Bursa.** Acquiring a public or listed company is a different exercise. Take-overs of such companies are governed by the Capital Markets and Services Act 2007 and the Securities Commission's **Rules on Take-overs, Mergers and Compulsory Acquisitions**, which impose disclosure duties, conduct standards and — once an acquirer crosses the **33% voting-share threshold** (or acquires more than 2% in any six-month period while holding between 33% and 50%) — an obligation to make a mandatory general offer to the remaining shareholders. Material transactions by a listed company also engage Bursa Malaysia's Listing Requirements. These deals need specialist advice from the outset.

## What stamp duty and tax apply?

Tax often decides the structure, so it belongs in the plan, not the post-mortem.

**Stamp duty.** A transfer of unlisted shares is stamped at **0.3%** of the higher of the consideration or the value of the shares (for unlisted shares, LHDN applies valuation guidelines), and the instrument must be stamped with LHDN — generally within 30 days of execution — before the transfer is registered. In an **asset sale that includes real property**, you instead pay ad valorem stamp duty on the property transfer at the prevailing graduated rates, which can be materially higher than 0.3% — a key reason property-heavy businesses are often sold as share deals.

**Real property gains tax (RPGT).** Malaysia taxes gains on the disposal of real property under the RPGT regime, at rates that depend on the holding period and the type of disposer. Critically, RPGT also reaches the disposal of shares in a **Real Property Company (RPC)** — broadly, a company whose assets are substantially real property or RPC shares — so a share sale is **not** automatically free of RPGT. If the target owns significant Malaysian property, model the RPGT exposure before fixing the structure.

**Other tax considerations.** An asset sale can trigger balancing charges on depreciated assets and SST consequences depending on what is sold, and the structure affects whether tax attributes (such as carried-forward losses) survive. Because stamp-duty and RPGT rules are revised regularly — often at Budget — confirm the current rates and reliefs with LHDN or a tax adviser before relying on specific figures.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating a share sale as a clean purchase.** It is the opposite: you inherit every liability, including the undisclosed ones. Thin due diligence plus weak warranties is how buyers end up litigating after completion.
- **Underestimating an asset deal's friction.** Buyers choose asset deals for the clean slate, then discover that key contracts need counterparty consent, licences are non-transferable, and employees do not transfer automatically — raising retrenchment and continuity issues nobody budgeted for.
- **Assuming "no MyCC filing" means "no approvals".** It is true there is no general merger filing as of 2026, but sector regulators (aviation, communications, energy, finance) and foreign-equity conditions can still gate the deal, and listed targets trigger the SC take-over rules.
- **Overlooking the RPC trap.** A share sale of a property-rich company can still attract RPGT. Buyers who assume share deals are tax-free on the seller's side misprice the transaction.
- **Ignoring change-of-control clauses.** A share sale usually preserves contracts — unless a material contract or licence lets the counterparty terminate on a change of control. Diligence should flag these early.
- **A weak SPA.** Vague warranties, no indemnities for known risks, soft conditions precedent, and no escrow or retention leave the buyer exposed and the seller over-exposed. The SPA is where diligence findings are turned into enforceable protection.
- **Missing the stamping deadline.** An unstamped share transfer cannot be registered; stamp it within the required window to keep completion valid.

## What's next

Decide the structure first, because tax, liabilities and the approval list all flow from it. If the target is property-light and its contracts and licences are portable, a share sale is often the cleaner route — but commit to real due diligence and a properly drafted [sale and purchase agreement with robust warranties and indemnities](/en/business/contracts-act-malaysia), because you will inherit everything. If you want to leave liabilities behind or the target is property-heavy, model an asset sale and map every consent, [share-transfer](/en/company-secretary/share-transfer) formality and tax consequence before you sign. Check early whether the deal sits in a regulated sector or crosses a listed-company threshold, keep the general [Competition Act 2010](/en/business/competition-act-malaysia) position (no economy-wide merger filing yet) in view alongside the sector regimes, and budget the stamp duty and RPGT into the price rather than discovering them at completion. And if the deal is ultimately a wind-down rather than a sale, the mechanics of [closing a company](/en/business/close-a-company) are a separate path worth planning for.
