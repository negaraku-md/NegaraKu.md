Few three-letter abbreviations carry as much weight in Malaysia's car market as "AP." It is the reason an imported car can cost far more here than abroad, the subject of decades of political argument, and a permit most buyers never see but always pay for.

The Approved Permit is, at its core, an import licence. But around that simple idea sits a policy with real economic and social history — which is why it is worth understanding beyond the headline.

## What is an Approved Permit?

An AP is a permit that allows a controlled good to be imported into Malaysia. The legal backbone is the **Customs Act 1967** and the **Customs (Prohibition of Imports) Order**, which list goods that may not be imported without approval. Many categories of goods need one; motor vehicles are simply the most visible and most debated.

For vehicles, the issuing authority is the **Ministry of Investment, Trade and Industry (MITI)**. Applications run through the electronic **ePermit** system, and the permit is cross-checked against the importer's Customs declaration before the vehicle is released. No valid AP, no clearance.

## The three types of AP

Not all APs are the same. They serve different importers and different vehicles.

| Type | For whom | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Franchise AP** | Registered brand franchise holders / official distributors | Importing **new** vehicles of the franchised brand |
| **Open AP** | Independent ("parallel") importers | Importing **used / reconditioned** vehicles of any brand, subject to conditions |
| **Private AP** | Individuals | Personal vehicle, e.g. Malaysians returning from working or studying abroad |

Franchise APs are generally issued to distributors at no charge. Open APs, by contrast, are tied to eligibility conditions and a levy, and it is the Open AP that sits at the centre of most public debate.

## Why does the system exist?

The AP system is not only a border-control tool; for vehicles it has also functioned as an economic-participation policy.

Open APs were used to broaden **Bumiputera** participation in the motor-vehicle import trade, a sector that had historically been dominated by other communities. Eligibility for Open APs has accordingly been tied to Bumiputera ownership and management conditions, alongside financial-standing and experience requirements. Supporters frame this as a legitimate affirmative measure; an association of Malay importers, PEKEMA, has argued that Open APs fall within **Article 153** of the Federal Constitution, which concerns the special position of the Bumiputera. That is an advocacy position rather than a settled legal ruling, and it is contested.

This article describes the policy and the debate around it; it does not take a side on them.

## How Open APs are allocated and what they cost

Open APs come with strings attached. Reported conditions have included **100% Bumiputera ownership** and key management, a sound financial position, and prior experience in motor-vehicle sales and distribution. Reconditioned vehicles within a defined age window (reported as one to five years from first registration) have been eligible for import.

The permit itself carries a cost: Open APs are widely reported to attract a **levy of RM10,000 per permit**. The share of APs reserved for Bumiputera/Open holders versus franchise holders has been reported differently by different sources over the years, which is one reason transparency has been a recurring demand.

## What has the controversy been about?

Two themes recur: **concentration** and **transparency**.

In **2005**, the issuance of APs became a national controversy when a small number of holders were reported to control a large share of permits — one businessman was widely labelled the "AP King." Critics argued that permits were sometimes resold at a profit rather than used to build genuine import businesses, turning a participation policy into a tradable asset.

Successive governments responded with tighter criteria and transparency pledges. In **2015** the Open AP policy was revised and holders were required to reapply; in **2019** the government set stricter eligibility rules, including the 100% Bumiputera-ownership requirement verified against company registration. The **National Automotive Policy 2020** kept the Open AP system in place despite the criticism.

## Is the AP system being abolished?

Periodically, yes in proposal — but not in practice, so far.

Abolishing Open APs has been floated more than once, and dates have been mentioned and then passed. The most recent clear government position came in **July 2023**, when the Minister of Investment, Trade and Industry said there was **no intention to phase out** the AP system for car imports. Around the same time, newer direct-import and distribution arrangements for some manufacturers drew attention to how the system adapts to new entrants rather than disappearing.

Because the policy has been revised so often, anyone making a business or purchase decision should confirm the current rules directly with MITI.

## What's next

Watch three things: whether the Open AP framework is restructured or given a fresh end-date; how direct-import and new-energy-vehicle arrangements reshape the role of franchise and open importers; and whether the long-standing calls for a published, transparent allocation of APs are met. The AP is a small piece of paper that continues to shape a large part of how Malaysians buy cars.
