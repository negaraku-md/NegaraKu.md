The worst moment in a scam is the one just after you realise it was a scam — when the money has left your account and you do not know what to do, or whom to call. In Malaysia, there is a single answer to that question: **dial 997.** That number reaches the **National Scam Response Centre (NSRC)**, and calling it quickly is, quite literally, your best chance of getting your money back.

This guide explains what the NSRC is, what happens when you call, and why the clock is the most important part of the story.

## What the NSRC is

The National Scam Response Centre is a Malaysian government **rapid-response centre for financial scams**, launched in **October 2022**. Its defining idea is co-location: instead of a victim having to separately contact the police, their bank and the regulators — losing precious time at each step — the NSRC puts them in one place.

It is run by the **police**, working with **Bank Negara Malaysia (BNM)**, the **Malaysian Communications and Multimedia Commission (MCMC)**, and representatives from **commercial banks and telecommunications companies**. That mix is deliberate: stopping a scam in progress needs the police to act, the banks to freeze accounts, and the telcos to help trace communications — all at once.

## What happens when you call 997

When you dial 997 to report a scam, the process is built for speed:

1. **Report.** A police officer on duty answers, lodges a report, and collects your details — including the **bank account the money was sent to**.
2. **Alert.** The officer alerts the relevant financial institutions, whose representatives are at the centre.
3. **Freeze.** The first priority is to **temporarily freeze the receiving account** — the "mule" account the scammer used — before the money can be moved on.
4. **Investigate and return.** The transaction is then investigated, with recovered funds returned to victims where possible.

Behind the scenes, a **National Fraud Portal** helps trace funds and detect mule accounts using data integration and analytics, and the centre has moved toward **24/7 operation** to shorten response times further.

## Why speed is everything

The single most important thing to understand about the NSRC is that **its power depends on the money still being there.** Scammers move stolen funds through chains of mule accounts within hours. A freeze only works if it reaches the receiving account before the next transfer.

That is why officials repeatedly urge victims to **call within 24 hours** of realising they have been scammed. A report made in the first hour has a far better chance of recovering funds than one made days later. If you think you have been scammed, the right order of actions is simple: **call 997 first**, then preserve your evidence (screenshots, transaction records, phone numbers) and make a formal police report.

## How it fits with the rest of the system

The 997 call is the *emergency* response — the fast freeze-and-trace. It does not replace the broader criminal investigation, which is handled by the police, including the **Commercial Crime Investigation Department (CCID)**; see the [CCID guide](/en/public-safety/commercial-crime-investigation-department-ccid/) for how commercial-crime cases are investigated. For practical prevention — recognising scams before they happen — see [staying safe from scams and emergencies](/en/money-daily-life/staying-safe-scams-and-emergencies/).

## Common misconceptions

- **"There's nothing I can do once the money's gone."** Often untrue in the first hours — a fast 997 call can lead to the receiving account being frozen before the money moves on.
- **"I should call my bank first, then the police, then the regulator."** The NSRC exists precisely so you don't have to: one call to 997 reaches them together.
- **"997 replaces a police report."** It starts the rapid response; you should still make a formal police report for the investigation.
- **"It's only for online banking scams."** It handles financial scams broadly — investment scams, phone scams, e-commerce fraud and more.
