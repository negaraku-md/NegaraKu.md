Fire gives no warning. In a matter of minutes, a spark from an overloaded socket or an abandoned frying pan can level a house — and in 2025 alone, the Fire and Rescue Department of Malaysia (JBPM) recorded 16,111 fire cases with 98 deaths and estimated property losses of RM2.09 billion. The good news: almost all of them are preventable, whether in your living room or on the factory floor.

This guide brings together two things that are usually discussed separately — home fire-prevention practices, and compliance obligations (specifically the Fire Certificate) for buildings and businesses.

## What most often burns down homes in Malaysia?

According to JBPM figures for 2025, the leading cause of fires was open burning of rubbish (34.4 percent), followed by cigarettes and smoking activity (25 percent) and electrical sources (18.6 percent). For residential fires specifically, electrical-related problems are consistently the main cause.

Three patterns recur at home:

- **Electrical** — old or faulty wiring, overloaded sockets and extension boards, and running several high-powered appliances at once from a single supply point.
- **Kitchen** — cooking left unattended, hot oil catching fire, and LPG gas leaks.
- **Negligence** — cigarette butts, candles, and flammable items placed too close to a heat source.

The key point: these causes are not bad luck, but maintenance failures and habits that can be corrected.

## How do you protect your home from fire?

Prevention at home does not require expensive equipment — it requires discipline. Focus on three layers: avoid triggers, detect early, and be ready to get out.

**Reduce electrical risk.** Use only certified electrical products (look for the SIRIM mark). Never bridge a blown fuse directly or modify electrical safety systems — they are designed to cut off the supply during a short circuit. Replace worn cables promptly and avoid plugging too many high-powered appliances into a single socket.

**Control the kitchen.** Do not leave cooking unattended. Close the LPG cylinder valve after cooking, check hoses and clamps from time to time, and store cylinders in a well-ventilated space. If an oil fire breaks out, do not throw water on it — cover it with a lid or a damp cloth.

**Install detectors and extinguishers.** Smoke detectors give the most valuable early warning — those few extra seconds are what save lives. Place them on every floor, including near bedrooms, and test them regularly. Keep at least one portable multi-purpose (ABC) fire extinguisher and make sure every family member knows how to use it.

**Plan your escape routes.** Ensure there are two ways out of every room where possible, and that all occupants know them and have practised using them. During a fire, get out immediately and call 999 — do not shelter in a bathroom or room hoping to wait it out; enclosed spaces can be overwhelmed by smoke.

## Which buildings are required to have a Fire Certificate?

Here the focus shifts from habits to the law. Under the **Fire Services Act 1988 (Act 341)**, certain buildings are classified as *designated premises*. Section 27 grants the power to declare categories of premises based on their use, size or location, while **Section 28** requires every designated premises to have a valid Fire Certificate.

The categories of designated premises are set out in the *Fire Services (Designated Premises) (Amendment) Order 2020*, with specific size or height thresholds. For example:

| Type of premises | General threshold that makes it "designated premises" |
|---|---|
| Hospitals & nursing homes | 2,000 m² and above or 5 storeys and above |
| Hotels | Depends on design (e.g. 100+ rooms, or 51–101+ rooms depending on number of storeys) |
| Hostels & dormitories | 6–10+ storeys or 3,000–5,000+ m² |
| Offices | Exceeding 30 m in height or 10,000+ m² |
| Shops | 3,000+ m² |
| Factories | 2,000+ m² (with automatic sprinkler system) |
| Places of assembly | 1,000–2,000+ m² |
| Libraries | 2,000+ m² |
| Storage & underground car parks | 1,000–7,000+ m²/m³ |

By contrast, **private residential homes and premises for public worship are exempt** under Section 28(3). However, the Director General may still require an inspection if a fire hazard exists.

In short: if you live in an ordinary house, you do not need a Fire Certificate — but if you operate a hotel, factory, large office or place of assembly, you most likely do.

## How is a Fire Certificate obtained and renewed?

The Fire Certificate is issued by JBPM (BOMBA) through the State Headquarters or Zone Office. It confirms that the building's fire-protection systems — escape routes, alarm systems, sprinklers, hydrants, and passive safety measures — are functional and comply with requirements.

Key things to remember:

- **Valid for one year** from the date of issue.
- **The renewal application** must be submitted **not less than 30 days** before the expiry date, using the prescribed form.
- **The renewal fee** is usually half the cost of the original certificate; late applications may incur a penalty of around RM100.
- Maintenance of fire safety systems should be ongoing throughout the year, not only before an inspection.

## What are the risks of non-compliance?

This is not mere bureaucracy — the absence of a Fire Certificate is a criminal offence. Under **Section 33** of the Fire Services Act 1988, if there is no Fire Certificate in force for any designated premises, the owner of the premises may be convicted of an offence and subject to:

- **a fine of up to RM50,000**, or
- **imprisonment of up to 5 years**, or
- **both**, and
- **an additional fine of RM100 for each day** the offence continues after conviction.

Beyond the penalties, the real cost of non-compliance is lives and liability. A building without a functioning fire safety system puts occupants, workers and customers in danger — and the owner is exposed to civil claims should a tragedy occur.

## What to do next

For your home, start this week: check sockets and extension cords, test smoke detectors, turn off the gas after cooking, and discuss an escape plan with your family. Keep the emergency number **999** somewhere easily visible.

For a building or business, check whether your premises falls under *designated premises* in the Fire Services (Designated Premises) (Amendment) Order 2020. If so, confirm the current Fire Certificate status and mark the renewal date — remember that 30-day limit. Consult your State JBPM Headquarters for the latest forms and inspection requirements, and consider a certified fire safety consultant for complex buildings.
