In most gated residential areas, factories, and shopping malls in Malaysia, the first person you meet is not a police officer — but a private security guard. This industry operates closely alongside public law enforcement, yet runs on an entirely different legal framework, and confusion about who can do what can expose the businesses that hire them to legal risk.

## Who regulates the private security industry?

Malaysia's private security industry is regulated by the **Ministry of Home Affairs (KDN)** under the **Private Agencies Act 1971 (Act 27)**. No company may offer commercial security services without holding a valid **Private Agency Licence**.

The day-to-day administration of the industry is now channelled through **eSIMS — the Electronic Security Industry Management System** (KDN calls it the "Security Industry Management System"), KDN's digital platform under the Public Safety and Order Division. Through eSIMS, companies apply for and renew licences, register local and foreign guards, request security screening for new employees, and track training compliance.

The Private Agencies Act 1971 also covers private investigator agencies, but the focus of this guide is security agencies and the guards they supply.

## What are the requirements to obtain and maintain a licence?

Licences are issued under **Section 3** of Act 27. Beyond company eligibility, KDN sets out a set of **operating and guard-recruitment conditions** that must be complied with on an ongoing basis — not merely at the point of first application. The specific recruitment conditions (citizenship, composition, physical standards, age limits, screening, and training) are listed in the KDN document *Syarat-Syarat Lesen Di Bawah Seksyen 3 Akta No. 27/71*.

Among the guard-recruitment conditions set by KDN under Section 3:

- **Citizenship** — only Malaysian citizens are permitted to serve as guards, except former Nepalese soldiers under an approved foreign-worker quota.
- **Composition** — one-fifth (1/5) of an agency's guards are required to consist of former Police and former Military personnel.
- **Physical standards** — male guards must be at least 1.57 metres tall and 47.6 kg; female guards at least 1.52 metres and 46.2 kg.
- **Age limit** — recruitment of guards older than 60 is not permitted, and candidates aged 56 and above require a health certification from a doctor.
- **Screening and health** — every guard must be certified fit by a doctor, and undergo a urine test within 7 (working) days of the date of appointment.
- **Training** — in-house training of no less than one week before starting duty, and the KDN-prescribed basic security guard course must be completed within 3 months of recruitment.

Police security-screening requirements and training quotas are tracked electronically in eSIMS, including the categories of trained local guards and trained Nepalese guards. Failure to meet the conditions or to renew the licence can result in a renewal application being rejected under Section 3.

## What is the difference between a security guard and auxiliary police?

This is the most important distinction, and it is often misunderstood. An **ordinary security guard has no special enforcement powers** — their authority is limited to that of any ordinary citizen (such as detaining someone committing a seizable offence and handing them over to the police).

**Auxiliary police**, on the other hand, are a different entity. They are appointed under **Sections 47 to 50 of the Police Act 1967 (Act 344)** and the **Police (Auxiliary Police) Regulations 1970**. Within a designated area, auxiliary police officers hold powers, duties, and legal protections **equivalent to a Royal Malaysia Police officer of equivalent rank**, with powers arising from the Penal Code, the Criminal Procedure Code, the Police Act 1967, and several other laws.

| Aspect | Private security guard | Auxiliary police |
|---|---|---|
| Law | Private Agencies Act 1971 (Act 27) | Police Act 1967 (Act 344), Sections 47-50 |
| Authority | Licensed by KDN (agency) | Appointed with police approval |
| Powers | Ordinary-citizen powers only | Police-equivalent powers within a designated area |
| Area scope | Client's premises | Employer's designated area |
| Typical employers | Residents' associations, factories, complexes | Universities (e.g. IIUM); also common in large organisations such as banks, airports, and statutory bodies |

Because of the higher cost and legal responsibility, auxiliary police are typically used by large organisations with high-risk assets — not every residential area.

## What does this mean for businesses and residential communities?

For gated-community residents' associations, building managers, and business owners, one simple principle applies: **hiring an unlicensed agency is a legal and operational risk**.

Some practical steps:

- **Verify the licence first.** Ask for the Private Agency Licence number and check the status of the company and its guards through KDN's eSIMS verification portal before signing a contract.
- **Check recruitment compliance.** A law-abiding agency uses citizen guards (or approved quota Nepalese guards), complete with screening and the basic course.
- **Understand the limits of their powers.** A guard at the gate cannot arbitrarily detain anyone or search vehicles without consent; such powers exist only for police or auxiliary police within a designated area.
- **Check training and certification.** Beyond KDN's minimum requirements, ask whether guards have completed the prescribed basic course and any industry competency certification as an additional quality marker.

For gated-and-guarded schemes, guards hired by residents' associations remain private guards under Act 27 — they do not acquire police powers simply because they are stationed at the entrance to a housing estate.

## Next steps

If you are setting up or renewing an agency, start at KDN's eSIMS and ensure the Section 3 conditions are met before submitting your application. If you are hiring, obtain and verify the agency's licence number through KDN's verification portal, and ask for proof of guard-recruitment compliance. For organisations considering auxiliary police, refer directly to the police, as such appointments involve a separate process under the Police Act 1967, not a private agency licence.
