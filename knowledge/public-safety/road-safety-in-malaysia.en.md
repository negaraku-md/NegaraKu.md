Every day, around 18 lives are lost on Malaysia's roads. That figure makes the roads one of the country's most significant causes of unnatural death, and the main reason laws, cameras and dedicated agencies are deployed to tackle it.

## How many people die on Malaysia's roads?

The scale of the problem is large. Throughout 2023, Bukit Aman recorded **598,635 road accidents** across the country. The Transport Minister confirmed that **6,443 deaths** were recorded for the year — averaging close to 18 deaths a day.

That burden is not shared equally between states. Selangor — the state with the most vehicles — recorded the highest number of accidents and deaths, followed by Johor.

| State | Total accidents (2023) |
| --- | --- |
| Selangor | 173,129 |
| Johor | 87,370 |
| Kuala Lumpur | 72,701 |

*Source: Bukit Aman (Traffic Investigation and Enforcement Department), as reported by Malay Mail. Selangor also recorded the highest number of deaths.*

The most vulnerable are motorcyclists. For 2023, motorcyclists accounted for **around two-thirds** of all road deaths (about 4,448 fatalities). This is why much of Malaysia's safety policy focuses specifically on two-wheeled riders.

## What are the basic laws: seat belts, helmets and drink-driving?

The backbone of road safety law is the **Road Transport Act 1987**. Under it:

- **Seat belts** must be worn by drivers and passengers.
- **Helmets** must be worn by motorcycle riders and pillion passengers.
- **Driving under the influence** is a criminal offence.

All of these are also on the list of offences that carry demerit points under the KEJARA system (discussed below).

Drink-driving laws were significantly tightened through the **Road Transport (Amendment) Act 2020**, which took effect on **23 October 2020**. The amendment lowered the permitted alcohol limit and sharply raised the penalties.

The amended alcohol limits:

- **Blood:** down from 80mg to **50mg** per 100ml.
- **Breath:** down to **22 micrograms** per 100ml.
- **Urine:** down to **67mg** per 100ml.

Key penalties under the amended act (figures to be verified against the gazetted text of the Act):

| Offence | Reference | Penalty (first offence) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Driving while unable to control the vehicle | Section 45 | Fine RM1,000–RM5,000; imprisonment up to 2 years; licence disqualification minimum 2 years |
| Causing **injury** by driving under the influence | Section 44 | Fine RM30,000–RM50,000; imprisonment 7–10 years |
| Causing **death** by driving under the influence | Section 44 | Fine RM50,000–RM100,000; imprisonment up to 15 years; disqualification minimum 10 years |

*Source: Road Transport (Amendment) Act 2020, as reported by paultan.org. Exact figures to be verified against the gazetted text of the Act.*

For the offence of causing death, a repeat offence can carry even heavier penalties.

## How do AES cameras enforce speed limits?

People cannot be everywhere dangerous at all times — so Malaysia turned to automatic enforcement. The **Automated Enforcement System (AES)** began operating in **September 2012**, starting with a small number of cameras at high-risk locations. The original plan at launch was to roll out up to **831 camera locations** across the country in phases (the current operational number is to be verified).

AES automatically catches two main offences:

1. **Driving over the speed limit** set at the location.
2. **Running red lights** at junctions.

The system was later rebranded as **AwAS (Automated Awareness Safety System)** and placed under JPJ management. Offences captured by AwAS do not just carry a compound — they also generate KEJARA demerit points, making the cameras part of a wider enforcement chain.

## How does the KEJARA demerit points system work?

Fines alone are easily forgotten; losing your licence is not. That is the logic behind **KEJARA**, the JPJ demerit points system that took effect on **15 April 2017** under the gazetted *Motor Vehicles (Demerit Points) Rules 2017*.

How it works:

- **Points are only counted when** an offender pays a compound or fine for a listed offence — not merely on receiving a summons.
- Accumulating **20 points** can trigger licence suspension.
- The suspension period increases each time: **3–6 months** (first time), 6–8 months (second), 8–10 months (third), and 10–12 months (fourth and subsequent).
- **The licence is revoked** if suspended three times within a five-year period. After that, the offender must reapply for the licence from scratch.

Since 2020, **drink-driving offences have been removed** from the KEJARA system because such cases are now tried directly in court rather than settled through a compound.

## Who drives the national road safety policy?

Two main bodies lead this effort, both under the Ministry of Transport:

- **MIROS (Malaysian Institute of Road Safety Research)** — established in **2007** as the knowledge and research hub for road safety. MIROS conducts accident analysis and road safety audits to shape evidence-based intervention programmes. In 2014, it was appointed as the **ASEAN Road Safety Centre**.
- **JPJ Road Safety Division** (formerly known as JKJR) — leads advocacy, education and awareness campaigns, and coordinates efforts with NGOs and the corporate sector.

Both agencies support the **Malaysia Road Safety Plan 2022–2030 (PKJRM)**, aligned with the UN Decade of Action for Road Safety 2021–2030. Its main target is clear: **to reduce road deaths by 50 percent by 2030**, with the ultimate goal of zero deaths.

## What's next

For road users, three actions reduce risk most tangibly: wear a seat belt or helmet every time, never drive after drinking, and obey the displayed speed limits — especially at AES camera locations.

If you receive an AES summons or traffic compound, check your KEJARA demerit points status through JPJ's official portal or app (MyJPJ) before paying, because it is that payment which triggers the counting of points. For the latest statistics and camera locations, refer to the official websites of the Ministry of Transport, MIROS and JPJ.
