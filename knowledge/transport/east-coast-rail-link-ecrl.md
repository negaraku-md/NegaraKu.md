For decades the fastest way between Kuala Lumpur and the east-coast states meant a long haul over the mountains by road. The East Coast Rail Link is Malaysia's bet that a single line of track can change that — and, in the process, rebalance where the country's growth happens.

It is also one of the most scrutinised infrastructure projects in the country's history: a China-built, China-financed railway that was signed, suspended, renegotiated and revived, all while the numbers kept moving.

## What exactly is the ECRL?

The East Coast Rail Link is a **roughly 665 km standard-gauge railway** running from **Kota Bharu** in Kelantan, down the coast through **Terengganu** and **Pahang**, and then across the peninsula to **Gombak** and **Port Klang** in the Klang Valley. It carries **both passengers and freight** and is served by around **20 stations** along the corridor.

Its owner is **Malaysia Rail Link Sdn Bhd (MRL)**, a company wholly owned by the **Minister of Finance Incorporated**. The builder is **China Communications Construction Company (CCCC)**, acting as the engineering, procurement, construction and commissioning (EPCC) contractor. When the line opens, it is to be run by a **50:50 joint venture** between MRL and CCCC.

The trains are designed for a maximum of about 200 km/h, with service speeds of **160 km/h for passenger** services and **80 km/h for freight** — turning a journey that takes most of a day by road into a few hours by rail.

## The route and its phases

The alignment was originally organised into two phases:

| Phase | Stretch | Approximate length |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Phase 1 | Kota Bharu to Gombak | ~600 km |
| Phase 2 | Gombak to Port Klang | ~88 km |

The current **665 km** figure reflects a **2021 realignment**: the southern Section C was rerouted to run via Negeri Sembilan on its way to Port Klang, confirmed on 2 December 2021, which added roughly 25 km to the line.

The practical effect is a line that threads the three east-coast states together and then punches through the **Titiwangsa mountain range** — including long tunnels — to reach the industrial and port heartland of the west coast.

## How it is funded

The ECRL is the flagship Malaysian project under China's Belt and Road Initiative, and its financing structure is central to the whole story.

According to reporting and development-finance trackers, about **85% of the project cost** is covered by a **soft loan from China Exim Bank**, with the remainder raised locally. The Phase 1 loan has been described as a preferential buyer's credit of roughly **RMB61.88 billion (about RM39.1 billion)** at an interest rate of around 3.25%, with a long maturity and grace period. Those exact terms are flagged for verification below, and should be read against the official loan agreement.

## The cost: a figure that kept moving

The headline cost has changed more than once, which is why different sources quote different numbers.

- The **original 2016 deal** put the construction cost at about **RM65.5 billion**.
- After the 2018 suspension and a renegotiation, a **supplementary agreement signed on 12 April 2019** cut the construction cost to **RM44 billion** — a reduction of about RM21.5 billion — partly through value engineering and a revised alignment, according to the New Straits Times.
- The **2021 Section C realignment** then lifted the construction cost to **RM50.27 billion**, the figure the Ministry of Transport now cites, with a further **RM24.69 billion in development costs** on top.

So the same project can be described as a RM44 billion line or a RM50-billion-plus line depending on which version and which cost base is meant — a distinction worth keeping in mind when comparing headlines.

## Signed, suspended, revived

The project's timeline is as much political as it is technical:

- **November 2016** — a framework agreement is signed and the ECRL is launched.
- **9 August 2017** — groundbreaking; construction begins.
- **3 July 2018** — MRL instructs CCCC to **suspend all work** while the newly elected government reviews the cost and terms.
- **12 April 2019** — a **supplementary agreement** is signed, reducing the construction cost to RM44 billion, raising local participation in civil works, providing for a refund of part of an advance payment, and establishing the joint-venture operating company. Work resumes.
- **2 December 2021** — the southern **Section C realignment** is confirmed, settling the route into the Klang Valley.
- **2025-2026** — physical progress is reported above 90%, with the Kota Bharu-Gombak stretch targeted for completion by the **end of 2026** and commercial operations expected from **2027** (some official references cite January 2028 for the full line to Port Klang).

Because these dates have slipped before, the current completion and operations timeline is exactly the kind of detail to confirm with the Ministry of Transport or MRL rather than assume.

## More than a passenger line

What makes the ECRL strategically distinctive is its **freight role**. The line is designed to act as a **land bridge** across the peninsula, carrying containers between **Port Klang** on the west coast and the **east-coast ports** such as Kuantan. In principle, cargo can cross Peninsular Malaysia by rail instead of sailing the long way around Singapore and through the Strait of Malacca.

That dual purpose ties the railway directly to Malaysia's wider logistics and port ambitions — the reason it is often discussed alongside the country's seaports rather than purely as a commuter service.

## The corridor case: why the east coast

The deeper rationale is economic geography. The three states the line serves sit within the **East Coast Economic Region (ECER)**, which has historically lagged the industrialised west coast in income and investment.

The ECRL's backers argue it will:

- **Cut travel time** between the east coast and the Klang Valley from a long road trip to a few hours.
- **Open up transit-oriented development** and industrial parks around stations.
- **Create construction and operating jobs**, with a stated push for **local participation in civil works** (reported as rising to around 40%) and **technology transfer** to Malaysian engineers and vendors.

Critics counter that the debt load and the dependence on a single foreign contractor and lender carry real risks, and that the promised corridor effects will take years to prove. Both the opportunity and the risk are genuine — which is why the project remains closely watched.

## Common misreadings

- **Treating the ECRL as finished.** As of 2025-2026 it was still under construction; opening dates are targets, not facts.
- **Quoting one cost as the cost.** RM44 billion, RM50.27 billion and the larger development-inclusive totals describe different versions and cost bases of the same project.
- **Seeing it as a passenger train only.** Its freight and land-bridge role is central to the business case.
- **Confusing it with the KL-Singapore High-Speed Rail.** They are entirely separate projects, on opposite sides of the peninsula and at very different stages.

## What's next

The signals to watch are concrete: the Ministry of Transport and MRL confirming the final completion and operations dates, the published fare structure, and the first revenue freight and passenger services. Until trains are running commercially, treat the opening timeline and the economic-corridor benefits as projections to verify rather than settled outcomes.
