For most of Malaysian Borneo's history, getting from one town to the next meant a slow crawl along a single-lane trunk road, shared with lorries, logging traffic and the occasional washed-out bridge. The Pan Borneo Highway is the attempt to change that: a decade-long effort to turn that trunk road into a modern, mostly dual-carriageway link running the length of Sarawak and Sabah.

It is also a project of two very different stories. The Sarawak section is close to done; the Sabah section, after ten years, is still mostly unbuilt. Understanding the highway means holding both halves in view at once.

## What exactly is the Pan Borneo Highway?

The Pan Borneo Highway is a programme to **upgrade the main road across Malaysian Borneo into a mostly dual carriageway** — a divided highway with two lanes in each direction for most of its length, replacing long single-carriageway stretches. It runs through both **Sarawak** and **Sabah**, and carries the Asian Highway route number **AH150**, which continues through **Brunei** on its own alignment.

The combined Malaysian route is **more than 2,000 km**. Its south-western end is at **Telok Melano** in Sarawak, near the Indonesian border, and its north-eastern end is at **Serudong** in Sabah, near the Indonesian Kalimantan border on the other side of the island. In between, it threads together the coastal cities, interior towns and ports of both states.

The highway is a **federal government project**, delivered through the **Public Works Department (Jabatan Kerja Raya, JKR)** and the **Ministry of Works**. It is not a tolled concession in the way some Peninsular Malaysian expressways are; it is publicly funded road infrastructure.

## The route, phases and lengths

The two state sections are planned and built separately, which is part of why their progress diverges so sharply.

| Section | Main stretch | Approximate length | Work packages |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sarawak (Phase 1) | Telok Melano to Miri | ~1,060-1,077 km | 11 (WPC01-WPC11) |
| Sabah (Phase 1) | Sipitang / Sindumin to Tawau area | ~706 km | 35 |
| Brunei (AH150) | Through Brunei Darussalam | ~168 km | separate (Brunei) |

Sabah's plan extends well beyond Phase 1, with later phases (such as Kudat-Semporna, Ranau-Tamparuli and others) still largely at the tender stage. An older and still widely quoted framing put the **whole highway at about 1,663 km** — roughly 936 km in Sarawak and 727 km in Sabah — at a total construction cost of around **RM27 billion**. Because the alignment and phase definitions have shifted over the years, length figures differ between sources, which is why the precise numbers are flagged for verification below.

## How it is delivered and funded

Both state sections began under a **Project Delivery Partner (PDP) model**, the same arrangement used for some major Klang Valley rail projects, in which a lead company manages design and construction across many packages.

- In **Sarawak**, the PDP and turnkey role was held by **Lebuhraya Borneo Utara Sdn Bhd (LBU)**.
- In **Sabah**, it was held by **Borneo Highway PDP Sdn Bhd (BHP)**, a joint venture involving UEM, MMC and Warisan Tarang Construction.

Both PDP arrangements were **terminated around 2019 to 2020**, after which **JKR took over management** and the works continued through more conventional contracts. In Sabah, that transition was disruptive: work was interrupted before restarting around September 2020, and the change in contract model is one reason cost figures for the Sabah section have been restated more than once.

Funding is federal. Under **Budget 2026**, **RM1.67 billion** was allocated to continue the Pan Borneo Highway, part of a wider package of road spending for Sabah and Sarawak.

## The cost: figures that do not line up

There is no single clean cost number for the Pan Borneo Highway, and anyone comparing headlines should be careful. The figures differ by **state, phase, date and contract model**, and several have risen over time:

- An early overall estimate put the whole highway at around **RM27 billion** for about 1,663 km.
- The **Sarawak** section has been reported at roughly **RM16 billion**.
- For **Sabah Phase 1**, reported figures range from an early **RM12.86 billion** estimate, to around **RM17.91 to RM18.96 billion** in the Auditor-General's reporting, to about **RM24.89 billion** cited by the Works Ministry in July 2026 after the switch to a federal conventional-contractor model.

These numbers describe different things and are **not directly comparable**. A restated Sabah cost does not mean the same scope simply became more expensive; it often reflects a changed contract model, added land-acquisition costs, or an extended implementation period. Treat any single figure with caution and check it against the latest official statement.

## Progress: a tale of two states

As of the **Auditor-General's Report 2/2026**, covering the position on **31 March 2026**:

- **Sarawak** was nearly complete: of its 11 work packages, **10 were reported at 100 per cent** and the eleventh at around 99.9 per cent. Most of the Sarawak section is open to traffic.
- **Sabah** was far behind: of its **35 Phase 1 packages, only four were reported complete** — a figure the Auditor-General expressed as roughly 11.4 per cent of the work. Sabah's state government disputed that percentage, saying it understated the actual physical progress on the ground.

Even in Sarawak, some work continues: the upgrade of certain **17.1 km Redline sections** is reported for completion by **early 2028**, and at least one section near Lambir had its deadline extended to **31 December 2028** after delays. In Sabah, later phases carry targets of **2028 to 2029 or beyond**. Because these dates have slipped repeatedly, they are best read as targets to confirm rather than fixed commitments.

## Why it matters for Borneo

The case for the highway is, at bottom, about **connectivity and access** in two states that are large, relatively sparsely populated and historically underserved by road compared with Peninsular Malaysia.

A modern divided highway is expected to:

- **Cut travel times and improve safety** between major towns, reducing the hazards of long single-lane stretches shared with heavy vehicles.
- **Improve rural access**, linking interior communities to markets, schools, clinics and ports.
- **Support trade and logistics**, giving goods a faster land route between inland areas and coastal ports, and strengthening the road spine that regional development corridors rely on.
- **Narrow the infrastructure gap** between Borneo and the peninsula, a long-standing theme in how Sabah and Sarawak discuss their development needs.

These are the stated benefits; how fully they materialise depends on the project actually being finished, which, on the Sabah side especially, remains a work in progress.

## Common misreadings

- **Treating it as one uniform project.** It is really two state programmes at very different stages — Sarawak nearly done, Sabah years behind.
- **Quoting one cost as "the" cost.** RM16 billion, RM24.89 billion and the larger combined totals describe different states, scopes and contract models of the same highway.
- **Assuming it is finished.** Large parts of the Sarawak section are open, but Sabah's Phase 1 was mostly incomplete as of 2026, with later phases barely started.
- **Confusing the delivery model.** It began under a Project Delivery Partner model but is now largely managed by JKR through conventional contracts after the PDP roles were terminated.
- **Reading it as a toll highway.** It is a federally funded public road, not a tolled concession.

## What's next

The signals to watch are concrete: JKR and the Ministry of Works confirming completion of the remaining Sarawak packages and upgrade sections, the award and start of the many outstanding Sabah packages, and the latest official progress and cost figures in each annual budget and Auditor-General report. Until the Sabah section is substantially built, treat its timelines and benefits as projections to verify rather than settled outcomes.
