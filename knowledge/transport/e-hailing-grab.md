Open Grab, or Bolt, or inDrive, in Kuala Lumpur and tap "book". Nothing about
that moment feels regulated. But the ride that shows up is standing on three
separate licences that had to exist before your driver could legally accept
your fare — and none of them belong to the app alone.

## How a ride actually happens

E-hailing has one defining feature that separates it from a metered taxi:
**there is no street-hailing and no rank.** Every trip starts inside an app —
you request a car, the app matches you to a nearby driver-partner, and the
fare and route are set before the car arrives. The driver is not the
operator's employee; they are a partner using the platform, driving their own
or a company-registered vehicle.

That is why e-hailing needed its own regulatory category rather than simply
being folded into taxi law: a taxi licence covers a car that can be flagged
down on the street, and an e-hailing trip by definition cannot be.

## Who is actually regulating this

E-hailing sits under the **Land Public Transport Act 2010 (Act 715)**, the
same statute that governs buses, taxis and other land public transport. In
**Peninsular Malaysia**, day-to-day licensing is carried out by the **Land
Public Transport Agency (APAD)** — Agensi Pengangkutan Awam Darat — which sits
under the Ministry of Transport. In **Sabah and Sarawak**, the equivalent
functions sit with each state's own **Commercial Vehicles Licensing Board
(Lembaga Pelesenan Kenderaan Perdagangan, LPKP)**, rather than with APAD
directly.

Three distinct approvals stack on top of one ride:

| Layer | Who holds it | What it covers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Operator licence | The app company (Grab, and other e-hailing companies) | Permission to run an "intermediation business" matching riders to drivers |
| E-Hailing Vehicle Permit (EVP) | The vehicle | Permission for that specific private car to be used for e-hailing trips |
| PSV licence | The driver | Personal permission to drive a public service vehicle carrying fare-paying passengers |

Miss any one of the three and the trip is operating outside the licensed
system, regardless of how the other two check out.

## The PSV licence: what a driver actually needs

The **PSV (Public Service Vehicle) licence** is not issued by the e-hailing
company or by APAD — it comes from **JPJ (Jabatan Pengangkutan Jalan)**, the
same department that issues an ordinary driving licence. It is a distinct,
additional licence layered on top of a driver's existing licence, not a
replacement for it.

To apply, JPJ requires that the applicant already holds a **Competent Driving
Licence (CDL) valid for more than one year**, and has completed the required
medical examination, theory classes and practical training before sitting the
theoretical and practical tests. The application is lodged at a JPJ office
with the completed medical form (JPJL8A), the training certificate (JPJL2C),
a passport-sized photo, and the applicable application fee.

Because JPJ issues the PSV licence to the driver personally, it travels with
them rather than belonging to Grab or any single app — a driver who holds one
can, in principle, drive for more than one operator, subject to each
operator's own onboarding conditions.

## Grab and the alternatives

Grab is the largest e-hailing operator in Malaysia by visibility, but it is
not the only one APAD has licensed. APAD publishes and periodically updates a
list of currently licensed e-hailing companies on its own website, and the
market has included operators such as Bolt, inDrive, Maxim, and AirAsia Ride
alongside smaller and more specialised apps. Which operators hold a current
licence, and for how long, changes over time — APAD's own published list is
the authoritative check, not a review site or app-store ranking.

## Common mistakes

- **Assuming a valid driving licence is enough.** A CDL lets you drive; only a separate PSV licence lets you carry fare-paying passengers.
- **Treating the app's approval as the whole picture.** An operator being licensed does not mean the specific car or driver in front of you is covered — the EVP and PSV licence are separate, vehicle- and person-specific approvals.
- **Assuming APAD covers the whole country.** Sabah and Sarawak run their own Commercial Vehicles Licensing Boards, separate from APAD's Peninsular Malaysia remit.
- **Confusing the operator licence with ordinary company registration.** The "intermediation business licence" is a distinct land-transport approval under Act 715, on top of standard company registration.

## What's next

If you are checking whether a car or driver is properly licensed, start with
APAD's own published list of currently licensed e-hailing companies rather
than the app's marketing page. If you are the driver, the PSV licence
application begins at a JPJ office, not with the e-hailing operator — get
that in hand before the platform's own onboarding checks become relevant.
