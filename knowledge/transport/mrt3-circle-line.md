Most new rail lines are built to carry people somewhere new. The MRT3 Circle Line is being built to tie together the lines Kuala Lumpur already has — a ring around the city whose main job is connection, not coverage.

It is also one of the most on-again, off-again projects in the country's transport history: proposed, fast-tracked, cancelled in 2018, revived, and finally cleared for land acquisition in 2025, all while the cost estimates kept moving.

## What exactly is MRT3?

The MRT3 Circle Line is the planned **third Mass Rapid Transit line** in the Klang Valley — a **roughly 51.6 km loop** that runs around the perimeter of central Kuala Lumpur rather than through it. Where the existing MRT lines are radial routes crossing the city, MRT3 is an **orbital line**, closing the rapid-transit network into a ring.

It is owned by **Mass Rapid Transit Corporation (MRT Corp)**, the government company that also owns the MRT1 Kajang Line and MRT2 Putrajaya Line, and it is designed to be operated by **Rapid Rail** — the same Prasarana subsidiary that runs the rest of the metro — as a **fully automated, driverless** line using four-car trainsets at about 100 km/h. A full trip around the loop is estimated at roughly 75 minutes.

The approved scheme describes around **33 stations**, reported as **26 elevated and 7 underground**, served by **two depots**, with about **11.9 km of the alignment in tunnel** and the remainder elevated. For how MRT3 fits alongside the LRT, Monorail and KTM services it is meant to connect, see the network overview, [how Kuala Lumpur's MRT, LRT, Monorail and KTM lines connect](/en/transport/klang-valley-rail/).

## The point of a circle: interchanges

The reason MRT3 matters is in its **interchange stations**. About **ten** of its stops are designed to connect to other lines, letting passengers travel between suburbs on opposite sides of the city without funnelling through the central business district first. The approved alignment's connecting points include the following (station names and codes may change before opening):

| Interchange station | Connects to |
| --- | --- |
| Bukit Kiara Selatan | MRT Kajang Line (MRT1) |
| Titiwangsa | LRT Ampang Line, LRT Sri Petaling Line, KL Monorail, MRT Putrajaya Line (MRT2) |
| Setiawangsa | LRT Kelana Jaya Line |
| Pandan Indah | LRT Ampang Line |
| Taman Midah | MRT Kajang Line (MRT1) |
| Salak Selatan | LRT Sri Petaling Line |
| Kuchai | MRT Putrajaya Line (MRT2) |
| Pantai Dalam | KTM Komuter (Port Klang Line) |
| Universiti | LRT Kelana Jaya Line |

Titiwangsa is the standout node, bringing together several lines at a single point. A provisional station on the western side is also planned to meet the KTM Komuter line. The exact final list is flagged for verification below.

## How it differs from MRT1 and MRT2

All three MRT lines share the same owner, operator and driverless technology, but they do different jobs:

- **MRT1, the Kajang Line**, was the first MRT line to open. It runs **radially** across the Klang Valley, underground beneath the city centre before rising onto elevated track towards Kajang.
- **MRT2, the Putrajaya Line**, is the second radial line, running a longer route with the network's largest underground stretch, from Kwasa Damansara to Putrajaya.
- **MRT3, the Circle Line**, runs **around** the core instead of across it. It adds very little brand-new catchment on its own; its value is as a connector that turns a set of separate radial lines into an integrated ring.

In network terms, MRT1 and MRT2 are spokes; MRT3 is the rim.

## The cost: a figure that keeps moving

MRT3's price tag is the part of the story to treat with most caution, because different sources quote different numbers for different scopes.

- MRT Corp has indicated a **construction cost of around RM31 billion**, broadly comparable to the Putrajaya Line.
- Adding **land acquisition** (reported in the region of several billion ringgit) and other costs, wider totals of about **RM39 billion** have been reported, and at the high end headline figures of around **RM50 billion** have appeared.
- The **2023 Budget** set a target to re-evaluate the project and keep total spending **below RM45 billion**.

So the same project can be described as a RM31 billion line or a RM50 billion line depending on whether the figure covers construction only or an all-in total. No single official all-in number is settled, which is why the cost is flagged for verification.

## Cancelled, revived, approved

The project's timeline is as much political as technical:

- **2010s** — MRT3 is proposed as the final line of the Klang Valley MRT programme and, at points, fast-tracked.
- **30 May 2018** — the newly elected government **cancels** the project on fiscal grounds.
- **Late 2019** — the project is placed **under review** by Transport Minister Anthony Loke.
- **2021** — **Cabinet approval** to proceed again; studies and the alignment are developed.
- **4 March 2022** — the government **reconfirms** MRT3, with an alignment of about 50.8 km later revised upward.
- **September–December 2024** — a **public inspection** draws large-scale feedback, reported as overwhelmingly supportive.
- **17 July 2025** — the Transport Minister approves the **Final Railway Scheme**, clearing the way for **land acquisition**; the number of land lots required is cut from **1,012 to 690**.
- **End of 2026** — land acquisition targeted for completion, paving the way for construction.
- **From ~2027** — construction expected to begin, with **opening targets ranging from a phased 2028 start to a full opening around 2032**.

Because these dates have slipped before — most dramatically the 2018 cancellation — the current construction and opening timeline is exactly the kind of detail to confirm with MRT Corp rather than assume.

## Common misreadings

- **Treating MRT3 as finished or under construction now.** As of late 2026 it was at the land-acquisition stage following the July 2025 scheme approval; opening dates are targets, not facts.
- **Quoting one cost as the cost.** About RM31 billion is a construction figure; all-in totals including land have been reported much higher, up to around RM50 billion.
- **Expecting it to be a radial line like MRT1 and MRT2.** It is a loop around the city, built to connect the existing lines rather than cut through the centre.
- **Confusing it with the KL–Singapore High-Speed Rail.** They are entirely separate projects: MRT3 is an urban metro loop in the Klang Valley, while the [KL–Singapore High-Speed Rail](/en/transport/kl-singapore-high-speed-rail/) is an intercity line proposal.

## What's next

The signals to watch are concrete: MRT Corp confirming the final cost and the construction and opening dates, the completion of land acquisition, the award of main construction packages, and the final station list and interchange design. Until contracts are let and ground is broken, treat the cost, the timeline and even the station count as working figures to verify rather than settled outcomes.

For how the line fits the wider network it is meant to complete, see [how Kuala Lumpur's MRT, LRT, Monorail and KTM lines connect](/en/transport/klang-valley-rail/); and for the country's other headline rail project, the [KL–Singapore High-Speed Rail](/en/transport/kl-singapore-high-speed-rail/).
