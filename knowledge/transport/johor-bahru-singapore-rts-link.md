Every working day, tens of thousands of people inch across the Johor-Singapore Causeway - one of the busiest land borders on earth - by car, bus and motorcycle, often queuing for an hour or more. The RTS Link is the two governments' bet that a short train, not a wider road, is the way to break that jam.

It is a modest 4 km of track with an outsized job: carry commuters across the strait in about five minutes, let them clear both countries' immigration in a single stop, and take enough traffic off the Causeway to make the daily crossing bearable again.

## What exactly is the RTS Link?

The Johor Bahru-Singapore Rapid Transit System (RTS) Link is a **4 km elevated cross-strait rail shuttle** running between **Bukit Chagar** station in Johor Bahru and **Woodlands North** station in Singapore. Of that length, about **2.3 km is in Malaysia and 1.7 km in Singapore**, crossing the Johor Strait on a dedicated bridge that sits beside the existing Causeway.

Its defining feature is **co-located CIQ** - customs, immigration and quarantine facilities at *both* terminals. Travellers clear **both** the Malaysian and Singaporean border checks **once**, at the station they depart from, before boarding. That single-clearance design is the main reason the link is expected to beat the road crossing on time.

The line is operated by **RTS Operations Pte Ltd (RTSO)**, a joint venture between Malaysia's **Prasarana** (through its subsidiary PROSPER) and Singapore's **SMRT** (through SMRTS), under a **30-year concession**. RTSO owns, finances, operates and maintains the trains and rail systems. The two government grantors are the **Land Transport Authority of Singapore** and the **Ministry of Transport Malaysia**.

## The route and its two stations

The alignment is short and point-to-point, with no intermediate stops:

| Station | Location | Connects to |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bukit Chagar | Johor Bahru, beside JB Sentral | JB Sentral rail/bus hub, Johor Bahru city centre |
| Woodlands North | Woodlands, Singapore | Thomson-East Coast Line (TE1) |

At the Singapore end, **Woodlands North** is an interchange with the **Thomson-East Coast Line**, plugging the shuttle straight into Singapore's MRT network. At the Johor end, **Bukit Chagar** sits next to **JB Sentral** and the city's bus terminal, within reach of central Johor Bahru.

The service is planned around **eight driverless four-car trains** (CRRC Zhuzhou light-rail vehicles), running roughly from early morning to midnight, with a peak frequency of about **3.6 minutes**. Trains are maintained at the **Wadi Hana depot** in Johor Bahru.

## Capacity and the Causeway problem

The business case rests on relief. The RTS Link is designed to carry up to **10,000 passengers per hour in each direction**, with a journey time of **about five minutes** end to end. Projected ridership is around **40,000 passengers a day at launch**, rising toward **140,000** over the longer term. The authorities and operator cite a resulting cut in **Causeway traffic of at least 35%**.

The comparison the project is built against is the road crossing. The **Causeway** and the **Second Link** carry cars, buses, motorcycles and lorries, and at peak hours the Causeway in particular is notorious for its queues. The RTS Link does not replace those roads - it is meant to pull a large share of the *commuter* flow off them and onto rail. (For the road option, see [driving to Singapore and Thailand](/en/transport/driving-to-singapore-and-thailand/).)

## How it is funded

The RTS Link is a shared bilateral project rather than a single-country build, and the cost is split between the two governments.

Reporting indicates an infrastructure cost-sharing split of roughly **61:39 between Singapore and Malaysia**, with a total in the order of **S$3.2 billion**. Malaysia's share was **reduced by about 36% in 2019**, from a reported RM4.93 billion to around **RM3.16 billion**, after a cost-cutting renegotiation during a suspension of the project. These figures are flagged for verification below and should be read against the latest official statements; the operator RTSO separately finances the trains and rail-systems assets under its concession.

Fares, meanwhile, are set by the **operator**, not fixed in this cost split. At the time of writing they had not been officially announced; the two governments said the fare and the opening date would be released jointly. Media reporting put likely one-way fares at roughly **S$5 to S$7 (about RM15 to RM21)**, designed to account for the Singapore-Malaysia exchange rate - estimates to treat with caution until confirmed.

## Signed, suspended, revived

Like several Malaysia-Singapore megaprojects, the RTS Link's path has been more stop-start than its short length suggests:

- **1990** - the idea of a rail link across the strait is first discussed between the two countries' leaders.
- **16 January 2018** - a bilateral agreement on the RTS Link is signed.
- **2019** - the project is **suspended** (from May, extended to the end of October) while Malaysia seeks cost reductions; a revised deal cuts Malaysia's share.
- **May-July 2020** - work is **suspended again**, this time because of the COVID-19 pandemic.
- **30 July 2020** - a resumption ceremony is held at the midpoint of the Causeway, and Prasarana and SMRT sign the joint-venture agreement for the operating company.
- **Late 2020 to early 2021** - construction begins (Malaysia in November 2020, Singapore in January 2021).
- **2025-2026** - works reach the final stages; progress is reported around 90% by April 2026.
- **October 2026** - the passenger service target is revised from end-2026 to **February 2027**, with system tests and trial runs due to finish by **31 December 2026**, followed by safety certification and regulatory approval.

Because the opening date has already slipped, the February 2027 target is exactly the kind of detail to confirm with the Ministry of Transport, the LTA or RTSO rather than assume.

## More than a commuter shuttle

For Johor, the link is also an economic bet. Bukit Chagar anchors a push to regenerate central Johor Bahru and feeds the wider development story of **Iskandar Malaysia** and projects such as **Forest City** on the Johor coast. A fast, reliable cross-border rail option changes the daily calculus for the large population that lives in Johor and works in Singapore - and, its backers argue, for where people choose to live and invest on the Malaysian side.

That is why the RTS Link is often discussed alongside Johor's growth corridors rather than purely as a transport line. (See the [Iskandar Malaysia guide](/en/business/iskandar-malaysia-guide/) and [Forest City and the special financial zone](/en/business/forest-city-sfz/).)

## Common misreadings

- **Treating the RTS Link as open.** As of late 2026 it was in final testing; February 2027 is a target, not a fact, and earlier dates already slipped.
- **Confusing it with the KL-Singapore High-Speed Rail.** They are entirely separate projects: the RTS Link is a 4 km cross-border shuttle; the [HSR](/en/transport/kl-singapore-high-speed-rail/) is a long inter-city line that has been shelved and revisited more than once.
- **Thinking it replaces the Causeway.** It is a passenger rail shuttle meant to *relieve* the Causeway and Second Link roads, not to carry vehicles or freight.
- **Assuming you clear immigration twice.** The co-located CIQ means a single clearance at the departure station - the whole point of the design.
- **Taking the fare as settled.** Reported figures are estimates; the official fare had not been announced.

## What's next

The signals to watch are concrete: the Ministry of Transport, the LTA and RTSO jointly confirming the **final opening date** and the **fare structure**; the completion of **safety certification** after trial runs; and the first revenue passenger services. The transition of the short **KTM Shuttle Tebrau** train (reported to be retired around mid-2027) is another marker. Until trains are carrying fare-paying passengers, treat the opening timeline, capacity and fare figures as projections to verify rather than settled outcomes.
