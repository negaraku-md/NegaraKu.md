Many qualified persons with disabilities (OKU) in Malaysia face a real gap in securing job opportunities and interviews — and that is the gap the law is trying to close. Malaysia has had an employment-rights framework for persons with disabilities (OKU) since 2008, plus tax incentives for employers, yet hiring figures remain below target. This guide explains what the law actually requires, and what employers can claim.

## What does Section 29 actually require?

The main framework is the **Persons with Disabilities Act 2008 (Act 685)**, which came into force on 7 July 2008 through P.U.(B) 268/2008. The Act defines OKU as those who "have long-term physical, mental, intellectual or sensory impairments which, in interaction with various barriers, may hinder their full and effective participation in society" (Section 2).

**Section 29** is the core employment provision. It imposes several layered obligations on employers:

- **Section 29(1)** — OKU have the right of access to employment on an equal basis with persons without disabilities.
- **Section 29(2)** — employers shall protect the right of OKU to just and favourable conditions of work, including equal opportunity and **equal remuneration for work of equal value**, safe and healthy working conditions, protection from harassment, and the redress of grievances.
- **Section 29(3)** — employers shall endeavour to promote stable employment by **reasonably assessing the abilities of OKU**, providing a suitable workplace, and carrying out proper employment management.
- **Sections 29(4) and (5)** — the National Council for OKU shall formulate policies including affirmative-action programmes for the private sector, and promote training and self-employment opportunities.

One important word: under **Section 29(6)**, the definition of "employer" includes the Government. These obligations apply to both the public and private sectors.

But we must be honest about enforcement. Section 29 is framed as rights and encouragement, not a prohibition that can be directly penalised. It does not provide specific penalties against employers who discriminate, and this is a common criticism from policy researchers — the protections exist on paper but lack an effective complaints mechanism.

## Is the 1% quota mandatory?

This is the most frequently misunderstood question. There are **two distinct things**: the public-service policy and private-sector encouragement.

For the **public sector**, the government sets a target of 1% of positions for OKU and reinforced it through **Service Circular No. 16 of 2010** (the One Percent Employment Opportunity Policy in the Public Service for OKU). This is an administrative policy binding on government agencies, not a statutory law imposing penalties.

For the **private sector**, there is no statutory quota. Section 29(4) merely directs the Council to formulate "appropriate policies and measures" — encouragement, not compulsion.

The reality is that even the public-sector target has not been met. According to a 2021 report, only about **0.35%** of public-service staff were OKU — far below the 1% target. In March 2024, a Minister in the Prime Minister's Department confirmed in the Dewan Rakyat that the government **has still not met the 1% quota**, even though the number of OKU in the public sector increased between 2014 and 2023. In other words, if even the public sector — which is meant to lead by example — falls short, the gap in the private sector can be expected to be larger.

## What tax incentives are there for employers who hire OKU?

This is where the most concrete encouragement lies. Private employers can claim a **double tax deduction** on wages paid to registered OKU employees. This is allowed under the **Income Tax (Deductions for the Employment of Disabled Persons) Rules 1982 [P.U.(A) 73/1982]**, effective from the year of assessment 1982. This deduction is **in addition** to the ordinary deduction for wages under Section 33 of the Income Tax Act 1967 — so the effect is that those wages are deducted twice from taxable income.

These Rules were amended in 2019 [P.U.(A) 204/2019] to also cover employees who become disabled as a result of an accident or critical illness.

The table below summarises the main incentives:

| Incentive | Legal framework | What qualifies |
|---|---|---|
| Double deduction for OKU wages | Income Tax (Deductions for the Employment of OKU) Rules 1982, P.U.(A) 73/1982 | Cash remuneration — salary, wages, bonus, overtime, commission, allowances of registered OKU employees |
| Double deduction for training | Income Tax (Deduction for Approved Training) Rules 1992 | Training costs for non-employee OKU in approved programmes |
| Deduction for premises modification | Paragraph 34(6)(e) of the Income Tax Act 1967 | Expenditure on equipment and workplace modifications for OKU |

Two important conditions to remember. First, the double deduction on wages covers only **cash remuneration**; benefits-in-kind do not qualify (refer to LHDN Public Ruling 3/2019). Second, all these incentives depend on the employee being **registered as OKU** — without an OKU Card, the claim is invalid. Employers are also encouraged to consult a tax agent for accurate calculations, as eligibility depends on records and verification satisfactory to the Director General of Inland Revenue.

Beyond tax incentives, PERKESO (SOCSO) also runs job placement and OKU hiring-encouragement programmes through cooperation with employers and government agencies.

## How does OKU registration and the OKU Card work?

All of the eligibility above — the Section 29 rights and the tax deductions — depends on registration status. The process is provided for in Act 685:

1. An **application** is made to the National Council for OKU (Section 22), in practice through the **Department of Social Welfare (JKM)** under the Ministry of Women, Family and Community Development.
2. The Registrar may **request additional documents or information** to support the application (Section 23), usually including medical certification according to the disability category.
3. Once approved, the name is entered into the **Register of Persons with Disabilities** maintained under Section 21.
4. The Registrar issues an **OKU Card** (Section 25). This Card, unless proven to have been cancelled, is **conclusive proof** that a person has been validly registered as OKU.

It is this OKU Card that employers need to keep on record to support tax claims and verify an employee's status. The Card must be returned to the Registrar when a person no longer qualifies as OKU (Section 25(3)).

## What does all this mean for employers?

Combine the sections above and the picture becomes clear for private employers:

- **You are not bound by a statutory quota**, but Section 29 imposes obligations of equal treatment, equal pay and a suitable workplace when you employ OKU.
- **There is a real financial reward** — the double deduction on wages effectively reduces the cost of hiring OKU compared with other employees, plus deductions for training and premises modification.
- **The gateway is the OKU Card** — make sure the employee is registered with the JKM before making any tax claim.

A simple example: a company that pays annual wages of RM24,000 to a registered OKU employee can claim that deduction twice — once under Section 33 and again under the 1982 Rules — thereby reducing its taxable income by an amount greater than the wages actually paid. (The exact amount and eligibility should be confirmed with a tax agent.)

## What's next

- Verify the employee's status: request a copy of the **OKU Card** and keep it in the employee's file before processing any tax claim.
- Refer to **LHDN Public Ruling 3/2019** and the Income Tax Rules 1982 [P.U.(A) 73] for accurate deduction calculations.
- Review internal policies against the Section 29 obligations — equal pay, workplace accommodations and a harassment complaints channel.
- For registration, contact the district **Department of Social Welfare** office or the JKM e-services portal.
- Public-sector employers should review compliance with **Service Circular No. 16/2010** and their agency's OKU recruitment plan.
