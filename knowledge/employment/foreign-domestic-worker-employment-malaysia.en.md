A foreign domestic helper enters Malaysia through an entirely different door from a factory or plantation worker — not through the sector quota, not through the foreign-worker One-Stop Centre, and not under the full protection of labour law. This regime stands on its own, and employers who assume the process is the same as hiring a general worker often go astray.

This guide explains the Foreign Domestic Helper (FDH) pathway managed by the Immigration Department of Malaysia: the permitted source countries, employer income requirements, the MoU-based minimum salary, the security bond, and the reasons domestic helpers are "carved out" from mandatory EPF contributions, most of the Employment Act 1955, and the sector quota system.

## Why are foreign domestic helpers under their own regime?

In the ordinary foreign-worker system, employers apply for a quota by sector, through the One-Stop Centre and a centralised management system, before a permit is issued. Domestic helpers do not go through that pathway. The application is made directly to the Immigration Department under the FDH category, and is usually handled through a JTKSM-licensed employment agency without case-by-case quota approval.

This distinction matters because it determines nearly all the obligations that follow — from the documents required to whether statutory contributions apply. Domestic helpers are categorised as "domestic employees" (formerly "domestic servants"), a category that Malaysian law has long deliberately treated differently from ordinary workers.

An approved foreign domestic helper enters with a Visa With Reference (VDR) and holds a Temporary Employment Visit Pass (PLKS). According to the Immigration Department, an FDH candidate must be female, aged between 21 and 45, medically certified fit, and residing in her home country at the time the application is made.

## Which source countries are permitted?

The Immigration Department lists nine permitted source countries for foreign domestic helpers: **Indonesia, Thailand, Cambodia, the Philippines, Sri Lanka, India, Vietnam, Laos and Nepal**.

Each source country is bound by its own bilateral agreement (MoU) or embassy protocol, which sets the minimum salary, the legal recruitment channels, and contract endorsement. The most notable example is Indonesia, which signed an MoU with Malaysia in April 2022. The Human Resources Minister at the time confirmed the minimum salary under that MoU, saying "RM1,500 as per our MOU" (The Star). The MoU also mandates recruitment through a single official channel (the One Channel System) and contract endorsement by the Embassy of the Republic of Indonesia.

## How much employer income and security bond are required?

The employer's minimum income requirement and the security bond vary by source country. Based on the Immigration Department:

| Source country | Employer net income | Security bond |
|---|---|---|
| Indonesia | RM7,000 | RM250 |
| Philippines | RM5,000 | RM750 |
| Sri Lanka | RM5,000 | RM750 |
| India | RM5,000 | RM750 |
| Thailand | RM3,000 | RM250 |
| Cambodia | RM3,000 | RM250 |
| Vietnam | RM3,000 | RM1,500 |
| Laos | RM3,000 | RM1,500 |

The security bond is not a sunk payment — it is a guarantee that can be reclaimed when the domestic helper returns to her home country, subject to conditions. For Indonesia, the Immigration Department sets a minimum employer net income of RM7,000, and the recruitment cost through an appointed Malaysia Recruitment Agency (MRA) is capped at RM15,000 (maximum). This RM15,000 cap was also confirmed by the Ministry of Human Resources as the maximum recruitment cost for a new Indonesian domestic helper (The Star).

If an employer wishes to hire more than one helper, the income threshold rises. According to the Immigration Department, a second helper requires income above RM10,000, while a third helper requires income above RM15,000.

## Why are domestic helpers excluded from EPF?

This is among the most important features of the FDH regime. In October 2025, an amendment to the EPF Act made EPF contributions mandatory for foreign workers — a rate of 2% from the employer and 2% from the worker. Domestic helpers, however, are expressly excluded.

According to activpayroll's analysis of the amendment, "Domestic helpers (e.g., maids, cooks, cleaners) with valid permits are excluded from this requirement." This means that even though foreign co-workers in other sectors must now contribute, employers of domestic helpers are not required to do so. EPF contributions for domestic helpers remain voluntary only.

This exclusion stems from the definition of "domestic servant," which has historically been carved out of the scope of statutory contributions — a legal legacy that places domestic work in its own category, unlike ordinary employment.

## How does the Employment Act 1955 treat domestic helpers?

Since the Employment (Amendment) Act 2022 took effect on 1 January 2023, almost all workers in Malaysia are protected by the Employment Act regardless of wage. But domestic employees are still treated separately.

According to Rahmat Lim & Partners, "The Amendment Act continues to exclude domestic employees from Part XII of the Employment Act with the exception of the provision on rest days." Part XII covers rest days, working hours, leave and other terms of service. As a result, domestic employees:

- **Are entitled** to one rest day per week (a new protection under that amendment).
- **Are not subject** to the maximum weekly working-hours limit, paid public-holiday entitlements, and other standard terms of service that apply to ordinary workers.

This makes the domestic helper's employment relationship governed more by the employment contract and the source country's MoU than by the Employment Act itself — which is why embassy contract endorsement becomes so important.

## Levy, insurance and quota — what applies?

Because domestic helpers do not go through the sector quota system, employers do not need to apply for an FWCMS quota as they would for a general worker. The application is handled directly through the Immigration Department, usually via a licensed agency.

The annual foreign-worker levy still applies and is paid at permit renewal. Levy rates vary by sector and region and can change, so employers should confirm the current rate directly with the Immigration Department. Employers must also ensure the domestic helper is covered by hospital insurance and work-related injury protection as part of the permit conditions.

In short, foreign domestic helpers sit in a hybrid position: still subject to the levy and mandatory insurance, but carved out of the sector quota, mandatory EPF contributions, and most of the Employment Act's protections.

## What's next

Before beginning an application, confirm three basics: your preferred source country and the MoU that binds it, whether your income meets the threshold for that country, and the JTKSM-licensed employment agency that will handle the Immigration application. Keep the embassy-endorsed employment contract as the primary reference document, because the Employment Act protects only a small part of this relationship.

Levy rates, income requirements and minimum salaries can change when MoUs are reviewed — confirm the latest figures with the Immigration Department of Malaysia and the source country's embassy before signing any agreement.
