The contract of service is the binding document that sets the full terms you both operate under. Treat the offer as the step that opens the door and issue a proper written contract as well; relying on a signed offer alone leaves probation, leave and notice terms disputable.   - q: "Why does everyone say the start date matters more than the offer date?"
    a: "Because the statutory clocks — notifying LHDN, EPF liability, the first contribution month, the register entry — all run from the commencement of employment, the agreed first working day, not from when the offer was signed. An offer accepted in one month for a start two months later is a start-date problem.   - q: "Can I just pay my first employee in cash and sort out the paperwork later?"
    a: "That's the route to trouble. Wages are contemplated to be paid into the employee's own bank account, an itemised payslip is expected for each wage period, and EPF, SOCSO, EIS and PCB must be deducted and remitted from the first wage month. Set up the employer registrations and collect the employee's details before day one, so the first payroll is compliant rather than something you reconstruct afterwards."
  - q: "Do I have to use payroll software?"
    a: "No. A first payroll can be run by hand against the official EPF, SOCSO, EIS and PCB schedules, handed to an accountant or payroll bureau, or run on payroll software — all three can be compliant. The trade-off is cost versus how much of the rule-tracking you keep yourself: manual is cheapest but puts the onus on you to read the current rate schedules, a bureau offloads the computation for a fee, and software automates it for a subscription but still needs correct inputs."

Hiring your first employee is the moment a side project or a one-person
business becomes a company with duties to other people and to four government
agencies. It feels like a single decision — "I'll take them on" — but it is
really a sequence, and getting the order right is what separates a clean start
from a scramble of back-dated forms.

This is the operational companion to the
[new-hire statutory registration checklist](/en/employment/new-hire-statutory-checklist),
which owns the deadlines, the rates and the section numbers. This guide walks
the **journey** instead — offer, contract, setup, first payroll — and the
practical to-dos at each step. Two things on this page are genuinely its own
point to make, and everything else defers to the checklist:

1. **The offer letter is not the contract.**
2. **The start date, not the offer date, is the hinge** that every statutory
   clock runs from.

This is general information, not legal advice; confirm specifics with the
agencies and, where it matters, a qualified adviser.

## Stage 1 — The offer

An offer letter (sometimes a "letter of appointment") is your written invitation
to the candidate. It typically states the job title, the **start date**, the
salary and pay cycle, the probation period, the notice period, a summary of
leave and benefits, the reporting line, and any **conditions** the offer depends
on — satisfactory references, a medical check, proof of the right to work, or
(for a non-citizen) the required employment approval being in place first.

What it is *not* is the binding contract. The offer opens the door; the
**contract of service** (Stage 2) sets the terms you both operate under. This
matters because Malaysian law looks at the **substance** of the working
relationship, not the label on a document — the Employment Act 1955 defines a
contract of service broadly enough that even an oral arrangement to employ and
to serve can be one. So the question is never "did we sign something called a
contract," it is "what are the real terms, and are they written down."

The start date you put in the offer is the **commencement of employment** — the
single most important date in this whole process. Every statutory clock in the
checklist (notifying LHDN, EPF liability, the first contribution month, the
register entry) runs from that day, not from the day the offer is signed. An
offer accepted in March for a June start is a June set of obligations. Diarise
them from the start date.

> **To-dos:** settle job title, start date, salary, pay cycle, probation and
> notice; spell out any conditions the offer depends on; make clear in your own
> process that a fuller contract will follow; keep the signed offer on file.

## Stage 2 — The written contract of service

The binding document is the contract of service. A sound first contract nails
down: the **job scope**, **wages and the pay cycle**, **working hours**, the
**rest day**, whether **overtime** provisions apply, **leave** entitlements
(annual, sick, public holidays, and statutory maternity or paternity leave),
**probation** length and how it may be extended, **termination and notice**
(equal both ways), and **confidentiality and return of property**.

The Employment Act 1955 expects a written contract in defined cases and expects
it to carry a termination clause; issuing one is standard practice even where the
Act's wording would not strictly compel it, because you need the particulars for
the register of employees in any case. How each clause actually holds up — void
terms that fall below the Act, equal-notice requirements, lawful deductions, and
the expectation that wages are paid into a bank account — is set out
clause by clause in
[the Malaysian employment contract guide](/en/employment/employment-contract-malaysia).
Use that article to get the terms right; this one just reminds you to issue the
contract rather than leaning on the offer.

One hedge worth building into the contract itself: **whether some provisions
apply to your hire — overtime in particular — can depend on the employee's wage
level and category**, and the rules here were changed by the Employment Act
amendments that took effect in the 2020s. Don't promise a specific overtime or
coverage position in the contract until you have checked the current rule; point
to the statutory sources rather than guessing.

> **To-dos:** issue a written contract of service; set scope, wages and cycle,
> hours, rest day, leave, probation and its extension mechanics, equal notice,
> and confidentiality; have it signed on or before the start date; file it and
> use it to populate the register of employees.

## Stage 3 — Before day one: registrations and details

Two parallel jobs sit between "contract signed" and "first working day."

**A. Confirm your own employer registrations exist.** Your first hire makes you
an *employer* in the eyes of three agencies, so you must be registered as an
employer with **EPF (KWSP)**, **SOCSO and EIS (PERKESO)** and **LHDN** before the
first payroll. If you aren't yet, that registration comes first — the exact
windows (and, for a non-citizen hire, the prior-approval step that must precede
the start date) are owned by the
[new-hire statutory checklist](/en/employment/new-hire-statutory-checklist).
Register first, then run payroll.

**B. Collect the employee's details.** This is the operational heart of the
stage. Before you can run payroll you need:

- **Identity** — NRIC (MyKad) for a citizen or PR, or passport plus the relevant
  employment approval for a non-citizen.
- **A bank account in the employee's own name** for salary crediting. A
  cash-payment arrangement does not fit the expectation that wages go into a bank
  account.
- **EPF member number** — many employees already have one from a previous job; a
  first-ever employee is registered as a member (Stage 4).
- **SOCSO/EIS** — registration is **employer-driven**, so here you mainly need
  their identity details; you register them with PERKESO.
- **Tax reference and a TP3** — the TP3 form captures the remuneration, EPF and
  tax (PCB/MTD) **already deducted by a previous employer in the same calendar
  year**, so your first payroll computes tax on a correct year-to-date basis
  instead of treating the new salary as the year's only income. A first-ever
  employee can submit a blank TP3.

The clean way to hold this in your head is a split: **what the employee provides**
(IC or passport, bank account, any existing EPF member number and tax reference,
the TP3) versus **what you set up** (the employer registrations, the employee's
SOCSO/EIS registration, the register entry, and the payroll record itself).

> **To-dos:** confirm EPF/SOCSO/LHDN employer registration; collect IC/passport,
> bank details, EPF member number, tax reference and TP3; open the register of
> employees.

## Stage 4 — The employee's registrations

This stage is mostly collecting membership numbers and a reference so Stage 5 can
compute the right deductions. The mechanics change, so collect and link rather
than walk through screens:

- **EPF member number** — needed for contributions; created on first registration
  if the employee has never worked before (KWSP).
- **SOCSO and EIS** — you register the employee with PERKESO; they do not
  self-register.
- **Income-tax reference** — the employee needs a tax reference for PCB/MTD. In
  practice a tax file can be registered by the employee, and your **CP22**
  new-employee notification ties the hire into LHDN's system. The exact mechanics
  of whether CP22 creates the tax file or the employee registers are worth
  confirming on the LHDN site; the CP22 deadline itself is in the
  [statutory checklist](/en/employment/new-hire-statutory-checklist).

## Stage 5 — The first payroll run

A payslip for the first — and every — wage period should itemise **gross wages,
any allowances and overtime, each statutory deduction shown separately (the
employee's EPF share, SOCSO, EIS and PCB/MTD), any other lawful deductions, and
net pay**, for the stated wage period, given on or before the pay day. The
itemised-payslip duty sits in the **Employment Regulations 1957 read with the
Employment Act**, not in a single headline section of the Act, and the pay-slip
obligations were widened by the 2020s amendments.

The deductions your first payroll must compute and remit are:

- **EPF** — the employee's share deducted and the employer's share added (KWSP);
- **SOCSO** and **EIS** — through PERKESO;
- **PCB/MTD**, the monthly tax deduction to LHDN, computed year-to-date (this is
  why the TP3 from Stage 3 matters);
- the **HRD Corp levy**, *if your business is within scope* (that depends on your
  headcount and industry).

The actual percentages, the EPF Third Schedule amounts, the SOCSO and EIS
insured-wage ceiling, the minimum wage, and every remittance deadline are **not
reproduced here on purpose** — they move, and getting them from a stale secondary
source is how first payrolls go wrong. Read them from the official schedules and
from
[Malaysian payroll compliance](/en/employment/payroll-compliance-malaysia) and
[payroll rates and deadlines](/en/employment/payroll-rates-deadlines-malaysia),
and remember (as the checklist notes) that EPF is read off a fixed-amount
schedule, not applied as a flat percentage, up to its ceiling.

Wages for a wage period are payable within the statutory window after the period
ends — pay net salary into the employee's bank account by that date, and remit
each statutory deduction to its agency by its own deadline. The precise day-count
is in the Act and the payroll-compliance article; diarise it rather than
memorising a number that may change.

> **To-dos:** set the wage period and pay day; compute EPF, SOCSO, EIS and PCB
> (year-to-date, using the TP3); check whether the HRD Corp levy applies; produce
> an itemised payslip; credit net pay by the statutory date; remit each deduction
> to its agency on time.

## Common first-employer mistakes

- **Treating the offer letter as the contract.** Issue both — the offer, then a
  written contract of service.
- **Starting someone before your employer registrations exist.** EPF, SOCSO and
  LHDN employer registration comes before the first payroll, not after.
- **Misclassifying an employee as a "contractor" to avoid EPF and SOCSO.** The
  label does not decide it — substance does, and the exposure stacks (EPF arrears,
  SOCSO and EIS, tax, Employment Act offences and an unfair-dismissal claim all at
  once). See
  [employee, gig worker or independent contractor?](/en/employment/employee-vs-contractor-malaysia).
- **Forgetting PCB from month one** (and skipping the TP3), so the year-to-date
  tax is wrong and the employee is under-deducted.
- **No written contract**, leaving probation, notice and leave terms disputable
  and the register under-populated.
- **Misreading probation** as "free to terminate." A probationer still has
  protection; see the contract guide rather than assuming.
