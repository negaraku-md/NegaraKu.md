Malaysia is among the few countries in the world whose monarch is elected on a rotational basis rather than inherited one after another from a single lineage. Every five years, the nine Malay Rulers convene to determine which among them will occupy the Federal throne as the Yang di-Pertuan Agong. This system, together with the institution that operates it — the Conference of Rulers — forms the core of the country's constitutional monarchy.

This article explains the position and role of both institutions in a descriptive manner and based on the provisions of the Federal Constitution. It is a draft that has not yet been reviewed by a human editor.

## What is the constitutional position of the Yang di-Pertuan Agong?

Article 32 of the Federal Constitution provides that the Yang di-Pertuan Agong is the Head of State of the Federation, and that he takes precedence over all other persons in the country.

As a constitutional monarch, the Agong generally acts on the advice of the Cabinet or an authorised minister. Among the functions and powers associated with his office are appointing the Prime Minister who commands the confidence of the majority of the Dewan Rakyat, the discretion to grant or withhold assent to a request for the dissolution of Parliament, summoning meetings of the Conference of Rulers on certain matters, as well as appointing judges and granting pardons within certain jurisdictions. He also acts as the Head of the Religion of Islam for the states that have no Ruler, namely Melaka, Penang, the Federal Territories, Sabah, and Sarawak.

## How is the Agong elected and why on a rotational basis?

The Yang di-Pertuan Agong is elected by the Conference of Rulers from among the nine Malay Rulers for a five-year term. According to the official account of the Conference of Rulers, the election is conducted by secret ballot in accordance with the procedure set out in the Third Schedule of the Federal Constitution: each Ruler is given a ballot paper, and the secretariat is managed by the Keeper of the Rulers' Seal.

The rotational principle ensures that every eligible and willing Ruler has the opportunity to occupy the Federal throne before any state occupies it for a second time. This system is rooted in the arrangement agreed upon at the early formation of the Federation and makes Malaysia an example of a rotating constitutional monarchy.

The nine monarchical states whose Rulers are eligible for election are:

| Monarchical state | Ruler's title |
| --- | --- |
| Perak | Sultan |
| Selangor | Sultan |
| Negeri Sembilan | Yang di-Pertuan Besar |
| Pahang | Sultan |
| Johor | Sultan |
| Kelantan | Sultan |
| Terengganu | Sultan |
| Kedah | Sultan |
| Perlis | Raja |

The remaining four states — Melaka, Penang, Sabah, and Sarawak — are headed by a Yang di-Pertua Negeri (Governor), who is a member of the Conference of Rulers but is not eligible to be elected as Agong and does not vote in the election of the Agong.

## What is the Conference of Rulers and who are its members?

The Conference of Rulers (Majlis Raja-Raja) is a constitutional institution that was formally established in 1948 and is now governed by Article 38 of the Federal Constitution. Its official authorities describe it as the highest institution in the country and the only council of its kind in the world.

Its membership consists of:

- The nine Malay Rulers of the monarchical states.
- The four Yang di-Pertua Negeri (Governors) of Melaka, Penang, Sabah, and Sarawak.

The Conference generally meets several times a year, and extraordinary meetings may be convened when needed. Its chairmanship rotates among the ruling Malay Rulers. The Secretary of the Conference is the Keeper of the Rulers' Seal, who also heads the Conference's secretariat.

## What are the powers and functions of the Conference of Rulers?

In addition to electing the Yang di-Pertuan Agong and the Deputy Yang di-Pertuan Agong, the Conference of Rulers is given several important constitutional functions. Among them:

- **Power of removal.** Article 38(6) gives the Conference the power to remove the Yang di-Pertuan Agong from office. According to legal commentary, this power has never been used.
- **Consent to certain laws.** Laws that directly touch on the privileges, position, honour, or dignity of the Rulers require the consent of the Conference (Article 38(4)).
- **Certain constitutional amendments.** Some amendments cannot be passed without the consent of the Conference of Rulers under Article 159(5). According to the commentary of the Malaysian Bar, these include amendments touching on the position of the Rulers (Article 38(4)), the sovereign rights and succession of the Rulers (Article 71), the position of the Malay language (Article 152), and the special position of the Malays and the natives (Article 153); the full list in Article 159(5) also encompasses several other provisions.
- **Consultation.** The Conference is consulted regarding certain appointments such as judges of the superior courts, the Auditor General, and members of certain commissions.
- **Deliberation on national policy.** The Conference may consider national policy and matters relating to the religion of Islam and the position of the Malays.
- **Special Court.** When a Ruler or the Yang di-Pertuan Agong faces proceedings under Article 182, those proceedings are brought before a Special Court composed of five judges; according to the commentary of the Malaysian Bar, two of them are named by the Conference of Rulers.

Certain alterations of state boundaries under Article 2 also require consent, and according to the official authorities of the Conference, this provision has been used in relation to changes concerning Kuala Lumpur (1973) and Labuan (1984).

## How are the two institutions related?

The Yang di-Pertuan Agong and the Conference of Rulers are closely connected but distinct in their roles. The Agong is the individual who holds the office of Head of State, while the Conference is the collective body that elects him, has the power to remove him in certain circumstances, and holds the power of consent over matters touching on the position of the Rulers. Some legal commentaries describe this relationship as making the Agong akin to a representative of the Conference of Rulers at the Federal level.

Some observers emphasise the symbolic and unifying role of these institutions within the parliamentary democracy system, while academic discussion also examines the boundary between the discretion of the monarch and the convention of acting on advice. These views are discussed extensively in constitutional law writings.
