You were dismissed this morning and told your "performance was unsatisfactory" — but there was no warning letter, no internal inquiry, and no chance to defend yourself. In Malaysia, this situation has a legal name and one dedicated forum: representations under section 20 of the Industrial Relations Act 1967, heard at the Industrial Court. This is the layer of labour relations that the Employment Act 1955 does not manage.

The Industrial Relations Act 1967 (IRA 1967) governs the *collective* and *dispute* relations between employers and employees: how trade unions are recognised, how collective bargaining is conducted, and — the part most often encountered by ordinary people — the right to challenge a dismissal considered unfair. This guide explains that mechanism step by step, with section numbers and figures you can check.

## What exactly does the IRA 1967 govern?

The IRA 1967 is not a list of minimum wages or annual leave — that is the province of the Employment Act 1955. Instead, the IRA 1967 focuses on three pillars of industrial relations:

- **Trade union recognition** (section 9) — the process by which a union obtains the right to represent workers at a given workplace.
- **Collective bargaining and agreements** (sections 13-14) — how employment terms are negotiated collectively and bound into a collective agreement.
- **Dispute resolution and dismissal** (sections 20, 26 and 30) — including dismissal claims "without just cause or excuse" and Industrial Court awards.

One important distinction that is often misunderstood: the IRA 1967 protects all **"workmen"** — defined in section 2 as any person employed under a contract of service — **with no wage ceiling**. This means a highly paid senior manager or executive who is dismissed can still make a section 20 claim, even though they may fall outside certain protections of the Employment Act 1955.

## How does a trade union obtain recognition?

Before a union can bargain on behalf of workers, it must be recognised. The process begins when the union submits a written claim for recognition to the employer.

An employer who receives the claim must, within the period provided under section 9 (reported as **21 days**), either recognise the union or notify the union in writing of the grounds for refusal. If the employer refuses to recognise it, the union may bring the matter to the Director General of Industrial Relations (DGIR) to intervene under section 9. (This 21-day figure should be checked against the current statutory text before being used as a definitive fact — see the verification note.)

The DGIR then has several investigative powers:

- Require the union, employer, or employers' association to furnish relevant information.
- Refer to the Director General of Trade Unions to confirm the union's **competence** to represent the class of workers concerned.
- Enter the workplace to inspect records or **conduct a secret ballot**.

The secret ballot is conducted by the DGIR under Regulations 6 to 13 of the Industrial Relations Regulations 2009. A union typically needs to show majority support — in one reported case (Adabi Consumer Industries), over 72% of workers were confirmed as union members through a secret ballot. Once recognition is granted, the employer must bargain in good faith to form a collective agreement.

The 2020 amendments also introduced the concept of **"sole bargaining rights"** through sections 12A and 12B, and shortened the bar period for competing claims from three years to one year.

## What is "dismissal without just cause or excuse"?

This is the part of the IRA 1967 most frequently encountered by ordinary workers. Under **section 20(1)**, if a workman considers that he has been dismissed by his employer **without just cause or excuse**, he may make representations to be reinstated to his employment.

The phrase "without just cause or excuse" is the core. The employer must not only show *that* the worker was dismissed, but *why* — and that reason must be valid and proven. The burden of proving just cause lies on the employer.

This principle also covers **constructive dismissal**: where the employer breaches a fundamental term of the contract so as to force the worker to resign, the law may treat that resignation as a dismissal.

The most critical thing to remember is the **60-day limitation period**. Under section 20(1A), representations must be filed within **60 days** of the dismissal date. They are filed at any office of the Industrial Relations Department (JPP) near the workplace. Missing this period can end a claim before it begins.

## What happens after representations are filed?

The section 20 process moves through several clear stages. The 2020 amendments (effective **1 January 2021**) changed one of the most important steps.

| Stage | What happens | Reference |
|---|---|---|
| 1. File representations | The worker files at the JPP within 60 days | Section 20(1A) |
| 2. Conciliation | The DGIR attempts to reconcile employer and worker | Section 20(2) |
| 3. Reference to the Court | If conciliation fails, the DGIR refers directly to the Industrial Court | Section 20(3) |
| 4. Hearing & award | The Court decides whether the dismissal was fair, and the remedy | Section 30 |

Before the 2020 amendments, the third step depended on the **Human Resources Minister's discretion** — the Minister decided whether to refer the case to the Industrial Court. This discretion has been **abolished**. Now, if conciliation fails, the DGIR **must** refer the representations directly to the Industrial Court for an award. The aim is to speed up resolution and eliminate delays at the Ministerial stage.

During conciliation meetings, section 20(6) (as amended) allows the employer and worker to be represented by any person — except advocates and solicitors — with written authorisation and the DGIR's permission.

## What remedies apply if the Court finds the dismissal unfair?

If the Industrial Court finds that the dismissal occurred without just cause, it has the power to grant remedies under section 30. There are three main forms:

**1. Reinstatement.** This is the primary remedy under section 20(1) — restoring the worker to their original position. However, the court may decline reinstatement if the worker has reached retirement age, if the employer-employee relationship has broken down severely, or if the position no longer exists.

**2. Backwages.** Compensation for income lost from the dismissal date to the date of the award. Under the Second Schedule of the IRA 1967, backwages are capped as follows:

| Category of employee | Maximum cap on backwages |
|---|---|
| Confirmed (permanent) employee | 24 months' last-drawn salary |
| Employee on probation (probationer) | 12 months' last-drawn salary |

The court may reduce part of this amount if the worker has earned income after the dismissal, or if the worker's conduct contributed to the dispute.

**3. Compensation in lieu of reinstatement.** Granted where reinstatement is unsuitable. It is calculated as **one month's last-drawn salary for each completed year of service**, following Practice Note No. 1 of 1987 (this practice note has no binding legal force and the court retains discretion). The court assesses the amount on the basis of "equity and good conscience" under section 30(5).

According to the Second Schedule of the IRA 1967, an award for loss of future earnings is not included in the Industrial Court's remedies — a restriction that, according to legal sources, was introduced through the amendment that inserted the Second Schedule around 2007 (the exact effective date should be checked; see the verification note). Costs are rarely awarded except in exceptional circumstances.

For monetary awards, the 2020 amendments introduced **interest of 8% per annum** under section 30(1A), calculated from the 31st day after the award is made — a deterrent so that employers do not delay payment.

## Can an Industrial Court award be appealed?

Yes, and the 2020 amendments made it easier. Previously, the only way to challenge an Industrial Court award was judicial review at the High Court — a process limited to errors of law or procedure, not merits.

Section 33C (new) now allows a **direct appeal to the High Court** against an award, filed within **14 days**, without requiring leave beforehand. This opens the door to a review on "substantive merits" broader than judicial review.

If an employer fails to comply with an award or collective agreement, section 56 (as amended) raises the fine for non-compliance from RM2,000 to a maximum of **RM50,000**, with an additional fine not exceeding RM500 for each day the offence continues.

## IRA 1967 versus the Employment Act 1955 — what is the difference?

These two Acts are often confused. In short, the Employment Act 1955 sets *minimum terms* of individual employment, while the IRA 1967 manages *collective relations* and *disputes*, including unfair dismissal.

| Aspect | IRA 1967 | Employment Act 1955 |
|---|---|---|
| Main focus | Unions, collective bargaining, disputes, unfair dismissal | Minimum terms: wages, leave, working hours |
| Who is protected | All "workmen" with no wage ceiling | Its own scope under the Act |
| Forum | Industrial Court | Labour Court / Labour Department |
| Dismissal remedy | Reinstatement, backwages, compensation | Terms & conditions claims |
| Dismissal test | "Without just cause or excuse" | — |

For most dismissal claims, the correct route is section 20 of the IRA 1967 at the Industrial Court, not a civil claim or the Labour Court.

## Should I file a section 20 claim? A decision framework

Use this brief framework to assess your position:

1. **Are you a "workman"?** If you work under a contract of service, the answer is almost certainly yes — there is no wage ceiling.
2. **Were you "dismissed"?** This includes direct dismissal, non-renewal of contract in certain circumstances, and constructive dismissal (the employer forcing you to leave).
3. **Did the employer show just cause and fair procedure?** The absence of an internal inquiry, a show-cause letter, or a chance to defend yourself is often a sign of unfair dismissal.
4. **Are you still within 60 days?** If more than 60 days have passed since the dismissal date, the section 20 claim is most likely barred.

If the answers to 1, 2 and 4 are "yes" and there is doubt on 3, a section 20 claim should be seriously considered.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Missing the 60-day period.** This is the most damaging mistake. Count from the dismissal date, not the date you obtained advice.
- **Assuming only low-paid workers are protected.** The IRA 1967 imposes no wage ceiling — executives and managers are also "workmen".
- **Employer dismissing without an internal inquiry.** In misconduct cases, the absence of a fair domestic inquiry often renders the dismissal invalid even if the misconduct is true.
- **Assuming resignation closes the door.** Resignation resulting from the employer's breach may be treated as constructive dismissal.
- **Confusing backwages with compensation.** Backwages (capped at 24/12 months) and compensation in lieu of reinstatement (one month per year) are two separate items that may be awarded together.
- **Employer delaying payment of the award.** Interest of 8% per annum now applies from the 31st day, so delay adds cost.

## What next

If you have just been dismissed, the most urgent action is to mark the **60th-day deadline** on your calendar and gather documents — offer letter, payslips, dismissal letter, and any internal inquiry records. Section 20 representations can be filed at any office of the Industrial Relations Department.

If you are an employer or human resource representative, check whether your dismissal process has a provable reason and a fair procedure — because the burden of proof lies on you at the Industrial Court, and the 2021 amendments have sped up the path of cases to the court.
