You pay a 10% deposit for a condominium that exists only as an architect's drawing, then wait three years while paying loan instalments. What stops the developer from running off with your money, building late, or handing over a defective unit? The answer is a law more than half a century old: the Housing Development (Control and Licensing) Act 1966 — known in short as the HDA or Act 118.

The HDA is the primary protective framework for anyone buying an off-plan residential home from a private developer in Peninsular Malaysia. It does not merely regulate developers; it rewrites your sale and purchase contract so that its terms cannot be twisted to your detriment. This guide explains how it works, the figures you need to know, and what to do when something goes wrong.

## Who and what does the HDA protect?

The HDA applies when three conditions are met. First, the location must be in Peninsular Malaysia only — section 1(3) states that the Act "shall apply only in Peninsular Malaysia". Sabah and Sarawak have their own housing enactments and ordinances, so do not assume the same terms apply.

Second, it must involve a *housing development*. Section 3 defines this term as building "more than four units of housing accommodation" — so projects of five units or more are included, but the sale of four units or fewer is not.

Third, it must be residential. "Housing accommodation" covers buildings constructed or intended for human habitation, but does not include property on land designated for commercial development. A genuine shop lot falls outside the HDA.

If all three conditions are met, almost every aspect of your transaction — from the advertisement that drew your interest through to defect repairs after the keys are handed over — is governed by this Act and its regulations.

## Why must developers be licensed — and what is the APDL?

Protection begins before a single brick is laid. Section 5(1) prohibits anyone from carrying out housing development "except by a housing developer in possession of a licence issued under this Act." This licence is not an empty formality; it is a filter.

Under section 6, a developer company must have an issued and paid-up capital in cash of at least RM250,000 and place a deposit with the Controller of Housing. This deposit rate was raised from a fixed RM200,000 to 3% of the estimated construction cost through the 2012 Amendment (Act A1415), which came into force on 1 June 2015 — calculated to include financing costs, overhead costs and other expenses to complete the project, but excluding the cost of land. This deposit is not merely an entry payment — it is held by the Controller until the expiry of the development's defect liability period, so that there is money to protect buyers if the developer fails.

Besides the licence, the developer also needs an **Advertisement and Sale Permit** before it may advertise or sell. It is this combination of developer licence + advertisement permit that the industry refers to as the **APDL**. Section 7(b) requires the developer to display copies of this licence and permit prominently at its office. Every Schedule H agreement also records the developer's licence number and the Advertisement and Sale Permit number on its first page — so you can check them yourself.

What happens if a developer builds without a licence? Section 18 provides for heavy penalties: a fine of "not less than two hundred and fifty thousand ringgit but which shall not exceed five hundred thousand ringgit" (RM250,000–RM500,000) or imprisonment up to five years, or both.

### Your money is segregated: the Housing Development Account

One of the most important protections is rarely seen by buyers. Section 7A requires every licensed developer to open and maintain a separate **Housing Development Account** for each project. All the purchase money you pay goes into this account, and the developer cannot withdraw it at will — withdrawals are permitted only in accordance with the regulations.

The real effect appears when a developer goes bankrupt. Section 7A(5) states that the money in this account and money held by the stakeholder "shall... be deemed not to form part of the property of the licensed housing developer" if the developer enters liquidation. In plain terms: your money cannot be swept away by the developer's creditors. A developer who breaches section 7A can be fined RM250,000 to RM500,000 and imprisoned up to three years, or both (section 7A(10)).

## Schedule H versus Schedule G: which agreement for your property?

The HDA does not let developers write their own contracts. Under the power of section 24, the government prescribes the standard sale and purchase agreement forms that must be used. The two most relevant to buyers are Schedule G and Schedule H.

| Feature | Schedule G (landed) | Schedule H (strata) |
|---|---|---|
| Property type | Terrace, semi-detached, bungalow — sold together with land | Condominium, apartment, flat — parcels within a strata building |
| Delivery of vacant possession | 24 months from the date of the agreement | 36 months from the date of the agreement |
| LAD rate if late | 10% per year on the purchase price | 10% per year on the purchase price |
| Defect liability period | 24 months from vacant possession | 24 months from vacant possession |
| Defect clause | Clause 27 | Clause 30 |

The choice between the two is not yours — it is determined by the property type. If you are buying a strata unit, the developer must use Schedule H; if landed, Schedule G. These agreements also apply to projects under licences issued on or after 1 July 2015; older projects use earlier versions of the agreement.

The details below refer to the more complex Schedule H (strata), but the principles are parallel for Schedule G.

## How long does the developer have to deliver vacant possession?

For strata property, clause 26(1) of Schedule H provides: "Vacant possession of the said Parcel shall be delivered to the Purchaser... within thirty-six (36) calendar months from the date of this Agreement." The count starts from the date of the agreement, not the date you paid the deposit.

Vacant possession, moreover, is not simply the handing over of keys. Clause 27 requires the developer's architect to certify that construction is complete and that water and electricity supplies are ready for connection, and that the developer has applied for the Certificate of Completion and Compliance. You are also not permitted to occupy the unit until that certificate is issued.

Common facilities — pool, lifts, playground — are subject to the same period. Clause 28(1) requires common facilities to be completed within 36 months; if late, clause 28(2) imposes LAD of 10% per year on the last 20% of the purchase price.

## How is late-delivery damages (LAD) calculated?

This is the backbone of buyer protection. **Liquidated Ascertained Damages (LAD)** are fixed damages already agreed in the contract, so you do not need to prove actual loss — you simply calculate according to the formula.

Clause 26(2) provides that the developer is liable to pay LAD "calculated from day to day at the rate of ten per centum (10%) per annum of the purchase price" from the expiry of the 36-month period until the date you take vacant possession.

The formula is simple:

**LAD = 10% × purchase price × (number of days late ÷ 365)**

Concrete examples:

- **RM500,000 unit, 6 months late:** 10% × RM500,000 × (183/365) ≈ **RM25,068**
- **RM800,000 unit, 12 months late:** 10% × RM800,000 = **RM80,000**
- **Common facilities of a RM500,000 unit, 6 months late:** 10% × (20% × RM500,000) × (183/365) ≈ **RM5,014**

Clause 26(2) requires LAD to be paid "immediately upon the date the Purchaser takes vacant possession." In terms of timing, clause 26(3) clarifies that the cause of action to claim LAD accrues on the date you take vacant possession.

One important point: because the Schedule G/H agreement is a mandatory contract under section 24 and "time shall be the essence of the contract" (clause 8), the developer cannot rewrite this clause to shorten your LAD entitlement. Terms that attempt to prejudice the buyer risk being void.

## What happens after you take the keys?

Delivery of vacant possession is not the end of protection — it starts a new phase.

**Defect liability period (DLP).** Clause 30 gives you a period of **24 months** after taking vacant possession to report any defects, shrinkage or damage due to defective workmanship or materials. (This period was extended from 18 months to 24 months by the 2015 Regulations amendment.) When you give written notice, the developer must repair "at its own cost and expense within thirty (30) days."

**The 5% stakeholder money.** To ensure the developer actually carries out repairs, the Third Schedule retains 5% of the purchase price in the hands of the developer's solicitor as stakeholder, released in stages throughout the defect liability period. If the developer fails to repair within 30 days, clause 30 allows you to repair it yourself and claim the cost from this stakeholder money.

**Strata title.** Clause 11 requires the developer to apply for a separate strata title and transfer it to you "as expeditiously as possible" under the Strata Titles Act 1985.

## If the developer fails: the Tribunal for Homebuyer Claims

Suing a developer in the ordinary courts is expensive and slow. The HDA creates a shortcut: the **Tribunal for Homebuyer Claims**, established under section 16B.

Its key features:

- **Claim limit.** The Tribunal can handle claims up to **RM50,000** (the limit was raised by an amendment in force in 2015).
- **Time period.** Section 16N(2) limits claims to 12 months from the date of the Certificate of Completion and Compliance (CCC) or the date of expiry of the defect liability period. For defects, the courts have clarified that the period is counted from the expiry of the defect liability period.
- **Without a lawyer.** Section 16U(2) generally prohibits legal representation except for complex issues of law — making it accessible to ordinary people.
- **Fast.** Section 16Y(1) requires an award to be made, where possible, within 60 days of the first day of the hearing.
- **Enforceable.** Section 16AC deems a Tribunal award to be an order of the Magistrates' Court. A developer who fails to comply with an award commits an offence and can be fined not less than RM10,000 but not more than RM50,000 (the rate was raised by the 2012 Amendment), in addition to imprisonment (section 16AD).

Who can claim? Section 16A defines "homebuyer" broadly — including a person who buys from the first buyer. So a secondary buyer of an unfinished unit is also protected.

## Decision framework: steps before signing the SPA

Use this checklist before you commit:

1. **Verify the licence and APDL.** Ask for the developer's licence number and Advertisement and Sale Permit; they should be recorded in the agreement and displayed at the sales office (section 7(b)).
2. **Ensure the correct agreement form.** Strata must use Schedule H; landed must use Schedule G. A developer's "in-house" agreement that is not Schedule G/H is a warning sign.
3. **Check the vacant possession date.** Count 36 months (strata) or 24 months (landed) from the date of the agreement and note it down.
4. **Do not pay any 'booking fee' before the SPA.** After the 2015 amendment, no one including the stakeholder may collect such payments before the agreement is signed (regulation 11(2)).
5. **Ignore prohibited advertising promises.** Regulation 8(1A) prohibits developer advertisements from stating free legal fees, projected profits or rental income, claims of panoramic views, and travel times to popular destinations. Such promises cannot be trusted.
6. **Keep all records of payments and notices.** You will need them to calculate LAD and to file a Tribunal claim on time.

## Common buyer mistakes

- **Signing a non-Schedule G/H agreement.** Some developers try to insert additional terms that prejudice the buyer. Terms that conflict with the mandatory agreement risk being void — but it is better to avoid them from the start.
- **Missing the 12-month Tribunal window.** Many buyers wait too long to claim LAD or defect costs, so that the Tribunal's jurisdiction under section 16N(2) has already expired.
- **Calculating LAD from the wrong date.** LAD starts from the expiry of the 36/24-month period counted from the **date of the agreement**, not the intended completion date promised by the salesperson.
- **Paying earnest money outside the contract.** "Booking" payments before the SPA are now prohibited; paying them weakens your position.
- **Assuming the developer's deposit protects everything.** The developer's deposit with the Controller (now 3% of the estimated construction cost) and the Housing Development Account help, but they are not a full guarantee against an abandoned project — due diligence before buying remains essential.
- **Not reporting defects in writing within 24 months.** Verbal notice is not enough; clause 30 requires written notice to trigger the developer's 30-day repair obligation.

## What next

The HDA 1966 gives buyers of off-plan homes a clear, calculable set of rights — but those rights are only useful if you know them and use them on time. Keep your date of the agreement, vacant possession date, and defect liability period expiry date all in one place.

If your unit is late, start calculating LAD from the first day after the 36/24-month period and send a written claim to the developer. If defects appear, report them in writing promptly within 24 months. If the developer refuses to pay or repair, prepare a Tribunal claim within the 12-month window allowed.
