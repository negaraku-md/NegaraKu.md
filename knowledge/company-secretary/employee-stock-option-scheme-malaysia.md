Startups short on cash and mature companies chasing retention reach for the same tool: give employees a slice of the equity. An Employee Share Option Scheme (ESOS) lets staff buy shares later at a price fixed today, so they share in the upside they help create. It is powerful — and it is also a place where founders quietly get the law and the tax wrong, usually the tax.

Here is how an ESOS actually works in Malaysia: the company-law steps to grant it, the extra rules if you are listed, and — the part that trips everyone up — exactly how and when the employee is taxed.

## What an ESOS is (and what it is not)

An ESOS grants an **option** — a right, not an obligation — to buy a set number of company shares at a fixed **exercise price**, usually after a vesting period. If the shares are worth more than the exercise price when the option vests, the employee exercises and pockets the difference in value; if not, they simply let it lapse.

That option structure is what distinguishes an ESOS from its cousins, which Malaysian tax rules treat differently:

- an **ESPP** (share purchase plan) is paid for by salary deductions over time;
- a **share award / restricted-share / RSU** scheme gives shares free or below value, and is taxed on the *full* market value at vesting;
- **share appreciation rights** pay out the gain in cash rather than shares.

Get the label right at the design stage, because it changes both the company-law mechanics and the tax bill.

## The company-law mechanics: two sections that matter

Before a single option is granted, a company must clear two provisions of the **Companies Act 2016**.

**Section 75 — authority to allot.** Directors cannot, on their own, allot shares or grant rights to subscribe for shares. Section 75(1) requires **prior shareholder approval by resolution** to grant options and to allot the underlying shares; an allotment made without it is void. In practice, the members pass a resolution approving the ESOS and authorising the directors to issue shares under it.

There is a helpful wrinkle for multi-year schemes. A shareholder mandate normally lapses at the next AGM — but **section 76(5)** lets directors keep allotting after that, provided the shares are issued under an option or agreement the company granted *before* the approval expired. That is exactly what keeps an ESOS running: options granted under an approved scheme can still be exercised into shares years later.

**Section 85 — pre-emptive rights.** Where a company issues new shares ranking equally with existing ones, section 85 says they must **first be offered to existing shareholders** in proportion to their holdings, to protect them from dilution. An ESOS deliberately does the opposite — it sends shares to employees. So the pre-emptive right has to be **waived**. Because section 85 opens "subject to the constitution," it can be dis-applied by the company's constitution or by a members' resolution; which route applies depends on the individual company's constitution.

## Extra rules if you are listed

A company listed on Bursa Malaysia faces a further layer, set out in the Main Market Listing Requirements. The headline limits:

| Rule | Requirement |
|---|---|
| **Maximum size** | Not more than **15%** of total issued shares (excluding treasury shares) at any one time |
| **Pricing** | Based on the 5-day weighted average market price, at a discount of **not more than 10%** |
| **Duration** | Not more than **10 years** |
| **Approval** | The scheme must be approved by shareholders in general meeting |

Eligibility is restricted to directors and employees of the group, and a participant who already holds 20% or more of the company cannot be allocated more than 10% of the scheme's shares. Note the common confusion: the **15%** figure is the ESOS-specific ceiling — it is *not* the general "10% in 12 months" mandate that applies to ordinary share placements.

## How it is taxed — the part everyone gets wrong

This is where a knowledge base earns its keep, because most explanations online state the rule slightly wrong.

A share benefit from an ESOS is a **perquisite from employment** under **paragraph 13(1)(a) of the Income Tax Act 1967**, and it is taxed **in the year the option is exercised** — not when it is granted, and not when the shares are eventually sold.

The taxable amount is set by LHDN's Public Ruling 11/2012:

> the **lower of** the market value of the share on the date the scheme first became **exercisable**, or the market value on the date it is actually **exercised** — **less** the price the employee paid.

The critical detail is the phrase *"the lower of."* Many guides say the perquisite is simply "the value at exercise minus the price paid." That is wrong: the law takes the **lower** of two dates, which usually works in the employee's favour. For a listed company, market value is the average of the day's high and low traded price; for an unlisted company, it is the net asset value per share.

A worked example from the ruling makes it concrete. An option is offered at RM1.45. On the first exercisable date the share is worth RM1.85; the employee actually exercises later, when it is worth RM1.95. The perquisite is the lower value, RM1.85, minus the RM1.45 paid — **RM0.40 per share**, taxed in the year of exercise. The later rise to RM1.95, and any gain when the shares are finally sold, has **no further tax consequence** for the employee.

## The employer's duties

The company carries the reporting load. Under Public Ruling 11/2012:

- **Notify LHDN within 30 days** of the option acceptance period closing — a notification under **section 83** of the Income Tax Act.
- **Report the perquisite** in the employee's EA form for the year of exercise, and submit the exercise details to the employer's LHDN branch.
- **Deduct the tax** through the monthly tax deduction in the month of exercise, or under a CP38 directive where instalments are arranged.
- **Keep records** as required under section 82A.

A note on the company's own tax: the cost of an ordinary ESOS is generally **not deductible** to the employer, though a special deduction exists where treasury shares are used, and case law has allowed deductions in specific group arrangements. Treat deductibility as a question for the company's tax adviser, not an assumption.

## What's next

If you are designing an ESOS, sequence it properly: settle the scheme rules and eligibility, get the **section 75** shareholder approval and the **section 85** waiver in place, and — if you are listed — build in the 15% cap, the 10% pricing discount and the 10-year limit from the start. Loop in the company secretary early; the paperwork is theirs.

If you are an employee holding options, the single thing to remember is *when* the tax lands: on **exercise**, on the **lower** of two market values minus what you paid — not on grant, and not on sale. Model that into any decision about when to exercise, and keep the EA-form entry for the year you do. For the underlying share-allotment rules, see the related guides on the Companies Act 2016 and on issuing shares.
