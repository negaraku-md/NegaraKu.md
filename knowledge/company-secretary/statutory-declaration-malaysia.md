Sooner or later a business owner is handed a document and told, "this needs to be a statutory declaration." It is one of those legal formalities that sounds heavier than it is — a written statement you swear is true in front of an authorised person — but the weight is real: sign a false one and you have committed a criminal offence. Here is what a statutory declaration is, who can take it, and where it turns up in company life.

## What it is

A **statutory declaration** — *akuan berkanun* in Malay — is a written statement of fact that the maker solemnly and voluntarily declares to be true. It is governed by the **Statutory Declarations Act 1960**, now reprinted as **Act 783**. (You may still see the old citation "Act 13" from the 1969 revision — that number is superseded.) The Act's purpose, in its own words, is "to provide for the making and taking of statutory declarations."

Unlike a contract, a statutory declaration is one-sided: it is simply your assertion of facts, given legal formality by being declared before an authorised officer.

## Who can take one

Under **section 2** of the Act, a statutory declaration may be taken by any of:

- a **Sessions Court Judge**;
- a **Magistrate**;
- a **Commissioner for Oaths** (*Pesuruhjaya Sumpah*); or
- a **Notary Public** (subject to the Notaries Public Act 1959).

In everyday practice it is a **Commissioner for Oaths** who commissions the declaration, for a small prescribed fee. Note one thing the Act does *not* say: a **Justice of the Peace is not listed** as able to take a statutory declaration under this Act, despite what some guides claim.

## The penalty for a false declaration

This is what gives a statutory declaration its force. **Section 3** provides that declarations made under the Act are deemed to be declarations under **sections 199 and 200 of the Penal Code** — the offences of making, or using, a false statement in a declaration that a court or public officer is bound to accept as evidence.

The punishment is by reference to the false-evidence provision: where the declaration touches a **judicial proceeding**, the penalty can reach **up to seven years' imprisonment and a fine**; in other cases, a shorter term applies. Either way, a statutory declaration is not a formality to sign carelessly.

## Where it is used in business

Statutory declarations appear across company and commercial life — verifying identity or facts, declaring the loss of a document, or confirming a state of affairs. But two clarifications matter for company work, because they are widely misstated:

- **Incorporation does *not* use a statutory declaration.** Under the **Companies Act 2016 (section 14)**, registering a company is done by an application in which each promoter or director gives a **statement** — consenting to act and confirming they are not disqualified. The old statutory declaration of compliance from the Companies Act 1965 was abolished.
- **A members' voluntary winding up *does* use a solemn declaration.** Under **section 443** of the Companies Act 2016, the majority of directors make a **declaration of solvency** — that, having inquired into the company's affairs, they believe it can pay its debts in full within 12 months — with a statement of affairs attached, lodged with the Registrar. This is the strongest genuinely statutory-declaration-style use in company law.

Separately, a director's **solvency statement** for capital reductions, share buy-backs and similar actions is required under **section 113**, and knowingly making a false statement in a Companies Act filing is itself an offence under section 591.

## What's next

If you are asked for a statutory declaration, prepare the wording carefully, make sure every fact in it is true, and take it to a **Commissioner for Oaths** to commission. If the document is a company filing, check whether what you actually need is a statutory declaration, a **section 113 solvency statement**, or a **section 443 declaration of solvency** — they are distinct, and your company secretary can point you to the right one. Above all, remember the stakes: a false declaration is a criminal offence, not a paperwork slip.
