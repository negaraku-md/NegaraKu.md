faq:
  - q: "Is founder vesting a legal requirement in Malaysia?"
    a: "No. There is no 'vesting of shares' in the Companies Act 2016 and no statute that imposes it. Founder vesting is a purely contractual arrangement — the founders agree in a shareholders' agreement (or founders' agreement) that some of the equity a founder holds can be taken back if they leave early, and they back that up with transfer restrictions in the company's constitution, which section 33(1) makes binding on the company and every member. The four-year schedule and one-year cliff that people quote are conventions from venture-capital practice, not rules of Malaysian law."
  - q: "What is 'reverse vesting' and why is it used for founders?"
    a: "In an ordinary option-style vesting arrangement (as with an employee share option scheme), a person earns the right to acquire shares over time and holds nothing until they exercise. Founders usually do the opposite: they subscribe for and are issued all of their shares at incorporation, are entered in the register of members, and enjoy full voting and dividend rights immediately — but the shares are subject to a contractual right of the company or the other founders to buy back the portion that has not yet 'vested' if the founder leaves before the schedule completes. Because the founder starts as full owner and the unvested shares can be pulled back, rather than earned up, this is called reverse vesting. It keeps the founder aligned and on the cap table while protecting the company if they walk away early."
  - q: "Can the company just buy back a departing founder's unvested shares?"
    a: "Not in a private company. Section 127 of the Companies Act 2016 permits a company to purchase its own shares only where those shares are quoted on a stock exchange — that is, a listed public company — and even then subject to a solvency test, a 10% cap and constitutional authority. A private Sdn Bhd therefore cannot buy back its own shares and cannot hold treasury shares. Reverse vesting is instead implemented as a compulsory transfer: the leaver's unvested shares are transferred, at the agreed nominal price, to the remaining founders, a new incoming person, or a nominee holding on trust, using the ordinary share-transfer machinery in sections 105 and 106."

Two people incorporate a Sdn Bhd, split the shares 50/50, and start building. Six months in, one of them loses interest and walks — keeping half the company. The remaining founder now carries all the work while a passive ex-partner sits on 50% of the equity, blocking the cap table any future investor will want to see clean. This "dead equity" problem is the single most common founder disaster, and the fix is vesting: making a founder's equity something they keep by staying, not something they own outright from the first day.

The trap is that Malaysian law gives you no ready-made tool for this. There is no "vesting of shares" in the Companies Act 2016 — nothing that says an unvested share behaves differently from a vested one. Founder vesting is built entirely out of contract and the constitution. Here is how it actually works, and why the mechanism used looks backwards.

## What "founder vesting" means — and what it is not

Vesting simply means a founder earns or keeps their equity over time rather than owning all of it unconditionally on day one. The market-standard schedule, imported from Silicon Valley and mirrored in UK venture practice, is **four years with a one-year cliff**: nothing is secured for the first twelve months (the cliff), 25% is secured at the one-year mark, and the rest accrues monthly or annually over the remaining three years. A founder who leaves in month eleven keeps nothing; one who leaves in year three keeps roughly what they have earned.

Treat those numbers as **convention, not law**. Four years, one-year cliff, monthly accrual — none of it is written in any Malaysian statute. Founders are free to agree three years, five years, a shorter cliff, or milestone-based vesting instead of time-based. The figures are a negotiating default that investors recognise, nothing more.

## Why founders use "reverse" vesting

There are two ways to arrange vesting, and founders almost always use the second.

- **Forward (option-style) vesting.** A person is promised shares and *earns the right to acquire them* over time — they hold nothing until each tranche vests and they take it up. This is how an [employee share option scheme](employee-stock-option-scheme-malaysia) works, and it needs shareholder approval to allot new shares under **section 75** and a waiver of the **section 85** pre-emptive right each time shares are issued.
- **Reverse vesting.** The founder subscribes for and is *issued all of their shares at the start*, is entered in the register of members, and holds full voting and dividend rights immediately — but the shares are subject to a contractual right of the company or the co-founders to **buy back the portion that has not yet vested** if the founder leaves before the schedule completes.

Founders prefer reverse vesting because they genuinely own their shares from incorporation: they vote, they receive any dividends, and — importantly for tax — the clock on the shares starts running at the low founding value rather than at some later, higher price. The shares are theirs to lose, not to earn. As each period passes, fewer shares remain subject to clawback, until at the end of the schedule none are. It is called *reverse* vesting precisely because it runs the opposite way to the option model: you begin as full owner and the unvested slice can be pulled back, instead of accruing upward from zero.

## Why a Sdn Bhd cannot simply take the shares back

The obvious way to reclaim unvested shares would be for the company to buy them back and cancel or park them. **A private company cannot do this.**

Under **section 127** of the Companies Act 2016, a company may purchase its own shares only where those shares are *quoted on a stock exchange* — a listed public company — and only then subject to a solvency test, a 10% cap, and authority in its constitution. Section 127 is a narrow exception to the general prohibition in **section 123** on a company acquiring or dealing in its own shares. A private Sdn Bhd falls outside it entirely: it cannot buy back its own shares and, consequently, cannot hold **treasury shares**. (See the related note on [share buy-backs and treasury shares](share-buy-back-treasury-shares).)

So reverse vesting in Malaysia is never a company buy-back. It is implemented as a **compulsory transfer of shares**. When a founder leaves, their unvested shares are transferred — at the agreed nominal price — to one of:

- the **remaining founders**, pro rata, so the active team's stake grows;
- a **new incoming founder or hire** brought in to do the work; or
- a **nominee or trustee** who holds the shares temporarily on trust for eventual reallocation.

The transfer itself uses the ordinary machinery: a duly executed and stamped instrument of transfer under **section 105(1)**, and registration of the transferee within the **section 106(1)** timeline. (See [transferring shares in a Sdn Bhd](share-transfer) for the full sequence.) The founder's shares are real shares moving to a real transferee — there is no treasury limbo for them to sit in.

## How founder vesting is documented

Because none of this is statutory, it only exists if it is written down properly, in two linked documents that do different jobs.

**The shareholders' agreement (or founders' agreement)** carries the commercial terms — the vesting schedule, the cliff, what counts as "leaving", the buy-back price (typically nominal, e.g. RM1 or par value for unvested shares), and the good-leaver / bad-leaver treatment. It binds the founders as a contract. On its own, though, a contract cannot force the register to change if a departing founder refuses to sign a transfer. (See [shareholders' agreements in Malaysia](shareholders-agreement-malaysia).)

**The constitution** supplies the enforcement teeth. A well-drafted constitution contains a **transfer restriction** and a **compulsory-transfer or pre-emption clause** — a "deemed transfer notice" that treats a leaving founder as having offered their unvested shares, and empowers a director or the company secretary to sign the instrument on the leaver's behalf if they will not. **Section 33(1)** makes the constitution binding on the company and each member as if they had personally signed and sealed it, which is why the restriction must live in the constitution and not only in the side agreement. The directors' power under **section 106(1)** to refuse to register a transfer that breaches a constitutional restriction is what stops a founder from selling unvested shares out from under the arrangement.

Leaver provisions usually split departures two ways:

| Leaver type | Typical trigger | Common treatment of unvested shares |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Good leaver** | Death, incapacity, or leaving by mutual agreement | Sometimes allowed to keep vested shares only; occasionally partial acceleration by negotiation |
| **Bad leaver** | Resignation before the cliff, dismissal for cause, or breach | Unvested shares clawed back at nominal value; sometimes vested shares too, at a discount |

These labels and their consequences are conventions the parties choose, not categories defined by statute — Malaysian law will enforce whatever the founders agreed, provided the drafting is clear and the transfer mechanics are valid.

## Why investors expect it

By the time a startup raises a serious round, vesting is rarely optional. Investors insist on founder vesting — often requiring founders to **re-vest** existing shares on new terms at the round — for three reasons: it keeps founders committed for the years the company needs them; it protects the cap table from dead equity held by someone who has left; and it gives the company a clean way to reallocate a departed founder's stake to whoever does the remaining work. A term sheet that finds no vesting in place will usually make putting it in a condition of the investment, so founders are better off agreeing it among themselves at the start, when the stakes feel abstract, than negotiating it under a live funding deadline.

## The tax and stamp-duty flags

Every clawback is a share transfer, so the usual transfer charges are in play — and the "nominal" price does **not** make them nominal. Stamp duty on a transfer of unlisted shares is assessed on the **higher of the consideration or the market value** of the shares, and a disposal of unlisted Malaysian shares can fall within the **capital gains tax** regime that took effect for companies in 2024, again by reference to value rather than the stated price. A founder transferring back shares "for RM1" can therefore still face duty and tax computed on what the shares are actually worth.

This guide deliberately does not restate the rates. For the stamp-duty computation, LHDN's valuation basis and the filing deadlines, see [transferring shares in a Sdn Bhd](share-transfer); that article also covers when capital gains tax bites on an unlisted-share disposal.

## What's next

Founder vesting is one of the few things worth documenting *before* there is anything to fight over. Agree the schedule, the cliff and the leaver treatment among the founders while everyone is aligned, then have a company secretary or corporate lawyer put the commercial terms in the shareholders' agreement **and** the enforcement clauses in the constitution — a side agreement alone will not move the register when a founder refuses to cooperate.

Because the mechanism is a compulsory transfer rather than a company buy-back, sequence the drafting with the transfer rules in mind: the constitution must let the shares move without the leaver's fresh signature, and the eventual clawback still has to run through stamping, registration and the tax checks.
