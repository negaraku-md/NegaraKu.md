Three ports, four changes of administrative status, and forty-one years. That is the Straits Settlements before it ever touches the Malay states at all.

Most summaries of this period jump straight to "the British ruled Malaya through Residents" as if it were one clean system, applied evenly, on one date. It was not. Penang, Melaka and Singapore were governed one way. Four Malay states were governed a different way, under a different title, from a different city. Five more states were governed a third way — and one of those five spent decades making sure it was never governed the same way as the rest.

The label "Straits Settlements" is the easy part. The layering underneath it is where the actual machinery of British Malaya gets built.

## Three ports, one administration — but which one?

Each of the three settlements arrived under British control separately, years apart, for different reasons.

| Settlement | How Britain acquired it |
| --- | --- |
| Penang | Occupied from 1786; the island's original Malay name gave way to "Pulau Pinang" once areca palms were planted after the British arrival |
| Singapore | Thomas Stamford Raffles signed a treaty with Sultan Hussein Shah of Johor on 6 February 1819 for trading rights; Sultan Hussein and the Temenggong ceded the island outright on 2 August 1824 |
| Melaka | Transferred from the Dutch under the Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 17 March 1824, in exchange for Britain's possessions at Bencoolen in Sumatra |

For a few years each port ran its own administration, with its own costs. In 1826, the East India Company folded all three into a single unit: the Presidency of the Straits Settlements, seated at Penang. It was the first time the three ports answered to one government — but "presidency" was a status the Company would not keep paying for.

## Downgraded within four years

The Presidency of the Straits Settlements lasted barely four years before Calcutta decided it was too expensive to run.

On 25 May 1830, the Straits Settlements was demoted to a mere residency under the Presidency of Bengal. The titles of governor and resident councillor were abolished outright. The number of senior civil servants was cut in half, from 19 to eight. Salaries were reduced and hiring frozen.

The demotion did not fix the underlying problem — it just moved the decision-making further away. Every administrative choice now had to travel to Calcutta and back, and from Calcutta, the settlements' concerns ranked low. In December 1832, Singapore overtook Penang as the seat of government, reflecting where the trade had actually gone. In 1851 authority shifted again: the Straits Settlements was removed from the Bengal presidency and placed directly under the governor-general of India. When the East India Company itself was abolished in 1858, the administration of India passed to the British Crown — and the Straits Settlements went on being ruled from Calcutta anyway.

That was the point at which the Straits mercantile community lost patience. Following the Indian Mutiny of 1857 the merchants petitioned the Colonial Office to take the Settlements over directly, writing of an Indian government ignorant of Straits affairs that had "shown a systematic disregard to the wants and wishes of their inhabitants." London approved the transfer in principle in 1858. It took nine more years to happen.

## Crown colony, at last

On 1 April 1867, the Straits Settlements was formally transferred to the Colonial Office and became a crown colony in its own right — authorised by the Straits Settlements Act 1866 passed at Westminster.

For the first time, the government answering for Penang, Melaka and Singapore sat in London, not Calcutta. A Governor, based in Singapore, now held real executive power: he could appoint judges and justices of the peace and direct policy without waiting on India. Lieutenant governors supervised Penang and Melaka underneath him. A Legislative Council, formed the same year, gave the merchant community — for the first time — a formal say in the laws made about it.

This is the version of the Straits Settlements that lasted the longest and did the most to shape what came next: a crown colony, run as a single administrative unit, with its own civil service and its own council. It is also where most popular accounts of "the Straits Settlements" stop. But the ports were never the whole story — they were the base Britain used to reach the peninsula behind them.

## The template moves inland: Perak, 1874

Nothing about Penang, Melaka or Singapore's status as a crown colony extended British authority into the Malay states next door. That took a separate instrument entirely.

On 20 January 1874, Raja Muda Abdullah of Perak signed the Pangkor Treaty (Perjanjian Pangkor) with the Governor of the Straits Settlements, Sir Andrew Clarke, at Pangkor Island. Its most consequential clause installed a British Resident in Perak, with full authority over the collection and administration of state revenue and general administration — except in matters of religion and Malay custom.

That single clause is the seed of everything that follows in this article. It is also, immediately, where the new arrangement was tested and found violent.

Perak's first Resident, J.W.W. Birch, was assassinated at Pasir Salak on 2 November 1875, while Muslims were celebrating Aidilfitri, in an uprising against the speed and manner of his intervention in Perak's internal affairs. The killing triggered what became known as the Perak War and hardened, rather than reversed, British resolve to extend the Resident system.

In the years that followed, Selangor, Negeri Sembilan and Pahang each received a Resident of their own on broadly the same model: an adviser whose advice on administration and revenue had to be followed, with religion and Malay custom carved out as the ruler's own domain.

Perak, meanwhile, built a consultative layer around its Resident that the Pangkor Treaty itself never specified. The Majlis Negeri Perak (Perak State Council) held its first meeting on 10 September 1877 at the Resident's residence in Kuala Kangsar, chaired by Raja Muda Yusuf, with Resident Hugh Low, Malay chiefs and Chinese Kapitans as members — proceedings conducted in Malay and recorded in Jawi. It was a genuine forum, not a rubber stamp: proposals were debated and voted on before becoming law. That council is the direct ancestor of today's Dewan Negeri Perak.

## Four states federate; five states do not

By the mid-1890s, Britain wanted one more layer: a single administration over the four Resident-run states together, rather than four separate ones.

Sir Frank Swettenham first proposed the idea in 1895. In 1896, Perak, Selangor, Negeri Sembilan and Pahang were brought together as the Federated Malay States (FMS), under a Resident-General based in Kuala Lumpur, which became the Federation's capital.

Centralising power in Kuala Lumpur had a direct cost for the individual State Councils that had grown up under each Resident: as the rulers were now made members of a Federal body instead, Perak's own State Council lost much of its earlier weight. That loss became explicit machinery on 11 December 1909, when a Federal Council was formed — concentrating decisions further at the Federation level and prompting some of the FMS rulers to withdraw from it in protest in 1927. Britain returned a portion of that authority to the individual states in 1930, and in 1932 the State Councils were revived as genuine legislative bodies with representatives from various communities and interests, the Resident continuing to advise while the Council debated and the Sultan assented to what it passed.

Five other Malay states never joined this Federation at all, and for different reasons:

- **Kedah, Kelantan, Perlis and Terengganu** only came into Britain's orbit in 1909, when an Anglo-Siamese Treaty — ratified on 9 July 1909, with the formal transfer effected immediately after — transferred Siam's suzerainty over the four states to Britain. Each then received its own British Adviser, outside the Federation.
- **Johor** held out longest and hardest. An 1885 treaty with Britain had already recognised Sultan Abu Bakar's line as sovereign rulers of Johor. When Britain pressed Sultan Ibrahim to accept a British officer in 1910, he accepted D.G. Campbell — but only as a "General Adviser," a title he insisted on precisely because every other state's equivalent post was called "Resident."

These five, collectively, became known as the Unfederated Malay States (UMS) — bound to Britain by individual treaties, without a Resident-General, a Federal Council, or Kuala Lumpur's authority over them.

## Four systems, side by side

Put together, the picture looks nothing like a single colonial system applied uniformly:

| Layer | Status | Central authority | Who joined |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Straits Settlements | Crown colony (from 1867) | Governor, reporting to the Colonial Office, London | Penang, Melaka, Singapore |
| Federated Malay States | Federation of protected states (from 1896) | Resident-General, Kuala Lumpur | Perak, Selangor, Negeri Sembilan, Pahang |
| Unfederated Malay States | Individually protected states | Separate British Adviser per state, no shared centre | Kedah, Kelantan, Perlis, Terengganu (from 1909) |
| Johor | Individually protected state | "General Adviser," a title distinct from Resident | Johor (from 1910) |

Four different labels, four different relationships to London, and only one of them — the Straits Settlements — was ever formally a colony in the strict sense. The other three all left the Sultans as the nominal sovereigns, with the argument in each case being over how much the adviser's "advice" actually had to be followed.

## Why the layering mattered later

This patchwork is exactly what Britain tried to erase in one stroke after the Second World War. The plan, known as the Malayan Union, inaugurated on 1 April 1946, folded the Federated states, the Unfederated states and two of the three former Straits Settlements — Penang and Melaka — into a single centralised unit, while Singapore was carved out as its own separate crown colony. The Straits Settlements, as an entity, ceased to exist that day.

The Malayan Union's flattening did not last: it was replaced, within a few years, by the Federation of Malaya — a framework that deliberately restored the individual standing of the Malay Rulers the 1946 plan had stripped away. That reversal is easier to understand once the pre-war layering is visible: 1946 was not creating a new problem so much as trying, badly, to solve an old one that this article has just walked through.

## Common mistakes

**"The Straits Settlements covered the whole Malay Peninsula."** It covered three ports — Penang, Melaka, Singapore — plus, for a period, some minor island dependencies. The peninsula's Malay states were governed under entirely separate arrangements.

**"All the Malay states had a British Resident."** Only four did: Perak, Selangor, Negeri Sembilan and Pahang, as the Federated Malay States. The other five had individual Advisers, and Johor's post was titled "General Adviser" specifically to avoid the word Resident.

**"A Resident replaced the Sultan."** No. Under the Pangkor Treaty model, the Resident's advice had to be followed on revenue and general administration, but religion and Malay custom remained the ruler's own domain, and the Sultan remained the formal sovereign throughout.

**"The Federated Malay States and the Straits Settlements were the same system."** One was a crown colony run by a Governor answering to London; the other was a federation of protected states run by a Resident-General in Kuala Lumpur. They ran on different legal footings and were dissolved into the Malayan Union on the same day for entirely different reasons.

**"Johor was governed just like Perak."** Johor never accepted a Resident. Its "General Adviser" arrangement, secured by Sultan Ibrahim in 1910, was a deliberately lighter tie than any of the federated or unfederated states around it.
