Long before any sultan sat on its throne, a slab of granite on a riverbank in the Terengganu interior had already written the state's destiny in stone. The Batu Bersurat Terengganu — the Inscribed Stone of Terengganu — carries a date that reads as the year 1303, and on its weathered faces, in the looping Arabic-derived letters of the [Jawi script](/arts-culture/jawi-script), a ruler commands his people to hold fast to Islam. It is the oldest dated evidence of Jawi writing and of Islamic law anywhere in the Malay world. The sultanate that would eventually bear the land's name arrived more than four centuries later — but the faith it would defend had been carved here first.

This is the story of how [Terengganu](/states/terengganu), on the north-eastern coast of Peninsular Malaysia, grew from one of the region's earliest Islamic footholds into a Malay sultanate — a branch of the Johor royal tree that weathered Siamese overlordship, British advice, and the birth of a nation, and whose throne stands to this day.

## The Batu Bersurat: Islam carved in 1303

The Terengganu Inscription Stone was found in 1887 at Kampung Buluh, near Kuala Berang in the district of Hulu Terengganu, reportedly after flooding exposed it on the bank of the Tersat River. According to UNESCO, the stele is "the earliest evidence of Jawi writing in the Muslim world of Southeast Asia," recording the spread of Islam and the growth of Islamic culture through a body of religious law.

The date on the stone is damaged and debated — scholars read it as either 702 AH (1303 CE) or 789 AH (1387 CE) — but the widely accepted reading, argued by the scholar Syed Muhammad Naguib al-Attas, is 1303 CE. In its Classical Malay text, a ruler styled *Seri Paduka Tuan* proclaims Islam the guiding faith and sets out a set of basic Sharia provisions, including rules on offences and penalties. A study in the University of Malaya's *Jurnal Fiqh* reads the stone as a window into how Islamic law was understood and applied in traditional Malay-Muslim society, even as some of its provisions bear the imprint of local custom.

Whatever its exact year, the Batu Bersurat shows that Terengganu was a settled Malay-Islamic polity generations before the modern sultanate. In 2009, UNESCO inscribed it on the Memory of the World International Register; the original is kept at the Terengganu State Museum.

## A branch of the Johor tree: the 1720s founding

The sultanate itself was born from the politics of the 18th-century [Johor-Riau Sultanate](/malaysia/johor-riau-sultanate), the great successor-state to Melaka. After the Melaka royal line ended in 1699, power in Johor passed to the Bendahara (chief minister) family. Terengganu's first sultan came directly from that house.

Tun Zainal Abidin was a son of Tun Habib Abdul Majid, the Bendahara Seri Maharaja of Johor, and a brother of Tun Abdul Jalil, who had been raised to the Johor throne as Sultan Abdul Jalil IV. Installed over Terengganu's territory by the Johor overlords — a step several accounts place around 1708 — Tun Zainal Abidin was consolidated as an independent ruler, Sultan Zainal Abidin I, in the 1720s. Many references give 1724 or 1725, with the installation backed by the Bugis-influenced Johor court of Sultan Sulaiman. (Sources disagree on whether to date the founding to the earlier installation or the later independence; the precise year remains contested.)

The lineage mattered. It made Terengganu, like Pahang after it, a direct offshoot of the Johor-Riau Bendahara line — one of the family of thrones that trace back, ultimately, to Melaka. Terengganu was thus woven into [the Malay sultanate system](/malaysia/malay-sultanate-system) from the moment of its founding, and Johor's influence over its politics lingered well into the century.

## Under the shadow of Siam

Geography placed Terengganu at the meeting point of two spheres of power: the Malay world to the south and the Siamese kingdom to the north. From the late 18th century, Terengganu — like neighbouring Kelantan, Kedah and Patani — fell within the orbit of Siamese suzerainty.

In practice this meant tributary ritual rather than direct rule. Terengganu periodically dispatched the *bunga mas dan perak* — ornamental trees worked in gold and silver — to the Siamese court at Bangkok, a gift that Siam read as a token of vassalage and that the Malay rulers often understood as diplomacy among friends. Internally, the sultanate governed itself; its rulers, religious scholars and chiefs ran the state's affairs, and Terengganu earned a reputation as a centre of Islamic learning and of fine Malay craftsmanship in textiles, brasswork and boat-building. This ambiguous overlordship would last until the European powers redrew the map.

## 1909: from Bangkok's orbit to British influence

The turning point came not through war but through a treaty signed far away. On 10 March 1909, Britain and Siam concluded the Anglo-Siamese Treaty — the Bangkok Treaty — under which, in the words preserved by the U.S. State Department's record of the agreement, Siam transferred to Britain "all rights of suzerainty, protection, administration, and control" over Kelantan, Terengganu, Kedah and Perlis.

Terengganu passed into the British sphere, but it did so on relatively light terms. Rather than joining the centralised Federated Malay States, it became one of the Unfederated Malay States, retaining a strong measure of self-government. Full British supervision came gradually: the sultanate accepted a British Adviser under a treaty concluded in 1919. From then until the Second World War, Terengganu was administered with a British Adviser alongside its sultan — a colonial relationship, but one that left the throne and the Malay administration formally intact.

## The modern house

Terengganu entered the Federation of Malaya in 1948 and became a state of independent Malaya in 1957, then of Malaysia in 1963. Under the Federal Constitution, its sultan is one of [the nine royal houses](/malaysia/nine-royal-houses) whose rulers form the Conference of Rulers and take turns, every five years, as the nation's elected constitutional monarch, the Yang di-Pertuan Agong.

That rotation has twice brought a Terengganu ruler to the national throne. Most recently, Sultan Mizan Zainal Abidin — who became Sultan of Terengganu in 1998 — served as the 13th Yang di-Pertuan Agong from 2006 to 2011, installed in a ceremony in Kuala Lumpur in April 2007 as one of the youngest kings in the nation's history. He remains the reigning Sultan of Terengganu, head of a royal house that has held its land for three centuries.

## Why it still matters

Terengganu's story compresses the whole arc of Malay-Islamic history into a single state. Its 1303 inscription stone is a national treasure — the physical proof that Jawi and Islamic law took root in the Malay world far earlier than once assumed, and a cornerstone of how Malaysia narrates the coming of Islam. Its royal house shows how the Melaka–Johor institution of kingship branched and survived, carried on the Bendahara line into a new state on the east coast.

And its path through Siamese tribute and British advice, without ever losing its throne, helps explain why Malaysia today is a federation of enduring sultanates rather than a republic. When the Conference of Rulers meets, or when a Terengganu sultan takes his turn as Agong, a line that began beside the Tersat River still runs through the heart of the modern state.
