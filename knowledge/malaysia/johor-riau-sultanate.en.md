In 1511, Portuguese cannon brought down a city — but not a throne. When the fortress of Melaka fell, Sultan Mahmud Shah, the last sultan of Melaka, did not surrender. He withdrew south together with the royal family, the chief ministers, and — most importantly — the entire institutional order that made Melaka a sultanate. The city was lost; the sultanate moved.

Seventeen years later, on the banks of the Johor River, that lineage took root again. This is the story of how the Melaka throne continued south through the Johor-Riau Sultanate — not as a new kingdom, but as the direct continuation of a Malay royal institution already more than a century old.

## From Melaka to Johor: an unbroken line

After the fall of Melaka, Sultan Mahmud Shah withdrew from place to place — Pahang, Bintan, and finally Kampar in Sumatra, where he passed away. The struggle to carry the throne forward fell to his son.

According to official records of the Johor State Government, "in 1528 Sultan Alauddin Riayat Shah, of the lineage of the Melaka sultanate, opened up Johor and settled at Johor Lama." He — also known as Raja Ali — was installed as the first Sultan of Johor, founding the state at Kota Batu, Johor Lama.

What matters here is not merely a geographic relocation. When Sultan Alauddin opened up Johor, he brought with him:

- **Royal lineage** — he was the son of the last sultan of Melaka, so the ruling bloodline of Melaka flowed directly into the Johor throne
- **Royal titles and protocol** — titles such as Sultan, the customs of installation, and Melaka's court protocol were carried forward
- **The order of chief ministers** — the offices of Bendahara (chief minister), Laksamana (admiral), and Temenggung (security chief), which formed the backbone of Melaka's administration

The Johor Sultanate, in other words, was not a new beginning. It was the [Melaka Sultanate](/en/malaysia/melaka-sultanate) continuing its life in a new location.

## Why "Johor-Riau"?

The name "Johor-Riau" reflects the sheer extent of this sultanate's territory at its height. Its influence was not confined to the modern state of Johor alone, but extended across the southern Malay Peninsula, the Riau Islands, and the Lingga Islands to the south.

The capital of this sultanate also moved repeatedly under the pressures of war and trade — shifting along the Johor River, and at one point to the Riau Islands. Because the seat of government once shifted between the Johor mainland and the Riau islands, historians often refer to this period as the era of the Johor-Riau Sultanate.

Throughout the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, Johor became an important trading power after the fall of Melaka, and was frequently caught up in a three-way rivalry with the Portuguese at Melaka and the Acheh Sultanate in northern Sumatra.

## Three dynasties, one throne

The Official Portal of the Johor State Sultanate organises the history of the Johor throne into three main dynasties. This lineage matters because it shows precisely when the Melaka line ended and how the institution was carried forward.

| Dynasty | Period | Beginning |
|---|---|---|
| Melaka-Johor Dynasty | 1528–1699 | Sultan Alauddin Riayat Shah II, son of the last sultan of Melaka |
| Bendahara Dynasty | 1699–1855 | Sultan Abdul Jalil Riayat Shah IV, of Bendahara lineage |
| Temenggung Dynasty | 1855–present | Temenggung Ibrahim, whose descendants rule Johor to this day |

### The end of the Melaka line: 1699

For nearly 170 years, nine rulers of the Melaka lineage occupied the Johor throne. That line ended in 1699 with the death of Sultan Mahmud Shah II — known in history as **Marhum Mangkat Dijulang** — without leaving a legitimate male heir.

This event became a major turning point. Because there was no direct heir from the Melaka line, the administration of Johor passed to the Bendahara Dynasty, when Bendahara Tun Abdul Jalil was installed as Sultan Abdul Jalil Riayat Shah IV.

According to the sultanate's official records, these transitions of the Johor throne took place "through negotiation, goodwill and reconciliation" — without significant conflict or harm.

## Territorial division: the 1824 Treaty

The territorial unity of Johor-Riau did not last. The Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824 (the Treaty of London), signed between Britain and the Netherlands, divided the two European powers' spheres of influence in the region.

The dividing line was drawn around Singapore: the area to the north — including Johor — fell within the British sphere, while the Riau-Lingga Islands to the south became the Dutch sphere. Following this treaty, the once-unified territory of Johor-Riau split into two: Johor on the Peninsula, and the Riau-Lingga Sultanate in the islands.

In this way, a sultanate that began as a continuation of Melaka was ultimately divided by a line drawn in London — not by Malay warfare, but by colonial diplomacy.

## Modern Johor: the Temenggung Dynasty

The Peninsula portion of Johor continued on its own path. In 1855, Sultan Ali Iskandar Shah handed over the government of the state of Johor to Temenggung Ibrahim — again, according to official sources, voluntarily and peacefully.

It was this Temenggung Dynasty that shaped modern Johor. The administrative centre was moved to Iskandar Puteri, later renamed Johor Bahru in 1866. This Temenggung lineage has continued to rule Johor to this day.

## Why it still matters

The Johor-Riau Sultanate is living proof that the Malay royal institution did not end with the fall of a single city. When Melaka fell in 1511, what moved south was not merely a fleeing sultan, but an entire form of government — lineage, titles, customs, and the order of chief ministers — which then took root again in Johor.

This is why historians regard Johor as Melaka's principal heir. Several other Malay states, such as Perak and Pahang, also trace their royal lineages back to the Melaka line. But it was Johor that directly carried forward Melaka's seat of government, bearing the institution across nearly two centuries until the original line ended in 1699.

When today's Federal Constitution preserves the institution of the Malay Rulers, or when royal ceremonies are performed at the state palaces, the historical roots trace back through Johor, and through Johor, to Melaka. A throne that began beneath a melaka tree around the year 1400 still channels its legacy into Malaysian statehood six centuries later.
