When Britain sat down to decide whether Malaya was ready to govern itself, one of the things it said it wanted to see was evidence that the territory's different communities could work a government together. The Alliance was the vehicle that came to the table claiming to show exactly that — three parties, each organised around one community, contesting elections as a single bloc and asking, jointly, for independence.

This article describes what the Alliance was and what it did in the independence negotiations. It does not endorse any interpretation of those events, and it does not characterise the communities the parties were formed to represent.

## Three parties, one ballot

The Alliance was not a single party in 1957. It was a coalition of three:

| Party | Formed | Contested as |
|---|---|---|
| United Malays National Organisation (UMNO) | 1946 | Alliance component |
| Malaysian Chinese Association (MCA) | 1949 | Alliance component |
| Malaysian Indian Congress (MIC) | 1946 | Alliance component |

Each party kept its own membership, leadership and organisation. What made them an "Alliance" was an agreement to divide seats among themselves and campaign together rather than against one another, so that in any given constituency voters faced one Alliance candidate rather than three competing communal candidates.

## How the coalition came together

The arrangement began at the local level. For the 1952 Kuala Lumpur municipal election, the UMNO and MCA branches in the city agreed an ad hoc electoral pact and contested jointly. The pact won nine of the twelve seats it contested — a result strong enough that the model was carried into further municipal contests rather than abandoned.

The Malaysian Indian Congress joined the arrangement in 1954, turning a two-party pact into the three-party coalition that would carry the "Alliance" name through independence. The coalition was only formally registered as a single political organisation on 30 October 1957 — after Merdeka. For the whole of the independence campaign, then, the Alliance operated as a working coalition rather than a legally constituted single party.

## The 1955 mandate

The first federal election for the Federal Legislative Council was held in July 1955. The Alliance won 51 of the 52 elected seats.

That result mattered for the negotiations that followed. A coalition that could show it had won almost every contested seat, across communities, could go to London and to the constitutional commission as the elected representative of the electorate rather than as one faction among several. Tunku Abdul Rahman, the UMNO leader, became Chief Minister of the Federation on the strength of it.

## The London delegation, 1956

In January 1956 a delegation travelled to London for a constitutional conference to settle the terms and timing of independence. It was led by Tunku Abdul Rahman and drew its elected members from the Alliance, alongside representatives of the Malay Rulers and the British government.

> The delegation put a specific question to Britain: when, and on what terms, would Malaya become self-governing? It came back with a target of August 1957, conditional on a workable constitution being drafted first.

The conference itself sat over January and February 1956. What the Alliance delegation secured was not the constitution — that was still to be written — but agreement in principle on a date and a process.

## The joint memorandum to the Reid Commission

The constitution was drafted by an independent body, the Reid Commission, convened in 1956 under Lord Reid and staffed by jurists from Britain, Australia, India and Pakistan. It invited submissions and received scores of memoranda from across Malayan society.

Among them was a joint memorandum from the Alliance. Because the three parties had to agree a single text before submitting it, that document represented positions the coalition's Malay, Chinese and Indian components had settled among themselves. It addressed five principal matters:

- the position of the Malay Rulers;
- Islam as the religion of the Federation;
- the position of the Malay language;
- the special position of the Malays;
- citizenship in the new Federation.

The Reid Commission's 1957 report drew on the memoranda it received, including the Alliance submission, in producing the draft that — after further negotiation — became the Federal Constitution that took effect at independence. The substance of these constitutional provisions is a separate subject; this article records only that the Alliance submitted an agreed joint position on them.

## The coalition as a negotiating format

The through-line from 1952 to 1957 is that the Alliance functioned as a format for reaching a single position out of three communally organised parties, and then presenting that position — at the ballot box, in London, and to the Reid Commission — as one voice. Whether that format is best understood as a compromise, a bargain, or something else is a matter of interpretation on which this article takes no position. What is documented is the sequence: an ad hoc 1952 pact, expanded in 1954, endorsed at the 1955 election, carried into the 1956 negotiations, and submitted as a joint memorandum before independence.

The Alliance continued as the governing coalition after 1957. It was later reconstituted and broadened into the Barisan Nasional in the 1970s — a separate development beyond the scope of this article.
