The New Economic Policy is usually remembered as a single word — "restructuring" — and argued about ever since. But the policy stated its own aims in writing, in a government plan tabled in Parliament, and those aims were two, not one, with a third idea sitting above both. This article sets out what the documents say, in the order the documents say it.

## The answer, stated plainly

The Second Malaysia Plan (1971-1975) declares national unity as the over-riding objective of the country, and sets out a **two-pronged strategy** to reach it:

- **First prong — eradicating poverty.** Raising income levels and increasing employment opportunities for all Malaysians, regardless of race.
- **Second prong — restructuring society.** Accelerating the process of restructuring Malaysian society to correct economic imbalance, so as to reduce and eventually eliminate the identification of race with economic function.

Two objectives, one overriding purpose. The Plan is explicit that the two prongs were meant to work together rather than in place of each other.

## The timeframe

The NEP was a **twenty-year framework, 1971 to 1990.** It was announced in the Second Malaysia Plan and then carried forward through the successive five-year Malaysia Plans of the period.

A detailed long-range blueprint followed. The First Outline Perspective Plan, covering 1971-1990, was published as part of the Third Malaysia Plan in 1976; it set out the socio-economic framework within which the NEP's objectives were to be pursued and put numbers to the two prongs.

| Marker | What it was |
| --- | --- |
| 1971 | NEP announced in the Second Malaysia Plan (1971-1975) |
| 1976 | First Outline Perspective Plan (1971-1990) published within the Third Malaysia Plan |
| 1990 | End of the twenty-year NEP framework |
| 1991 | Succeeded by the National Development Policy (NDP) |

## What "eradicating poverty" was measured against

The first prong had a headline number. As recorded in the Economic Planning Unit's later Outline Perspective Plan, the NEP's stated target was to reduce the incidence of poverty in Peninsular Malaysia from **49.3 per cent in 1970 to 16.7 per cent in 1990.**

The same official source records the outcome at the close of the period: poverty incidence stood at **16.5 per cent in 1990** — a figure the Plan then used as the baseline for the decade that followed.

Two points are worth keeping straight. The 49.3 per cent baseline and the 16.7 per cent target are Peninsular Malaysia figures. And the first prong was framed, in the Plan's own words, as applying to *all Malaysians regardless of race* — poverty eradication was not stated as an ethnic programme.

## What "restructuring society" was measured against

The second prong was about the pattern of the economy — who owned what, and who worked where — rather than about absolute income.

Its most-cited target concerned corporate ownership. The Plan set the aim of raising Bumiputera ownership of share capital in the corporate sector to **at least 30 per cent**, from a low single-digit share at the start of the period, with the balance held by other Malaysians and a reducing proportion by foreign interests. Alongside ownership, the restructuring prong aimed at employment: that occupations at every level and across sectors would, over time, reflect the country's ethnic composition, so that no ethnic group would be identified with a particular economic function.

The Plan's stated method matters as much as its targets. Restructuring was to be achieved through the **expansion of the economy** — a growing overall share to be redistributed as the economy grew — rather than by transferring existing assets from one group to another. The documented intention was that the process would not involve the dispossession of any community.

## The context in which it was written

Official history places the NEP's origin in a specific moment. Arkib Negara Malaysia records that the country introduced the Dasar Ekonomi Baru following the events of 13 May 1969, as part of a wider effort — alongside the [Rukun Negara](/en/malaysia/rukun-negara) and the restoration of parliamentary government — to rebuild national confidence and unity. The policy was driven by Tun Abdul Razak, then Prime Minister.

That places the NEP in the same period as two neighbouring events already covered here. The parliamentary suspension and the National Operations Council that governed in the interim are the same administrative backdrop against which [the Rukun Negara](/en/malaysia/rukun-negara) was drafted. And the demographic pattern the second prong set out to change is the subject of Malaysia's [population composition](/en/malaysia/population-composition).

## What the NEP was, and was not, in law

The NEP was a **policy framework expressed through national development plans**, not a single statute. There is no "New Economic Policy Act." Its objectives and targets live in the text of the Malaysia Plans and the Outline Perspective Plans, which are government planning documents tabled in Parliament.

This is a common point of confusion, so it is worth stating directly: the NEP set direction and targets; the specific legal and administrative instruments used to pursue it — in areas such as company equity conditions, licensing, education places and public-sector recruitment — were separate measures, some pre-dating the NEP and some introduced under it.

## Common misconceptions

**"The NEP had one goal."** The Second Malaysia Plan states two objectives — poverty eradication and restructuring — under one overriding aim of national unity.

**"Poverty eradication under the NEP applied only to one community."** The Plan's wording is *all Malaysians, regardless of race.* The restructuring prong was the ethnically targeted one; the poverty prong was not stated that way.

**"The NEP ended racial economic disparity by 1990."** The policy set 1990 targets and ran to 1990; the official record reports the poverty target essentially met and the corporate-equity target not reached. This article reports those documented positions without assessing them.

**"The NEP is still the current policy."** The twenty-year NEP framework ran 1971-1990 and was succeeded by the National Development Policy in 1991; later frameworks followed after that.
