On the northeastern shoulder of the Malay Peninsula, where the rivers run down to the South China Sea and the coast once looked as much toward the old kingdom of Patani as toward the Malay heartland, a throne was forged out of civil war. Its founder was not born a king. He was a warlord of Patani descent who outlasted his rivals, and the dynasty he began — the House of Long Yunus — still reigns in Kelantan today, more than two and a half centuries later.

This is the story of how that sultanate came together, why it spent so long sending trees of gold to a Siamese king, and how a treaty signed in Bangkok in 1909 — with no Kelantan signature on it — redrew the map of the state and set it on the road to becoming part of modern Malaysia.

## Before unification: Kelantan, Melaka and the pull of Patani

Kelantan is old. Archaeological traces of settlement reach back into prehistory, and early Kelantan was touched in turn by the trading and political worlds of Funan, Srivijaya, the Khmer realm, Majapahit, Champa and Siam. By the early fifteenth century it was a notable trading centre under an Islamic ruler, and in 1499 it is recorded as a vassal of the [Melaka Sultanate](/malaysia/melaka-sultanate), then the dominant power of the Straits.

When Melaka fell to the Portuguese in 1511, that southern anchor disappeared, and Kelantan drifted into the orbit of a different power: Patani, the Malay kingdom immediately to its north, in what is now southern Thailand. According to Encyclopaedia Britannica and other histories, local chieftains ruled a fragmented Kelantan and paid tribute to Patani, and by the seventeenth century most were effectively subject to it. The two regions shared language, custom, religion and ruling bloodlines; the border that now separates "Malaysia" from "Thailand" did not yet exist. This deep Patani connection is essential to the sultanate's origin — because the man who would unify Kelantan came from exactly that world.

## Long Yunus and the unification of the 1760s

The modern Kelantan state was welded together in the second half of the eighteenth century, out of a long contest among local rajas. The figure credited with that unification is **Long Yunus**, described in the standard accounts as an aristocratic warlord of Patani origin.

His rise ran through blood and exile. After his father was killed in 1756, Long Yunus fled to the neighbouring Terengganu Sultanate, whose ruler eventually backed his bid for power. Returning at the head of that support, he defeated his rivals and proclaimed himself Raja of Kelantan, becoming the founder of the modern state. Sources differ on the precise year — accounts place his accession variously around 1760, 1763, 1764 or the mid-1770s — but all agree the decisive unification belongs to the 1760s–1770s, and that it is from Long Yunus that the present royal house descends.

Long Yunus died in 1795, and for a few years Kelantan again fell under the overlordship of Terengganu. The dynasty's formal elevation came with his son, **Long Muhammad**, who declared himself **Sultan Muhammad I** around 1800 and was installed as ruler in the years that followed. With that title, a warlord's conquest became a sultanate — the institution that endures today.

## The age of the bunga mas

For most of the nineteenth century, Kelantan existed under the suzerainty of Siam (Thailand). This was not day-to-day rule from Bangkok — Siamese oversight of Kelantan was comparatively light, and the sultan governed his own realm — but it was expressed through one of the most evocative customs in the region's history: the **bunga mas dan perak**, the "flowers of gold and silver."

The bunga mas was a tribute of two small ornamental trees, one worked in gold and one in silver, accompanied by other costly gifts, sent roughly **every three years** to the Siamese court. Kelantan was one of several northern Malay states that sent it, alongside Terengganu, Kedah, Patani and others.

Crucially, the two sides never agreed on what it meant. To the Siamese kings, the bunga mas was a token of vassalage and recognition of their suzerainty. To the Malay courts, it was more readily understood as a gift of friendship between rulers. This ambiguity sat at the heart of the relationship — and it is why the events of 1909 could be read, depending on where one stood, either as the lawful transfer of a Siamese dependency or as the handing-over of a state that considered itself its own master.

## 1909: a border drawn in Bangkok

The turning point came not in Kelantan but at the negotiating tables of empire. By the early twentieth century, Britain — anxious about rival European influence in the peninsula — sought to formalise its hold over the northern Malay states. The result was the **Anglo-Siamese Treaty of 1909**, also called the Bangkok Treaty, signed in Bangkok on 10 March 1909, with ratifications exchanged in London that July.

By its terms, Siam transferred "all rights of suzerainty, protection, administration and control" over Kelantan, Terengganu, Kedah and Perlis to Britain. At a stroke, Kelantan passed from the Siamese sphere into the British one and became an **Unfederated Malay State**, with a British Adviser installed at the court. A follow-up agreement in 1910 required Kelantan to accept that adviser and barred it from independent foreign relations.

The treaty was negotiated between Britain and Siam alone. As the academic study by Mustafa Ahmad, Azmi Arifin and colleagues records, the ruling Sultan — Long Senik, remembered as Sultan Muhammad IV — protested that Kelantan was an independent state, a protest Britain rejected. The same treaty fixed a boundary along Sungai Golok, cutting Kelantan off from the Patani lands to the north with which it had shared so much. A single diplomatic line thereby separated two halves of one cultural world; disputes over that border echo in Malaysia–Thailand relations to this day.

## The House of Long Yunus endures

What is striking about Kelantan is how little the founding dynasty was displaced by all this upheaval. Through Siamese suzerainty, British protection, the Japanese landing at its coast in December 1941 — Kelantan was the first point of the Japanese invasion of Malaya — and the coming of independence in 1957, the **House of Long Yunus** held the throne.

That continuity runs unbroken to the present. The current ruler, **Sultan Muhammad V** (born 1969), acceded on 13 September 2010 and traces his descent directly to Long Yunus and Sultan Muhammad I. Between 2016 and 2019 he also served as the Yang di-Pertuan Agong, the elected federal king of Malaysia — a reminder that Kelantan's eighteenth-century warlord-dynasty now sits fully within the national institution of [the Malay sultanate system](/malaysia/malay-sultanate-system). The seat of the sultanate remains at Kota Bharu, the state capital that grew up around a royal fort in the nineteenth century.

## Why it still matters

Kelantan's story adds something distinctive to the family of Malay monarchies. Where [Melaka Sultanate](/malaysia/melaka-sultanate) and its southern heirs trace a classical Straits lineage, Kelantan's roots lie in the Patani north and in a long accommodation with Siam rather than with the Melaka line. It is a reminder that the Malay states did not all form the same way.

That history is also written into the map. The present border between Kelantan and Thailand, the bunga mas that ended only with British rule, and the cross-border kinship of the northeast all flow from the events traced here. As one of Malaysia's constitutional Malay Rulers, the Sultan of Kelantan embodies a continuity that reaches back to a civil war of the 1760s — a living thread connecting a warlord's throne to a modern federation.
