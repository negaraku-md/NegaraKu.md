Imagine a state with no files, no forms, no numbered departments. Orders were carried by word of mouth, justice was pronounced at the balairung, and the entire order of society rested on a single word that cannot be translated precisely into modern language: *daulat*. That was the world of the Malay sultanate — a system that ordered thousands of lives along coasts and rivers for centuries, and whose shadow is still visible in Malaysia's Constitution today.

This article explains how that system worked: the position of the Sultan, the bond that tied ruler to subject, the adat that governed daily life, and the ranks of chiefs who actually ran the government. It is a historical and structural account — not a political judgement.

## The Sultan at the top — but what did that mean

In the Malay sultanate, the Sultan was the apex of the order. He was head of state, head in matters of war and external relations, and — after Islam's arrival in the Malay world — head of the religion of Islam in his state. He was also a symbol of unity: a single focal point that turned scattered villages, rivers and tribes into one kingdom.

But that position was not merely an office. It rested on the concept of **daulat** — the ruler's authority and power, regarded as sacred. It was daulat that set a ruler apart from an ordinary chief, and the ceremonial cry of *"Daulat Tuanku"* is an acknowledgement of it.

In the tradition of the *Sejarah Melayu* (Sulalatus Salatin), daulat was not unconditional absolute power. It was bound to the ruler's justice. A tyrannical ruler was understood to be capable of losing his daulat — an idea that placed moral responsibility, not just entitlement, on the throne.

## Waadat: a pact that bound both sides

The foundation of the relationship between ruler and subject in Malay tradition was **waadat** — a pact of loyalty that was mutual, not one-directional.

The story is preserved in the *Sejarah Melayu* through the pledge between Demang Lebar Daun and Sang Sapurba before the former handed over his daughter. The people pledged never to commit derhaka (treason against the ruler) against the ruler; in return, the ruler pledged never to humiliate or oppress the people. Both sides were bound.

From this pledge came two concepts that became the pillars of the classical Malay political order:

- **Taat setia** (loyalty) — the subject's obligation to obey and remain loyal to a ruler who keeps his pledge.
- **Derhaka** (treason) — the act of defying or opposing the ruler, regarded as the gravest offence in the classical Malay value system.

Neither of these was merely a rule. They were how a society without modern bureaucracy maintained order: not through extensive administration, but through bonds of loyalty that everyone understood.

## Adat: law before there was a written code

Beneath the layer of ruler and chiefs, the daily life of Malay society was governed by **adat** — a body of norms, law and procedure passed down from generation to generation. Adat determined matters as practical as inheritance of property, marriage, and how disputes were resolved.

On the Peninsula, two main adat systems developed:

| Aspect | Adat Temenggung | Adat Perpatih |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main area | Most Malay states | Negeri Sembilan (and part of Melaka) |
| Power structure | Centred on the ruler/government | Builds upward from the smallest unit |
| Inheritance | Tends to follow the paternal line | Follows the maternal line (matrilineal) |
| General character | More centralised | More consensus-based |

Adat Temenggung organised society with power centred on the government, and became the pattern in most sultanates. Adat Perpatih, rooted in the Minangkabau, organised society from the bottom up — from the perut (family), to the suku (clan), to the luak (territory) — and passed property and titles down the maternal line. That is why Negeri Sembilan has its own distinct arrangement, with a ruler titled **Yang di-Pertuan Besar** chosen by the Undang (the territorial chiefs), rather than inherited directly as in most other states.

As Islam spread, syariah law merged with adat, and written codes such as the Hukum Kanun Melaka gathered part of it into textual form. For the origins of this institution, see [the Melaka Sultanate](/en/malaysia/melaka-sultanate).

## Chiefs: who actually ruled

The Sultan stood at the apex, but day-to-day government — collecting taxes, keeping the peace, adjudicating cases, running the ports — was carried out by the **chiefs** (pembesar). The most organised and most-cited model is the administrative system of the Melaka Sultanate.

In Melaka, four principal chiefs formed the backbone of the administration:

- **Bendahara** (chief minister) — the highest-ranking chief after the Sultan: chief administrator, principal adviser, head of the armed forces, and acting regent when necessary.
- **Penghulu Bendahari** (treasury chief) — managed the treasury and state revenue.
- **Temenggung** (security chief) — kept internal peace and security, including heading the police and the palace prison.
- **Laksamana** (admiral) — commander and head of the navy.

These four chiefs did not stand alone. They headed a layered structure known as the **Sistem Pembesar Empat Lipatan** (the Four-Tier Chiefly System): beneath the Pembesar 4 came the Pembesar 8, then the Pembesar 16, and the Pembesar 32. Each tier answered to the tier above it, forming an administrative network that connected the palace all the way down to district and village level.

This arrangement matters because it clarifies something often misunderstood: power in the Malay sultanate was not concentrated in one person alone. It was spread across chiefs who each had their own duties, territories, and followers. An influential Bendahara — such as Tun Perak in Melaka — could become the pillar that stabilised a kingdom.

## One special post: the Syahbandar

Because many Malay sultanates were port kingdoms, one more office stood out: the **Syahbandar** (harbourmaster). In Melaka, there were several Syahbandar, each overseeing a specific trading community (for example, Gujarati merchants, or merchants from China and the eastern islands).

This arrangement gave every trading community an official they could turn to, with predictable rules — one reason the Malay ports were able to attract merchants from across Asia. Administration, not just geographic location, was what made a port prosper.

## How it all fit together

Taken together, the order of the classical Malay sultanate can be understood as four mutually supporting layers:

1. **The Sultan** — the source of daulat, head of state and religion, symbol of unity.
2. **Waadat** — the mutual bond of loyalty that tied ruler to subject.
3. **The chiefs (pembesar)** — the layered ranks who carried out the actual administration.
4. **Adat** — the body of law and norms that governed daily life.

None of these layers functioned on its own. Daulat without justice could fade away; chiefs without a Sultan had no source of authority; adat provided the framework, but it was the ruler and the chiefs who upheld it.

## From the old throne to constitutional monarchy

This institution did not vanish with the arrival of colonial rule or with independence. It was absorbed into the framework of the modern state.

Today, Malaysia has nine Malay states with a Ruler, each carrying a different title according to its own history:

| State | Ruler's Title |
| --- | --- |
| Johor, Kedah, Kelantan, Pahang, Perak, Selangor, Terengganu | Sultan |
| Negeri Sembilan | Yang di-Pertuan Besar |
| Perlis | Raja |

Four other states — Melaka, Penang, Sabah and Sarawak — have no Ruler and are headed by a **Yang di-Pertua Negeri**.

These nine Malay Rulers sit on the **Conference of Rulers**, a body established under **Article 38 of the Federal Constitution**, with its functions and duties set out in the **Fifth Schedule**. Among its official roles are electing the **Yang di-Pertuan Agong** and the Deputy Yang di-Pertuan Agong under the Third Schedule, consenting to certain laws touching the privileges of the Rulers, and appointing members of the Special Court. All Rulers and Yang di-Pertua Negeri are members, but only the nine Malay Rulers take part in electing the Yang di-Pertuan Agong.

In this way, the core elements of the Malay sultanate — the ruler's position as head of state and religion, and loyalty as the foundation of order — continue in constitutional form, where the ruler governs within the bounds of written law. For the context of independence and nation-building, see [Independence 1957](/en/malaysia/independence-1957).

## Common misunderstandings about this system

- **Assuming the Sultan ruled alone.** In practice, day-to-day power was always distributed through layered chiefs; the Sultan was the apex of the order, not its sole executor.
- **Confusing daulat with absolute power.** In the tradition of the *Sejarah Melayu*, daulat was bound to the ruler's justice and to waadat — it was a two-way pact, not an unconditional entitlement.
- **Assuming all Malay states are alike.** Negeri Sembilan practises Adat Perpatih with a matrilineal structure and a ruler chosen by the Undang — different from the Adat Temenggung pattern in most other states.
- **Equating "Sultan" with every ruler's title.** Perlis is headed by a Raja and Negeri Sembilan by a Yang di-Pertuan Besar; the titles reflect each state's own history.
