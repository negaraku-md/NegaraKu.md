Every year, Malaysia's calendar contains two dates that are easily mistaken for the same thing: 31 August and 16 September. Both are marked with flags, parades and patriotic songs. Yet the two commemorate different events, six years apart, and describe two differently shaped countries.

The short answer: **31 August 1957 is the date the Federation of Malaya became independent from Britain; 16 September 1963 is the date the wider Malaysia federation was formed.** In 1957, a country called Malaysia did not yet exist.

## Two dates, two events

| | National Day / Merdeka Day | Malaysia Day |
|---|---|---|
| **Date** | 31 August 1957 | 16 September 1963 |
| **Event** | Independence of the Federation of Malaya from Britain | Formation of the Malaysia federation |
| **Covers** | The eleven Peninsular states only | Malaya + Sabah + Sarawak + Singapore |
| **Federal public holiday** | Since independence | Nationwide, decades later |

The core difference is one of scope. What happened in 1957 involved only the Peninsula. Sabah (then North Borneo), Sarawak and Singapore were still separate British territories, and did not take part in that independence.

## 31 August 1957: Malaya, not Malaysia

On 31 August 1957, the Federation of Malaya ended British rule after a negotiated process — a delegation to London in 1956, a constitution drafted by the Reid Commission, and a midnight flag-raising ceremony in Kuala Lumpur.

The full detail is set out in [Merdeka: How 31 August 1957 Actually Happened](/en/malaysia/independence-1957). What matters for this comparison is a single fact: the country that became independent that day was **Malaya**, a Peninsular federation. It was not Malaysia, and cannot be called that, because Malaysia did not yet exist.

## 16 September 1963: four territories become one

Six years later, Malaya joined with North Borneo (Sabah), Sarawak and Singapore to form a new federation named Malaysia. The merger was carried out under the Malaysia Agreement 1963 and took effect on 16 September 1963.

This process was not a tidy affair either — it was opposed by Indonesia and the Philippines, Brunei withdrew, and Singapore later separated again in 1965. The full background is set out in [1963: Building Malaysia Out of Four Different Territories](/en/malaysia/formation-of-malaysia-1963) and [Singapore's separation 1965](/en/malaysia/singapore-separation-1965).

## Why 16 September and not 31 August 1963?

This is a frequently asked question. The formation of Malaysia was originally targeted for **31 August 1963** — deliberately chosen to echo Malaya's independence date in 1957.

That date was postponed for two reasons:

- **UN mission.** A United Nations mission was sent to verify the views of the people of North Borneo and Sarawak on their participation. The date was delayed so that this work could be completed first.
- **Diplomatic fallout.** Indonesia under Sukarno and the Philippines each objected to the formation of Malaysia, and the postponement also helped manage the diplomatic fallout of that dispute.

As a result, the actual date shifted to 16 September 1963.

## "National Day" versus "Merdeka Day"

You will come across two names for the date of 31 August: **Merdeka Day** and **National Day**. Both refer to the same date.

The difference lies in emphasis. "Merdeka Day" refers directly to the event of Malaya's independence in 1957. "National Day" is a more all-encompassing term — it frames the 31 August observance as belonging to all Malaysians, including Sabah and Sarawak, which were not yet part of the federation in 1957. Official government documents and sources use both terms for the same date.

## Common mistakes

- **"Malaysia became independent in 1957."** What became independent in 1957 was Malaya. Malaysia was formed in 1963.
- **"National Day and Malaysia Day are the same day."** No. National Day is 31 August; Malaysia Day is 16 September.
- **"Sabah and Sarawak joined Malaysia on Merdeka Day 1957."** Sabah and Sarawak joined the federation on 16 September 1963, not 1957.
- **"Malaysia Day has always been a nationwide public holiday."** It became a nationwide federal public holiday only decades after the federation was formed.

## Why it still matters

The distinction between the two dates is not simply a matter of the calendar. It reflects how Malaysia was formed: a Peninsular federation that became independent first, in 1957, then merged with three other territories on their own separately negotiated terms in 1963.

For the people of Sabah and Sarawak, 16 September marks the date their territories entered the federation — and the terms of that entry, under the Malaysia Agreement 1963, remain a reference point for ongoing conversations about their autonomy and standing within the federation. The two dates, taken together, tell a more accurate history than either date standing alone.
