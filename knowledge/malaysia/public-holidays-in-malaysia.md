Plan a trip, a product launch or a payroll run across Malaysia and you quickly discover something that catches outsiders off guard: a day that is a public holiday in Kuala Lumpur may be an ordinary working day in Kuching, and vice versa. Malaysia does not have one national holiday list so much as a national layer and thirteen state layers stacked on top of each other.

That design is not an accident — it follows directly from what Malaysia is: a federation of states, home to several major religions, with a King and nine hereditary state Rulers whose birthdays are themselves holidays. This guide explains how the system works, so you can read any holiday calendar correctly.

## Two layers: federal and state

Public holidays in Malaysia are set at two levels.

- **Federal (national) holidays** are gazetted for the whole country — days such as National Day (Merdeka, 31 August), Malaysia Day (16 September), and the main religious festivals.
- **State holidays** are declared separately by each of the 13 states and the federal territories. These include each state Ruler's birthday and dates of particular local, religious or cultural significance.

Because of the state layer, the *total* number of public holidays — and occasionally the dates — differ depending on where you are. There is no single correct national number; it depends on the state.

## What the holidays reflect

Malaysia's holiday calendar is, in effect, a portrait of the country:

- **Religion.** Holidays mark the festivals of the major faiths — Islamic (Hari Raya Aidilfitri, Hari Raya Aidiladha, Awal Muharram, Maulidur Rasul), Buddhist (Wesak), Christian (Christmas) and Hindu (Deepavali, Thaipusam in some states), among others.
- **Royalty.** The birthday of the **Yang di-Pertuan Agong** (the King) is a national holiday, and each state with a hereditary Ruler also observes that Ruler's birthday as a state holiday.
- **Nationhood.** **Merdeka Day** (31 August) marks independence; **Malaysia Day** (16 September) marks the formation of Malaysia.

Dates for religious festivals that follow the lunar or other traditional calendars — especially Islamic holidays confirmed by **moon sighting** — move each year relative to the Gregorian calendar, which is why a fixed-date list is only ever valid for one year.

## Holidays and your pay: the Employment Act

Public holidays as a *day off* are gazetted under the **Holidays Act 1951**. Public holidays as a *paid entitlement for workers* come from a different law: the **Employment Act 1955**.

Under the Employment Act, covered private-sector employees are entitled to a minimum of **11 paid public holidays** a year — made up of **five compulsory holidays** plus **six more chosen by the employer** from the gazetted list. (Confirm the current figure against the Act, which has been amended in recent years; see the verification note on this page.) Employers may grant more, and public-sector holidays are governed by their own circulars.

## Why state differences matter in practice

A few concrete examples show why the state layer is not a technicality:

- **Deepavali** is a federal holiday observed in most of the country, but it is **not** a public holiday in **Sarawak**.
- **State Rulers' birthdays** fall on different dates in each state, so a holiday in Selangor is a working day in Johor.
- States such as **Kelantan** and **Terengganu** observe a Friday–Saturday weekend rather than Saturday–Sunday, which shifts how holidays interact with the working week.

For anyone running operations across multiple states — or simply travelling — the practical rule is to check the specific state's gazetted list, not just the national one.

## Holidays vs school holidays

Public holidays are distinct from the **school** calendar, which has its own term dates and mid-year and year-end breaks set by the Ministry of Education (and split between two regional groupings of states). Those are covered separately in the [academic calendar and school holidays](/en/education/academic-calendar-and-school-holidays/) guide.

## What's next

The key to Malaysia's holidays is to remember the two layers — national and state — and that many dates move each year with the religious calendars. Before you rely on any specific date, check the current year's gazette from the Prime Minister's Department and the relevant state, since the list is refreshed annually and varies by location. For the school-term companion to this calendar, see the [academic calendar and school holidays](/en/education/academic-calendar-and-school-holidays/).
