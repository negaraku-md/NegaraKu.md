For most Malaysians alive today, "the government" and "Barisan Nasional" were, for a very long time, almost the same phrase. From its first election in 1974 to its defeat in 2018, the National Front held the federal government without interruption — a continuous run of 44 years. This article traces how it was assembled, who belonged to it, and how that long run ended, keeping to the documented record.

## Where did Barisan Nasional come from?

BN did not appear from nothing. It grew out of the Alliance, the older partnership that had governed since independence in 1957 and that brought together three ethnically-based parties: the United Malays National Organisation (UMNO), the Malaysian Chinese Association (MCA) and the Malaysian Indian Congress (MIC).

After the racial disturbances of 1969, the leadership set out to widen that partnership into something larger and more inclusive of former rivals. Over the early 1970s the Alliance was reconstituted and broadened into Barisan Nasional, and the new coalition contested its first general election in 1974, which it won. The idea was a "grand coalition" that absorbed parties which had previously sat in opposition, so that most of the political mainstream governed together rather than competing.

## Who were the component parties?

BN's defining feature was its structure: not a single party, but a federation of parties, each retaining its own identity, membership and leadership while contesting elections under a shared symbol. Over the decades the membership changed, but the broad shape looked like this:

| Grouping | Examples of component parties |
| --- | --- |
| Founding core (from the Alliance) | UMNO, MCA, MIC |
| Peninsular parties that joined | Gerakan; PAS (1970s only) |
| Sarawak components | Parti Pesaka Bumiputera Bersatu (PBB), Sarawak United People's Party (SUPP), Parti Rakyat Sarawak (PRS), Progressive Democratic Party (PDP) |
| Sabah components | Various parties over time, reflecting the state's shifting party landscape |

Gerakan is a useful illustration of how the coalition absorbed former opponents. It had taken power in Penang in 1969 as an opposition party, then entered the BN fold and remained a component for decades.

## Why did PAS join and then leave?

One of the most-cited episodes in BN's early history involves the Islamic party PAS. In the 1970s PAS agreed to join the coalition and shared in the 1974 election result, with PAS figures taking places in the federal cabinet while the party held the Kelantan state government.

The partnership was short. In 1977 a political crisis erupted in Kelantan when PAS split, leading to a confidence crisis and a federal emergency in the state. PAS was subsequently expelled from BN, and a new party formed by former PAS leaders, Berjasa, was admitted in its place. The episode is often used to explain both the flexibility of the coalition model — parties could be added or removed — and the tensions that came with such a wide tent.

## How long did it govern, and what did that look like?

From its 1974 debut, BN held the federal government across every general election for 44 years, until 2018. Its dominance was such that changes of prime minister happened within the coalition rather than between coalitions, and for long stretches it commanded large parliamentary majorities.

Supporters and critics have long disagreed about how to characterise that era, and both readings appear in the public record:

- **One view** credits the coalition with political stability, sustained economic development and a power-sharing formula that managed a diverse, multi-ethnic society.
- **Another view** argues that its long incumbency blurred the line between party and state and made genuine turnover difficult.

This article does not adjudicate between those positions; it notes that they exist and that both are widely held.

## How did the long run end?

The turning point came at the 14th general election on 9 May 2018. The opposition coalition Pakatan Harapan won 113 of the 222 parliamentary seats, while Barisan Nasional won 79 — its first-ever loss of the federal government, ending a 44-year run in power.

The defeat reshaped the coalition itself. Within weeks, Gerakan announced it was leaving BN after winning no seats in the election, saying it would sit as an independent opposition party. In Sarawak, the four state component parties — PBB, PRS, SUPP and PDP — pulled out of BN on 12 June 2018 and regrouped as Gabungan Parti Sarawak (GPS), which was later registered as a legal entity in November 2018. What had been a large federation contracted, in some accounts, toward its UMNO-centred core.

## What's next

Barisan Nasional remains an active coalition in Malaysian politics after 2018, contesting state and federal elections and, at times, returning to state governments. Its post-2018 trajectory — including cooperation and competition with newer blocs such as Pakatan Harapan and Perikatan Nasional — continues to evolve, and later editions of this article will track those developments as they are documented.
