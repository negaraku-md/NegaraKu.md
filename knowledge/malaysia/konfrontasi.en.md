Malaysia was born on 16 September 1963 into a region that did not welcome it peacefully. A much larger neighbouring country had already declared its opposition, and within months the new Borneo border became the front line of an armed conflict that was never formally declared a war — but lasted for nearly four years. That conflict is known as Konfrontasi.

## Why Indonesia objected

Indonesia's President Sukarno regarded the proposed Federation of Malaysia — which united the Federation of Malaya with Singapore, North Borneo (Sabah) and Sarawak — as a neo-colonial construct designed to preserve British influence in Southeast Asia, and as a threat to Indonesia's standing in the region.

The term "konfrontasi" (*konfrontasi*) was announced as official policy by Indonesia's Foreign Minister, Dr. Subandrio, on 20 January 1963. The campaign was later summed up in the slogan "Ganyang Malaysia" — "Crush Malaysia" — rhetoric of that era expressing Indonesia's opposition to the formation of the federation.

The immediate background also included the Brunei Revolt of December 1962, an armed uprising that favoured a different political arrangement for the Borneo territories and was suppressed with the help of British troops. That event contributed to the tensions before Malaysia was formed.

## Manila, Maphilindo and the UN mission

Before the conflict fully escalated, there were diplomatic efforts to resolve the dispute. In late July to early August 1963, the leaders of Indonesia, Malaya and the Philippines met in Manila.

The meeting produced the Manila Accord (31 July 1963) and the concept of **Maphilindo** — a proposal for loose cooperation between Malaya, the Philippines and Indonesia grounded in shared Malay heritage. Among the terms agreed was support for self-determination for the peoples of Sabah and Sarawak, on condition that their views were independently ascertained before the federation was formed.

Following that, a United Nations opinion-verification mission was sent to North Borneo and Sarawak to confirm popular support for joining Malaysia. The date for the formation of Malaysia was moved from the original target of 31 August to 16 September 1963, partly to allow the mission to complete its work. When the federation was proclaimed on 16 September 1963, Indonesia and the Philippines did not accept the outcome, and Indonesia and Malaysia broke off diplomatic relations.

## "Ganyang Malaysia": how the conflict was conducted

Konfrontasi was not a conventional front-line war. It was conducted mainly through cross-border raids, infiltration and sabotage along Borneo's long jungle border.

The first recorded attack took place at the Tebedu police station, Sarawak, on 12 April 1963. In the early stages, infiltration was carried out by groups of "volunteers"; by 1964, regular Indonesian military units were also directly involved.

The conflict then spread to the Peninsula. In September and October 1964, Indonesia launched parachute attacks and amphibious landings in areas such as Labis (Johor) and Pontian — although these operations failed to achieve their objectives and many of the personnel involved were captured.

Malaysia defended its territory with the support of Commonwealth forces — including British, Australian and New Zealand troops — under the defence arrangements of the time. Commonwealth forces also carried out limited and secret cross-border operations (known as *Operation Claret*) to disrupt Indonesian positions within the territory of Kalimantan.

## How it ended

A political transition in Indonesia opened the way to a settlement. Following the events of 30 September 1965 and the political turmoil that followed, power gradually shifted from Sukarno to General Suharto.

Indonesia's new leadership, facing domestic economic pressure and a lack of international support for the campaign, opted for a diplomatic resolution. Peace talks began in Bangkok in June 1966.

Konfrontasi was formally ended through the **Jakarta Accord**, signed on **11 August 1966** by Indonesia's Foreign Minister Adam Malik and Malaysia's Deputy Prime Minister Tun Abdul Razak. The agreement ended the hostilities, restored diplomatic relations, and saw Indonesia accept the Federation of Malaysia.

## The lasting effects

In terms of casualties, the figures reported vary by source and are difficult to verify fully. According to the Australian War Memorial, Commonwealth losses included around 23 Australian personnel killed, while other historical sources estimate hundreds of casualties among Indonesian and Commonwealth forces overall. These figures should be understood as estimates, not final counts.

What has proved more lasting is the diplomatic impact. The restoration of relations after 1966 opened space for broader regional cooperation, and contributed to the climate that led to the establishment of the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) not long afterward — with Indonesia and Malaysia among its founding members. Today, Indonesia and Malaysia maintain close bilateral relations as neighbouring countries of shared heritage.
