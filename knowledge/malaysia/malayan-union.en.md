For one year and ten months, Malaya was governed under a constitution that today is barely known to anyone beyond its name. The Malayan Union began on 1 April 1946 and ended on 31 January 1948 — short enough that it never got around to leaving behind buildings, a currency, or a generation of administrators of its own.

Yet the two questions it raised were never closed again. Who was sovereign in the Malay states? And on what terms did someone become a citizen?

Both questions were answered once in 1946, answered differently again in 1948, and carried in modified form into the constitution of the independent Federation eleven years later.

## What happened on 1 April 1946

After the Second World War, Malaya was administered on a temporary basis by the British Military Administration. Its replacement framework was announced by the British government through a policy paper to Parliament in January 1946, and power was handed from the military to a civil administration on 1 April 1946.

The framework merged the Federated Malay States, the Unfederated Malay States, and the Straits Settlements of Penang and Melaka into a single administrative unit. Singapore was excluded and kept as a separate Crown Colony with its own legislative and executive councils. In the British Parliament, the separation was argued on the grounds of Singapore's distinct commercial interests and the composition of its population.

To enable this, the British Parliament passed the Straits Settlements (Repeal) Act 1946, which ended the Straits Settlements' status as a single colonial unit.

Arkib Negara Malaysia (National Archives of Malaysia) sums up the administrative effect of the Malayan Union as a change of status: from a protected state to a British colonial territory.

## The first constitutional issue: the standing of the Malay Rulers

Before 1946, Britain's relationship with the Malay states rested on a series of treaties with each Ruler. Its form was protection: the British advised, the Ruler remained sovereign on paper.

The Malayan Union framework changed that basis. To carry it out, a British mission led by Sir Harold MacMichael was sent to the Malay states at the end of 1945 to obtain the Rulers' signatures on new treaties surrendering jurisdiction to the British Crown. The mission's own methods became the subject of debate in the British House of Commons on 30 January 1946, in a debate devoted specifically to the mission.

Under the new framework, administrative power lay with a British Governor. According to Arkib Negara's records, the jurisdiction of the Malay Rulers was limited to matters of custom and Islam alone.

This was the core of the constitutional objection: not merely who governed day to day, but where sovereignty lay as a matter of law.

## The second constitutional issue: the terms of citizenship

The 1946 framework introduced a common citizenship across the merged territory, open regardless of descent.

During debate on the Straits Settlements (Repeal) Bill in the British House of Commons on 8 March 1946, the citizenship proposal was described along two routes: automatic acquisition through birth in the territory concerned, or through ten years' residence within the preceding fifteen years; and an application route after five years' residence, accompanied by an oath of allegiance. Arkib Negara describes it as citizenship granted to those born in Malaya and Singapore regardless of race, as well as to foreign residents who had settled for more than ten years, with equal rights including admission to the public service.

For the British government, common citizenship was a technical requirement for a unitary state eventually moving toward self-government. For those who objected, the eligibility terms were too loose and had been decided without adequate consultation with the Rulers and the Malay population. Both positions were recorded at the time, and the citizenship controversy itself has since been the subject of ongoing academic study.

## How the opposition was organised

The opposition took place across several channels at once, and was open and constitutional in character.

Intellectuals brought the issue into the Malay-language press, in outlets such as *Utusan Melayu* and *Majlis*. The first Malay Congress was held from 1 to 4 March 1946 at the Sultan Sulaiman Club, attended by 41 Malay organisations. Out of that process, the United Malays National Organisation was established on 11 May 1946 at Istana Besar, Johor Bahru, under the leadership of Dato' Onn Jaafar.

The official Conference of Rulers portal records that the Malay Rulers likewise rose together with the population in opposing the framework's implementation. The Parliament of Malaysia, for its part, records that the Malayan Union was fiercely opposed by Malays led by Dato' Onn Jaafar.

It should also be noted that the stricter citizenship terms in the 1948 replacement framework later became, in turn, grounds for objection from other quarters, including among non-Malays who found their eligibility narrowed compared with the 1946 proposal.

## The replacement framework: the Federation of Malaya

The Malayan Union ended on 31 January 1948. The Federation of Malaya was established on 1 February 1948, based on an agreement between the Malay Rulers and the British government.

Its design differences can be summarised as follows:

| Matter | Malayan Union (1946–1948) | Federation of Malaya (from 1948) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Head of central administration | British Governor | British High Commissioner |
| Standing of the Rulers | Jurisdiction limited to custom and Islam | Standing in their respective states restored; Conference of Rulers established |
| State form | Centralised union | Federation of states |
| Citizenship | Birth or a shorter residence period | Specific categories with a longer residence period |
| Singapore | Outside; separate Crown Colony | Remained outside the Federation |

Three bodies were established at the central level: the Federal Executive Council, the Federal Legislative Council and the Conference of Rulers. The Conference of Rulers was formally constituted in 1948, replacing the Durbar, which first sat on 13 July 1897 in Kuala Kangsar and was revived in August 1927.

According to the Parliament of Malaysia, the 1948 Federal Legislative Council consisted of 75 members — three *ex-officio* members, 11 representatives of the Malay states and the Straits Settlements, 11 British officials, and 50 unofficial members. This was the first time the council had a two-thirds majority of unofficial members. By 1955 its membership had grown to 98, with 52 members elected.

## The 1948 citizenship terms in more detail

The Hansard record of the debate in the British House of Commons on 4 May 1949 lists the categories that acquired Federation citizenship automatically. Among them:

- any subject of the Ruler of a Malay State;
- a British subject born in Penang or Melaka who had resided for 15 years within the territory of the Federation;
- a British subject born within the Federation whose father was born there or had resided there continuously for not less than 15 years;
- those born within the territory of the Federation who habitually spoke Malay and followed Malay custom;
- those born within the Federation whose parents were both also born there and who met the 15-year residence requirement;
- those whose father was already a citizen of the Federation on the date of their birth.

There was also an application route. Applicants aged 18 and above could apply to the High Commissioner by meeting a residence requirement — eight years out of 12, or 15 years out of 20 — alongside requirements of good character, competence in Malay or English, and willingness to take a citizenship oath. The language requirement could be waived for applicants over 45 years of age who had resided for 20 years and applied within two years of the provision coming into force.

Compare this list with the 1946 proposal and the fundamental difference becomes visible: the 1946 framework relied mainly on birth and a shorter residence period; the 1948 framework set out more specific categories, a longer residence period, and tied part of eligibility to subject status under a Ruler or to language and custom.

## Common misconceptions

**"Singapore was part of the Malayan Union."** No. Singapore was excluded from the outset and made a separate Crown Colony; it also remained outside the 1948 Federation. Its merger came much later, through the process described in [the formation of Malaysia 1963](/en/malaysia/formation-of-malaysia-1963).

**"The 1948 Federation returned full governing power to the Rulers."** Not entirely. The Rulers' standing in their respective states was restored and the Conference of Rulers was established, but executive power at the central level still lay with the British High Commissioner.

**"Malayan Union citizenship was granted without any conditions."** No. The 1946 proposal contained conditions of birth or residence, and, for the application route, an oath of allegiance. The difference from 1948 was the degree of strictness, not the existence of conditions.

**"The 1948 Federation meant independence."** No. It was a constitutional framework under British protection. Independence came on 31 August 1957 — see [independence 1957](/en/malaysia/independence-1957).

**"The Malayan Union was only a political issue, not a constitutional one."** Its debate turned on two matters that are very specific in legal terms: where sovereignty lay, and who was eligible to become a citizen. Both later re-emerged as written matters in the Federal Constitution.

## Why these two years matter

The events of 1946–1948 were the first time the question of the Rulers' sovereignty and the question of citizenship eligibility were set out in writing, negotiated openly, and settled through a constitutional document in modern Malaya.

The institutions born from it — the Conference of Rulers, a central legislative council with a majority of unofficial members, and a conditional federal citizenship — became the raw material later carried to the negotiating table by 1957, and into the Federal Constitution that came into force on Merdeka day.

Reading both frameworks side by side, not just one of them, is the most honest way to understand how Malaysia's constitution arrived at its shape.
