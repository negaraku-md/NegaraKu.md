Few places in Malaysia carry their past as openly as Kedah. Long before minarets rose over Alor Setar, the rice-green plain between the Merbok and Muda rivers was a landfall for traders riding the monsoon winds between India, China and the Malay world. Sailors and scholars knew this coast as Kadaram or Kataha; archaeologists know it today by the temple mounds of the Bujang Valley. Out of that cosmopolitan, Hindu-Buddhist beginning grew a Malay monarchy that would weather Srivijaya and Chola, Ayutthaya and Bangkok, and finally the British Empire.

What makes Kedah remarkable is not only its age but its continuity. The royal House of Mahawangsa still reigns, a living thread that runs from legend through to a constitutional monarchy inside modern Malaysia. This is the story of that line, told as the sources allow and careful where they disagree.

## Before Islam: the Bujang Valley and Kadaram

Kedah's documented history begins with trade, not with a throne. Along the northern bank of the Merbok River, around the settlement site of Sungai Batu, lay one of the earliest entrepots of maritime Southeast Asia. According to the account on the [history of Kedah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Kedah), most datable material from the area falls within the 1st millennium CE, with the much-quoted earlier dates treated cautiously as outliers. The Bujang Valley's *candi* (temple remains) show a society that absorbed Indian religion, script and statecraft while growing rich on the India-China sea route.

Early foreign texts call the place Kadaram or Kataha. Tamil and Arab sources treated it as a key node of the Srivijaya realm, the Sumatra-based thalassocracy that loosely held the straits from roughly the 7th century. That prominence made it a target: in the early 11th century the Chola emperor Rajendra I launched a naval expedition that sacked Kedah, and a later Sanskrit inscription of Kulottunga I, dated 1086 CE, was found in the valley. For centuries, then, Kedah was a Hindu-Buddhist port-polity caught between greater powers, an identity quite different from the sultanate it would become.

## The 1136 conversion and the founding tradition

The sultanate's own origin story comes from its court chronicles. The *Kedah Annals* (the *Hikayat Merong Mahawangsa*) relate that the reigning king, Phra Ong Mahawangsa, embraced Islam and took the royal title Sultan Mudzaffar Shah. The genealogy *At-Tarikh Salasilah Negeri Kedah* places that conversion in 1136 CE, crediting a visiting scholar with the king's instruction. From this moment, by tradition, the Hindu-Buddhist kingdom was restyled as the Sultanate of Kedah, with Mudzaffar Shah I reckoned the first of its line.

Historians treat the date with care. As the overview of the [Kedah Sultanate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kedah_Sultanate) notes, the *Kedah Annals* were compiled much later and are unreliable on the early sultans, and the 1136 date is widely considered unlikely given that Kedah still lay within the Srivijayan orbit at that time; the scholar Richard Winstedt, drawing on an Acehnese account, instead dates the conversion to 1474. Kedah is also frequently called the oldest Malay sultanate. Both claims — the 1136 founding and the "oldest" title — belong to tradition rather than to settled scholarship, and this article flags them accordingly rather than asserting them as fact.

## The House of Mahawangsa and a long dynastic line

Whatever its true starting point, the dynasty that claims descent from the legendary Raja Merong Mahawangsa has proved extraordinarily durable. Kedah's regnal lists run from a sequence of Hindu-era rulers into a line of Malay sultans numbering 29 to the present reign. That makes Kedah one of [the nine royal houses](/malaysia/nine-royal-houses) whose sovereigns form the electoral college of Malaysia's monarchy, and a prime example of [the Malay sultanate system](/malaysia/malay-sultanate-system) in which Islam, Malay custom (*adat*) and hereditary kingship fused into a single institution.

Kedah's trajectory ran parallel to, but separate from, the more famous [Melaka Sultanate](/malaysia/melaka-sultanate) that dominated the 15th-century straits. Where Melaka rose and fell within a century, Kedah's smaller northern kingdom endured by bending to whichever power held the upper hand — most often, its northern neighbour.

## Under the shadow of Siam

For much of the 18th and 19th centuries Kedah lived under the suzerainty of Siam. The visible token of that relationship was the *bunga mas dan perak* — ornate "golden and silver flowers" sent, as the entry on the [bunga mas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bunga_mas) explains, roughly every three years to the king of Ayutthaya. The two sides read the gift very differently: Kedah regarded it as a gesture of friendship, while the Thai court treated it as an acknowledgement of overlordship. That ambiguity sat at the heart of Kedah's politics for generations.

The pressure grew heavier in the early 19th century. Siam forced Sultan Dziaddin Mukarram Shah II to abdicate in 1803, and in November 1821 a Siamese army invaded and annexed the state outright. Sultan Ahmad Tajuddin Halim Shah II was driven into exile, the territory was fragmented, and direct Siamese administration followed. Resistance flared — Tengku Kudin briefly retook Alor Setar in 1831 before it was recaptured — but Kedah's room to manoeuvre was shaped from outside. The 1826 Burney Treaty between Britain and Siam effectively affirmed Siam's position over Kedah, and only in 1842 was Sultan Ahmad Tajuddin restored to a throne that now sat firmly within the Siamese sphere.

## The 1909 treaty and the British transition

The turning point came not from within Kedah but from an agreement between two empires. The Anglo-Siamese Treaty of 1909 — signed in Bangkok on 10 March 1909 and ratified in London on 9 July 1909 — saw Siam transfer its rights over the northern Malay states to Britain. Under Article 1, as recorded in the text of the [Anglo-Siamese Treaty of 1909](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Siamese_Treaty_of_1909), Siam ceded "all rights of suzerainty, protection, administration and control" over Kelantan, Terengganu, Kedah and Perlis. The practice of sending the *bunga mas* ended with the arrival of British rule.

The change was debated in the British Parliament almost at once. In the House of Commons on 21 July 1909, the Under-Secretary for Foreign Affairs confirmed that "the treaty was ratified on July 9th, and the formal transfer has now been effected," while insisting, according to the [Hansard record](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1909/jul/21/anglo-siamese-treaty), that "the integrity of the State of Kedah is not affected by the new treaty." In practice Kedah became a British-protected state, administered through a British Adviser rather than a Resident — a lighter touch that let the sultanate retain much of its distinctive character. It stayed outside the Federated Malay States, a status that shaped its politics well into the 20th century.

## Why it still matters

Kedah carried its monarchy intact through the Japanese occupation and the post-war reorganisation of British Malaya, and in 1957 it entered the newly independent Federation of Malaya — the nucleus of today's Malaysia — as one of its constituent states, its ruler one of the sovereigns who take turns as the nation's constitutional king. The living continuity of the line is embodied in the present ruler: as the profile of [Sultan Sallehuddin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sallehuddin_of_Kedah) records, he was proclaimed the 29th Sultan of Kedah on 12 September 2017 and formally installed at Istana Anak Bukit on 22 October 2018, succeeding his half-brother and tracing his own descent through his father, the 27th sultan.

To study Kedah is therefore to watch nearly a thousand years of adaptation without rupture: a Hindu-Buddhist port that became a Malay sultanate, a tributary that survived annexation, a protected state that became an equal partner in a federation. For modern [Kedah](/states/kedah) — Malaysia's "rice bowl" — the sultanate is not a museum piece but the institutional heart of the state's identity.
