## Three in four Malaysians now live somewhere DOSM calls "urban"

In 1970, most Malaysians were rural. By the 2020 Census, urbanisation had
reached **75.1%** — meaning roughly three in four people lived in what
Malaysia's national statistics agency classifies as an urban area, up from
70.9% a decade earlier and just 28.4% in 1970 ([DOSM, launching report](https://www.dosm.gov.my/portal-main/release-content/launching-of-report-on-the-key-findings-population-and-housing-census-of-malaysia-2020-);
[BERNAMA, quoting Chief Statistician Malaysia](https://www.bernama.com/en/news.php?id=2150549)).

That single number gets quoted constantly. What almost never gets explained is
what "urban" actually means when the Department of Statistics Malaysia (DOSM)
uses it — and that definition is the key to understanding both how fast the
shift happened and where it happened.

## At a glance

| Metric | 2020 | 2010 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| National urbanisation rate | 75.1% | 70.9% |
| Urban population | 24.3 million | 19.5 million |
| Rural population share | 24.9% | 29.1% |
| Urban households | 6.3 million (76.6%) | — |
| Rural households | 1.9 million (23.4%) | — |

*Source: DOSM, Key Findings of the Population and Housing Census of Malaysia
2020 — Urban and Rural.*

## How DOSM decides if a place is "urban"

This is the part most coverage skips, and it changes how you should read every
other urban statistic in Malaysia.

An area counts as **urban** under the 2020 Census methodology if it meets
either of two tests ([DOSM, HIS & BA 2022 Technical Notes, §7](https://storage.dosm.gov.my/technotes/hies_ba.pdf)):

1. **Gazetted area + built-up area test.** A gazetted area (a formally
   declared town, city or local authority boundary) plus the built-up area
   bordering it has a **combined population of 10,000 or more**. A "built-up
   area" is itself defined by employment: an adjoining area where at least
   **60% of residents aged 15 and above work outside agriculture**.
2. **Special development area test.** A non-gazetted development area that is
   physically separated from any gazetted or built-up area by more than five
   kilometres can still count as urban if it independently has at least 10,000
   people and the same 60% non-agricultural threshold.

Anywhere else — a gazetted town under 10,000 people, or land that isn't
gazetted at all — is classified **rural**.

DOSM also runs a finer four-way strata system for sampling purposes, which
explains why you'll sometimes see "Bandar besar" or "Bandar kecil" in Malay
publications:

| Strata | Population threshold |
| --- | --- |
| Metropolitan | 75,000 and above |
| Urban large | 10,000 – 74,999 |
| Urban small | 1,000 – 9,999 |
| Rural | Everything else |

For headline urbanisation figures, **Urban = Metropolitan + Urban large**;
everything else, including towns of a few thousand people, is folded into
"rural."

Two things follow from this that are easy to miss:

- **"Urban" is a population-density test, not a "this looks like a city"
  test.** A 12,000-person district town with a wet market and a few shophouses
  is urban by the same definition as Petaling Jaya.
- **The map of "urban Malaysia" is redrawn at every census.** DOSM's own notes
  say the urban areas identified in 2010 and 2020 "may not necessarily refer
  to the same locations," because places cross the 10,000-population or
  60%-non-agricultural line as they develop. Some of the 2010-to-2020 jump is
  genuinely new urban living; some of it is places that quietly crossed the
  threshold without changing much on the ground.

## Why the shift happened so fast

Malaysia's urbanisation didn't happen evenly — it tracks two overlapping
forces.

**Economic structural change.** As plantation- and mining-based rural
livelihoods gave way to manufacturing, services and government employment
concentrated around cities, the 60%-non-agricultural test in DOSM's own
definition mechanically pulled more places over the urban line. Urbanisation
here is as much a measurement of *what people do for work* as of *where they
live*.

**Boundary and reclassification effects.** Because gazetted boundaries expand
and new local authority areas are declared over time, some of the recorded
increase in "urban" population reflects towns being formally absorbed into
urban administrative boundaries rather than new migration.

**Concentration around one metropolitan core.** Unlike some countries where
urbanisation spreads across several regional cities, Malaysia's growth
disproportionately piled into a single conurbation — Kuala Lumpur and the
surrounding Selangor districts, commonly called the Klang Valley.

## The Klang Valley did most of the heavy lifting

Look at where the people actually are, not just the percentages. At the 2020
Census, four adjoining districts held ([DOSM, Administrative District report](https://www.dosm.gov.my/portal-main/release-content/key-findings-population-and-housing-census-of-malaysia-2020-administrative-district)):

| Area | Population (2020) |
| --- | --- |
| W.P. Kuala Lumpur | 1,982,112 |
| Petaling, Selangor | 2,298,130 |
| Ulu Langat, Selangor | 1,400,461 |
| Klang, Selangor | 1,088,942 |
| **Subtotal, four districts** | **6,769,645** |
| Malaysia, total | 32.4 million |

Those four districts alone — a small corner of the map — account for **more
than a fifth of the entire country's population**. Add the neighbouring
districts of Gombak and Sepang, which are also part of the built-up Klang
Valley conurbation, and the total climbs further still.

Selangor as a whole is now Malaysia's most populous state, with 6,994,423
people at the 2020 Census — a little over a fifth of the national total — and,
at 95.8% urban, it is nearly as urbanised as the two fully-urban federal
territories, Kuala Lumpur and Putrajaya.

At the other end of the scale, Kelantan (44.1% urban), Pahang (52.8%) and
Perlis (53.8%) remain the least urbanised states — a reminder that
"75.1% urban" is a national average sitting on top of very large regional
gaps.

## Internal migration isn't what most people assume

The instinctive picture of urbanisation is people streaming out of villages
into the capital. That pattern is largely over.

DOSM's **Migration Survey Report, Malaysia, 2024** — covering internal moves
in the 2023–2024 period — breaks *intra-state* migration (moves that stay
inside the same state) down by origin and destination strata:

| Intra-state move type | 2024 share |
| --- | --- |
| Urban → Urban | 84.6% |
| Rural → Rural | 7.6% |
| Urban → Rural | 7.2% |
| Rural → Urban | 0.6% |

Rural-to-urban migration — the move that built Malaysia's cities in the 20th
century — is now almost negligible in the within-state flow of people who
relocate in a given year. The overwhelming majority of movers are already
urban residents relocating to a different urban area, typically for housing,
jobs or family reasons. Johor recorded the highest share of internal migrants
in 2024 (70.3%), followed by Selangor (69.5%) and Sabah (68.7%).

The practical implication: once a country crosses roughly three-quarters
urban, the *remaining* rural population isn't a large untapped pool feeding
future urban growth. Most further urban change now comes from natural
increase, boundary reclassification, and urban-to-urban churn — not villages
emptying out.

## What urbanisation changes on the ground

The 2020 Census also captured demographic differences that follow from this
divide:

- **Household size.** Rural private households average 4.2 persons; urban
  households average 3.9.
- **Rural Malaysia is older, but urban Malaysia is ageing faster.** 7.3% of
  the rural population is aged 65 or above (2010: 6.2%), versus 6.6% of the
  urban population (2010: 4.6%). Rural areas crossed into "ageing" territory
  first — younger workers leave for urban jobs, leaving an older residual
  population — but the urban 65+ share climbed by two percentage points in the
  decade, nearly double the rural increase, so the gap is closing.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating "urban" as a synonym for "big city."** By DOSM's own test, a
  10,000-person town qualifies exactly like Kuala Lumpur does. Don't read
  "urban population" as "population living in major cities."
- **Assuming the urbanisation rate measures internal migration in progress.**
  It's a snapshot of where people currently live, built partly from
  reclassified boundaries — not a live gauge of village-to-city movement,
  which now accounts for a negligible share of within-state moves.
- **Comparing 2020 urban areas to 2010 urban areas as if they were the same
  places.** DOSM explicitly notes the underlying geography shifts between
  censuses as places cross the population and employment thresholds.
- **Reading the 75.1% national figure as evenly spread.** It hides a range
  from 100% (Kuala Lumpur, Putrajaya) to 44.1% (Kelantan).
