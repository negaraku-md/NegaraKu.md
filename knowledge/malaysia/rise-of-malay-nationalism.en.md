Before any major national party existed, the Malay movement began in school halls, in the pages of newspapers, and in the meeting rooms of small state associations. Between the 1930s and the end of the Second World War, it was these currents that slowly emerged into a political force that, in 1946, was able to unite Malays against a single British policy.

This article presents the historical record descriptively. It does not take any side; instead it sets out the facts and the various views that existed about this sensitive era.

## What sparked the rise of Malay associations in the 1930s?

By the late 1930s, a group of educated Malays began to form organisations "to seek and work out ways to advance and strengthen the position of the Malays". One of the most organised was the Selangor Malay Association (Persatuan Melayu Selangor, PMS), founded in June 1938 — its inaugural general meeting was held on 5 June 1938 and its formal registration on 4 August 1938.

The PMS was notable for its well-kept records, its ties with the Malay press especially *Majlis*, and a leadership more diverse than its contemporaries. According to academic studies, it also played a leading role in bringing about the two pre-war national congresses of Malay state associations. Other state associations — in Pahang, Perak and elsewhere — also emerged in the same period.

The character of these state associations was generally more moderate: they emphasised the socioeconomic advancement of the Malays, preserving the position of the rulers, and cooperating with the administration rather than opposing it directly.

## How did the Young Malay Union differ?

In the same year, 1938, an organisation of a different colour emerged: the Young Malay Union (Kesatuan Melayu Muda, KMM), founded by Ibrahim Yaacob. Most of its members were graduates of the Sultan Idris Training College (SITC) — an institution established by the British in 1922 to produce Malay teachers and junior officers, but which also became a place where anti-colonial sentiment was nurtured.

Ibrahim himself was born in Temerloh, Pahang in 1911 and studied at the SITC between 1929 and 1931. According to one account, the KMM was "vaguely Marxist" in ideology and opposed not only colonial rule, but also the leadership of the traditional elite. Its vision was Greater Malaya (Melayu Raya) — the unification of the Malay world, which envisioned linking around two million Malays in Malaya with tens of millions more in the Indonesian Archipelago. In 1941, Ibrahim published *Melihat Tanah Air*, a survey of the Malay homeland.

The following table summarises the two currents in general terms, without denying the overlap between them:

| Aspect | State associations (e.g. PMS, 1938) | Young Malay Union (1938) |
|---|---|---|
| Support base | The educated, officials, part of the elite | Teachers and young SITC graduates |

| Orientation | Malay advancement within the existing framework | Anti-colonial, leaning towards Greater Malaya |

## What was the impact of the Japanese Occupation on the Malay movement?

The atmosphere changed as war approached. Ibrahim Yaacob was detained by the British security authorities in October 1941, just before the arrival of Japanese forces in Malaya.

The Japanese Occupation lasted about three and a half years, from the fall of Singapore in February 1942 to the Japanese surrender to the Allies in 1945. Throughout this period, the experience of the Malay movement was mixed. Some nationalists saw the Japanese presence as an opportunity for independence and were prepared to cooperate, in the hope that Tokyo would eventually hand power to the locals.

In July 1945, Ibrahim Yaacob and several Malay nationalist leaders — some of them former KMM members — founded the Peninsular Indonesian People's Union (Kesatuan Rakyat Indonesia Semenanjung, KRIS), which championed the idea of uniting Malaya with a soon-to-be independent Indonesia. But the Japanese surrender in August 1945 ended the effort before it could take root. Ibrahim Yaacob later moved to Indonesia and spent most of the rest of his life there. That more radical aspiration of Greater Malaya did not come to pass.

The wartime experience — occupation, hardship, and the fracturing of relations between communities — left a deep mark that would later shape the political atmosphere after 1945.

## Why did the Malayan Union unite Malay opposition?

After the British return, London introduced the Malayan Union, which came into force on 1 April 1946. According to the record of the British Parliament's debates, to obtain the rulers' consent a British envoy, Sir Harold MacMichael, had secured their signatures on new agreements in late 1945.

Two features of the Malayan Union became the focus of opposition. First, it transferred the powers of the Malay rulers to a Governor, retaining their role only in matters of religion and custom. Second, it offered citizenship and equal rights to non-Malays who met the conditions of birth, residence and other requirements. In the British House of Commons debate on 17 April 1946, it was reported that "all the Malay Sultans had given notice that they could not recognise as valid the agreements they had signed with Sir Harold MacMichael".

Malay opposition took various forms, including:

- Large gatherings of Malays in several places, with participants wearing white cloth or white headbands as a sign of mourning for the loss of the rulers' political power.
- The rulers boycotting the installation ceremony of the Governor.

It was this wave that united dozens of Malay associations. It led to the founding of the United Malays National Organisation (UMNO) on 11 May 1946 in Johor Bahru, with Dato' Onn Jaafar as its first president. The Malayan Union lasted only about two years before being replaced by the Federation of Malaya.

## How do historians assess the legacy of this era?

Interpretations of the 1930s-to-1945 era vary, and readers should be aware of that diversity:

- **One view** sees the opposition to the Malayan Union as the birth point of modern Malay nationalism — the moment when Malays, for the first time on a large scale, acted as a single political community.
- **Another view** stresses that the roots go back earlier, to the state associations and the KMM movement in 1938, and treats 1946 as a culmination rather than a beginning.
- **An ongoing debate** also exists about how great the actual influence of the radical KMM current really was compared with the more conservative association current, given that the KMM's membership at the time was small.

This article does not favour any single interpretation. It sets out the verifiable facts and leaves readers to weigh them for themselves.
