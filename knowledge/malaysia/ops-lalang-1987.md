On the night the arrests began in late October 1987, the doors that closed were not only cell doors — three newspapers went dark within days, and the phrase "Ops Lalang" entered Malaysian political memory as shorthand for detention without trial. Nearly four decades later, it is still argued over: what it prevented, what it silenced, and what it says about the powers a state may hold over its citizens.

This article sets out the documented facts and the range of views around them. It takes no side.

## What was Operation Lalang?

Operation Lalang — commonly written *Ops Lalang*, from the Malay word for the fast-spreading *lalang* weed — was an operation carried out by the Royal Malaysian Police beginning on 27 October 1987. Over the following weeks, people were detained without trial under the Internal Security Act 1960 (ISA).

The most widely cited official tally is **106 people detained**; some accounts give a higher figure of **119**. Contemporary reports described the first day as an initial swoop of about **19 people** before the operation expanded — *The Star* of 28 October 1987 carried the headline that 19 had been "picked up in [the] swoop." Those held included opposition politicians, non-governmental organisation activists, trade unionists, educators, students, religious figures and others.

Detainees were held at the Kamunting detention camp in Perak, the facility long associated with ISA detention. Some were held for up to two years.

## Who was detained, and what were the newspapers?

The detainees spanned political and civil-society lines. Named figures reported across the sources include:

- **Lim Kit Siang** and **Karpal Singh**, then leaders of the opposition Democratic Action Party (DAP), among a number of DAP elected representatives
- **Chandra Muzaffar**, the first president of the reform group Aliran
- NGO members such as Theresa Lim Chin Chin and Br Anthony Rogers

Alongside the arrests, the government revoked newspaper publishing permits. The dates and titles are documented as follows:

| Publication | Language | Action | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| The Star (and The Sunday Star) | English | Permit revoked 29 Oct 1987 | Resumed publication 26 March 1988 |
| Sin Chew Jit Poh | Chinese | Permit revoked 29 Oct 1987 | Continued, eventually under new ownership |
| Watan | Malay | Permit revoked 29 Oct 1987 | Ceased publication in 1996 |

Accounts differ slightly on how the titles are counted — some describe "two dailies and two weeklies" — but the core list of affected mastheads is consistent across sources. Former staff have described months on reduced pay during the closures, and commentators have pointed to a lasting culture of self-censorship in the industry afterward.

## What was the Internal Security Act that made this possible?

The ISA was the legal instrument at the centre of Ops Lalang. It was enacted in **1960**, in the years after independence, against the backdrop of the communist insurgency and Emergency. Its defining feature was **preventive detention**: it permitted the authorities to hold a person without trial or criminal charge, for renewable periods.

Because detention did not require a conviction, the courts' role was limited largely to reviewing the legality of the process rather than the merits of the government's case. During Ops Lalang, several detainees filed *habeas corpus* applications challenging their detention.

The ISA remained in force for more than five decades. It was **repealed in 2012** and replaced by the Security Offences (Special Measures) Act 2012 (SOSMA), which provides for a shorter period of detention for investigation and which rights groups and the government continue to debate.

## Why did it happen? The competing accounts

This is where the record moves from documented fact to contested interpretation. Two broad framings coexist.

**The government's stated rationale.** The administration, then led by Prime Minister Mahathir Mohamad — who also held the Home Affairs portfolio — said racial and religious tensions had risen to a dangerous level and that action was needed to prevent communal violence. Tensions that year included a dispute over senior appointments at Chinese vernacular schools and a large pro-UMNO rally in Kuala Lumpur, at which some slogans invoked the May 1969 riots. In this account, the operation was a public-order measure taken to preserve stability.

**Critics' interpretation.** Opposition figures, civil-society groups and many later commentators argued that the operation coincided with, and was shaped by, an internal crisis in the ruling party. UMNO had split into rival camps — often labelled "Team A", aligned with Mahathir, and "Team B", associated with Tengku Razaleigh Hamzah and Musa Hitam — following a closely fought party leadership contest. In this reading, the ISA was used to suppress dissent and consolidate power at a moment of political vulnerability. Malaysia's first prime minister, Tunku Abdul Rahman, was among the critics; he argued that "a national crisis had to be created to bring UMNO together as a united force to fight a common enemy."

Both framings appear across the public record. A neutral summary is that the operation occurred at the intersection of communal-tension anxieties and an acute intra-party power struggle, and that observers weigh those factors differently.

## What was the legal and political aftermath?

The dispute inside UMNO moved into the courts. Litigation over the 1987 party election led, in 1988, to a High Court ruling that UMNO was an unlawful society on technical grounds. That same year, Malaysia experienced a **judicial crisis** in which the Lord President, Tun Salleh Abas, and Supreme Court judges Tan Sri Wan Suleiman and Datuk George Seah were suspended and then removed from office by tribunals. A later Panel of Eminent Persons reviewing those events concluded that the removals were unconstitutional and that Salleh Abas had been performing his constitutional duty to uphold the separation of powers. Commentators differ on how directly this judicial episode should be tied to Ops Lalang; it is commonly studied alongside it as part of the same period of executive–judiciary tension.

Most detainees were released over the following months, with the last released in 1989. The episode became a recurring reference in Malaysian debates over civil liberties, press freedom and the use of preventive detention.

On accountability, the public position evolved over time. In **February 2014**, Mahathir Mohamad said that responsibility for the operation lay with the Cabinet and that he accepted it, stating that the aim had been to ensure stability and peace. Critics, including some former detainees and opposition leaders, said this fell short of a full apology, and calls for a more explicit acknowledgment continued in later years.

## What's next

Ops Lalang endures less as a settled historical entry than as a live reference point. It is invoked whenever Malaysia debates security laws, press licensing or detention without trial — and the repeal of the ISA in 2012 did not end those debates, since its successor legislation remains contested.
