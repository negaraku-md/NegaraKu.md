For decades, every reference book gave the same number: Mount Kinabalu, Malaysia's highest peak, stood 4,101 metres above sea level. Then in 1997, a survey team pointed satellites at the summit instead of theodolites, and the number changed. Not by a rounding error — by six metres. The mountain hadn't shrunk. The old measurement had simply been wrong, and nobody had had the tool to prove it until GPS made the correction possible.

That kind of quiet re-measurement is common with old benchmarks, but it matters here because Low's Peak, Kinabalu's summit, is a genuinely contested piece of real estate: it's the point every guidebook, every constitutional reference to Sabah, and every UNESCO citation calls Malaysia's roof.

## The number, verified

The current, official elevation is 4,095 metres, measured at Low's Peak — the figure Sabah Parks, the state agency that manages the mountain, now publishes, and the one the IUCN's own assessment of the property confirms. The 1997 resurvey used satellite positioning rather than the older ground-based triangulation, and it is the resurvey's figure, not the pre-1997 4,101 m, that is now treated as authoritative.

Low's Peak itself is named after Hugh Low, a British colonial administrator who led one of the earliest recorded attempts partway up the mountain — he judged the final approach "inaccessible to any but winged animals" and turned back. The true summit was not reached until a later nineteenth-century expedition, but the peak kept his name regardless.

## A mountain built from young rock

Mount Kinabalu isn't a volcano. It's the exposed top of a granite pluton — a mass of magma that cooled underground and was later pushed upward as the rock above it eroded away. What makes it unusual is how recently that cooling happened: the granite is dated to roughly 7 to 8 million years old, which makes it one of the youngest exposed granitic plutons documented anywhere on Earth, according to the International Union of Geological Sciences, which lists it among the world's recognised geological heritage sites.

The uplift that keeps pushing the mountain higher is linked to the loss of a dense root of rock beneath Sabah around the same period — once that weight was gone, the crust above it rose. The mountain also carries a more recent signature: glacial features, including till deposits at the Pinousuk Plain, evidence that Kinabalu carried ice in the geologically recent past despite sitting just a few degrees north of the Equator.

## Protected on three separate counts

Kinabalu Park was gazetted in 1964 as one of Sabah's first national parks, built on the back of Royal Society scientific expeditions to the mountain earlier that decade. In December 2000, UNESCO inscribed it as a World Heritage Site — Malaysia's first — under natural criteria (ix) and (x): recognition of its ongoing ecological and evolutionary processes, and of its exceptional biodiversity and habitat for species conservation. The inscribed property covers about 75,370 hectares (753.7 km²).

That World Heritage listing sits alongside two other UNESCO designations — a Global Geopark and a Biosphere Reserve — making Kinabalu one of a small number of sites worldwide to hold all three at once.

| Designation | What it recognises |
|---|---|
| Kinabalu Park (1964) | Sabah's first gazetted national park |
| UNESCO World Heritage Site (2000) | Criteria (ix) and (x) — ecological processes and biodiversity |
| UNESCO Global Geopark | The mountain's granite and glacial geology |
| UNESCO Biosphere Reserve | Conservation and sustainable-use zoning |

The altitude gradient inside the park — from lowland rainforest up through montane forest to bare alpine granite at the summit — packs an outsized range of habitats into a small area. UNESCO's advisory assessments describe Kinabalu as the highest mountain between the Himalayas and New Guinea, and the park is credited with an estimated 5,000-6,000 species of vascular plants, including roughly 1,000 orchid species, alongside high numbers of bird and mammal species for its size.

## Common mistakes

**Citing 4,101 m as current.** That figure was superseded by the 1997 satellite resurvey; current official sources use 4,095 m.

**Assuming it's a volcano.** Kinabalu is a granite intrusion exposed by uplift and erosion, not a volcanic cone — there is no magma chamber feeding an active vent.

**Treating the climb as unrestricted.** Sabah Parks caps the number of climbers permitted onto the summit trail each day and requires a registered guide; the exact daily ceiling has been adjusted more than once in recent years, so climbers should confirm the current quota and fee directly with Sabah Parks rather than relying on older trip reports.
