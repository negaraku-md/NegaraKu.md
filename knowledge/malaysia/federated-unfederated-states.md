Nine Malay states, one imperial power — and yet not one arrangement but two. That split is the thing most summaries of colonial Malaya skip past, and it is the whole point of this article.

Before the Second World War, the peninsula's Malay states were governed by Britain in two distinct groupings, under two different instruments, from two different centres of gravity. Four states were bound tightly together and run almost as one. Five kept their distance and were dealt with one by one. The officer sitting beside the ruler had a different title, and a different reach, depending on which group the state was in.

Understanding which states were where — and how a Resident differed from an Adviser — is the clearest way to see how British Malaya was actually assembled.

## The starting point: one treaty, one Resident

The whole structure traces back to a single agreement.

On 20 January 1874, the Pangkor Treaty (Perjanjian Pangkor) was signed at Pangkor Island between a Perak claimant to the throne and the Governor of the Straits Settlements. Its central clause installed a British Resident in Perak — the first in any Malay state — with authority over the collection of revenue and general administration, but with Islam and Malay custom reserved to the ruler.

That reservation is the pattern for everything that follows. In neither grouping did the British officer touch religion or Malay custom, and in neither grouping did he formally replace the Sultan. What varied, from state to state, was how binding his advice was and how much of the state's machinery he sat atop.

Over the following years Selangor, Negeri Sembilan and Pahang each accepted a Resident on broadly the Perak model. Four states now shared the same kind of arrangement — and Britain's next move was to weld them into one.

## The Federated Malay States: four states, one centre

By the mid-1890s Britain wanted a single administration over the four Resident-run states rather than four separate ones. Sir Frank Swettenham proposed the federation in 1895, and in 1896 **Perak, Selangor, Negeri Sembilan and Pahang** were joined as the Federated Malay States (FMS).

The federation added a layer the individual treaties never had:

- a **Resident-General** based in **Kuala Lumpur**, which became the federal capital;
- a shared federal bureaucracy — railways, public works, a common civil service — spanning all four states;
- from 1909, a **Federal Council** that concentrated decision-making at the federal level.

Each state kept its Sultan and its own British Resident. But the centre of gravity had moved to Kuala Lumpur. Decisions that had once been debated in each state's own council increasingly flowed through the federal machinery instead — a shift that prompted some of the FMS rulers to press, in later decades, for authority to be returned to their states.

This is the defining feature of the FMS: not merely that each had a Resident, but that the four were administered *together*, as one connected unit, from one capital.

## The Unfederated Malay States: five states, five separate ties

Five other Malay states never joined that federation. Collectively they became known as the Unfederated Malay States (UMS): **Johor, Kedah, Kelantan, Perlis and Terengganu.**

They arrived in Britain's orbit later, and separately.

- **Kedah, Kelantan, Perlis and Terengganu** had been under Siamese suzerainty. Under the Anglo-Siamese Treaty, ratified on 9 July 1909, Siam transferred its rights of suzerainty, protection and administration over these territories to Britain. Each state then received its own British Adviser.
- **Johor** came in on its own track entirely. An 1885 treaty had already recognised Sultan Abu Bakar as the sovereign ruler of Johor. When Britain pressed for a British officer, Sultan Ibrahim accepted D.G. Campbell in 1910 — but had him appointed **General Adviser**, a title he insisted upon precisely because it was *not* the "Resident" that every other state's officer was called.

What united these five was structural: each dealt with Britain individually, through its own Adviser, and none was folded into a shared federal government. There was no Resident-General over them, no Federal Council binding them, no Kuala Lumpur exercising authority across them. In practice the UMS rulers retained more of their own administration than their Federated counterparts.

## Resident versus Adviser: the difference that mattered

The two titles are easy to blur. The distinction is best drawn on two axes: how binding the advice was, and how centralised the administration was.

| | Federated Malay States | Unfederated Malay States |
| --- | --- | --- |
| States | Perak, Selangor, Negeri Sembilan, Pahang | Johor, Kedah, Kelantan, Perlis, Terengganu |
| British officer | Resident (one per state) | Adviser (one per state); "General Adviser" in Johor |
| Central authority | Resident-General, Kuala Lumpur | None — each state tied to Britain separately |
| Shared federal council | Yes, from 1909 | No |
| Common bureaucracy | Yes — federal civil service, railways, works | No — each state ran its own |
| Degree of state autonomy | Lower; decisions pulled to the federal centre | Higher; more administration retained by the state |
| Reserved to the ruler | Islam and Malay custom | Islam and Malay custom |
| The Sultan | Remained sovereign ruler | Remained sovereign ruler |

The single most important row is the shared one at the bottom: in *both* systems, the Sultan remained the sovereign ruler of his state, and Islam and Malay custom were his. The colonial officer, whether Resident or Adviser, was formally there to advise. The real difference between the two groupings was not whether the Sultan reigned — he did in every case — but how much of his state's day-to-day administration was drawn into a shared British-run centre.

## Why Johor's title was not a trivial detail

It is tempting to treat "Resident" and "General Adviser" as interchangeable words. They were not treated that way at the time.

By choosing "General Adviser," Johor signalled that its arrangement with Britain was its own — later than the others, negotiated from the standing of a state whose sovereignty an earlier treaty had already affirmed, and deliberately worded to sit apart from the Resident system that governed the four Federated states. The title was a marker of degree: an adviser to be consulted, framed differently from an officer whose advice the Federated treaties bound the ruler to accept.

For a state that entered the arrangement last, the wording carried weight well beyond semantics.

## One end for both

For all their differences, the two groupings met the same fate on the same day.

On 1 April 1946 the Malayan Union folded the Federated states, the Unfederated states and two of the three former Straits Settlements — Penang and Melaka — into a single centralised administration, stripping away the individual standing of the Malay Rulers. That flattening drew sustained objection and did not last: within a few years it was replaced by the Federation of Malaya, a framework that deliberately restored the position of the Rulers the 1946 plan had set aside.

The pre-war split into Federated and Unfederated states is exactly the layering the Malayan Union tried to erase in one stroke — which is why it reads more clearly once the two groupings, and the two kinds of officer, are set side by side.

## Common mistakes

**"All nine Malay states were Federated."** Only four were: Perak, Selangor, Negeri Sembilan and Pahang. The other five — Johor, Kedah, Kelantan, Perlis and Terengganu — were Unfederated.

**"A Resident and an Adviser were the same thing under different names."** Both advised and neither touched Islam or Malay custom, but the Federated states shared a Resident-General, a federal bureaucracy and a Federal Council, while the Unfederated states each dealt with Britain alone and kept more of their own administration.

**"The British officer replaced the Sultan."** In both groupings the Sultan remained the sovereign ruler of his state throughout; the officer's role was advisory, with religion and Malay custom reserved to the ruler.

**"Johor was governed exactly like Perak."** Johor entered latest, on the strength of an 1885 treaty affirming its sovereignty, and its officer was titled General Adviser specifically to distinguish it from the Resident posts elsewhere.

**"The Unfederated states were never under any outside power before Britain."** Four of them — Kedah, Kelantan, Perlis and Terengganu — passed to British protection from Siamese suzerainty under the Anglo-Siamese Treaty ratified in 1909.
