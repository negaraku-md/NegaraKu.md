Stand on the waterfront at Bandar Seri Begawan today and Brunei looks small: a wealthy, oil-rich sultanate wrapped around two slivers of the Borneo coast, close enough to drive across in an afternoon. It is easy to forget that this is the surviving fragment of an empire — that five centuries ago a Sultan of Brunei could claim tribute from harbours strung along most of the island's northern and western shores, and that two of the Malaysian states you can reach from here by road, Sarawak and Sabah, were once his to give away. The story of how a Borneo sea-kingdom rose, reached its peak, and then handed over those coasts piece by piece is one of the essential threads of Malaysian history — even though Brunei itself never became part of Malaysia.

## From Po-ni to a Muslim sultanate

Long before there was a sultanate, there was a trading settlement. Chinese records from the Song and Ming dynasties describe a polity they called **Po-ni** (also written Boni or P'oni) at the mouth of the Brunei River, exchanging tribute and goods with the Chinese court and, for a time, sitting within the orbit of the Hindu-Javanese Majapahit empire. Encyclopaedia Britannica notes that Po-ni's rulers cultivated ties with China, and a ruler of the polity is recorded visiting the Ming court in the early fifteenth century.

The conversion of that polity into an Islamic sultanate is dated, in the traditional accounts, to the fourteenth or fifteenth century. Brunei's own court chronicles — the *Syair Awang Semaun* and the *Salasilah Raja-Raja Brunei* — credit a ruler named Awang Alak Betatar, who is said to have embraced Islam and taken the regnal title **Sultan Muhammad Shah** as the first sultan. The precise date is uncertain and the early genealogy legendary rather than firmly documented; what sources agree on is that by the 1400s Brunei had become a Muslim monarchy plugged into the same archipelago-wide network of Islamised trading ports as the [Melaka Sultanate](/malaysia/melaka-sultanate), part of the broader [Malay sultanate system](/malaysia/malay-sultanate-system) that still underpins Malaysian statehood.

## The golden age of Sultan Bolkiah

Brunei's height came under its fifth sultan, **Bolkiah**, whose reign is commonly dated to around 1485–1524. This was the age of Brunei as a *thalassocracy* — a kingdom whose power ran along coastlines and sea lanes rather than across a solid block of inland territory. According to the historical accounts, Bolkiah's Brunei controlled the coastal areas of north-western Borneo, taking in the lands that are today Brunei, [Sarawak](/states/sarawak) and [Sabah](/states/sabah), and its reach extended north-east to the Sulu Archipelago and into the Philippine islands, with influence around Manila Bay reinforced by a marriage tie to the ruling house of Maynila. Bolkiah is remembered in tradition as a seafaring sultan whose fleets carried Brunei's name far beyond Borneo.

It is this golden age that makes Brunei central to the history of East Malaysia. When later documents, treaties and disputes refer to a "Sultan of Brunei" with the standing to grant away rivers and coasts in Sarawak and Sabah, the authority they invoke is the residue of Bolkiah's thalassocracy — a sovereignty claim rooted in the sixteenth century and still being cited, and dismantled, in the nineteenth.

## Decline: civil war, Spain, and shrinking rivers

No sea empire holds together forever. From the late sixteenth century Brunei's grip began to loosen under a combination of outside pressure and internal strife. Spanish forces briefly captured the Brunei capital in 1578 during a conflict out of the Philippines, holding it only a matter of weeks before withdrawing to Manila. More corrosive was the **Brunei Civil War of about 1660–1673**, a succession struggle that drained the court, and the long competition with the Sulu Sultanate to the north-east that chipped away at Brunei's eastern claims.

By the early nineteenth century the sultanate had contracted into a looser collection of riverine territories governed by chiefs who owed only nominal loyalty to the capital. Piracy, disputed successions and the rising presence of European powers in the region left Brunei vulnerable — a kingdom still claiming a long coastline but no longer able to administer or defend much of it. Into that weakness sailed an English adventurer.

## 1841: Sarawak and the White Rajahs

In 1839 **James Brooke**, a former officer of the East India Company with his own armed schooner, arrived off the Sarawak coast and met Pengiran Muda Hashim, an uncle and senior minister of the Sultan of Brunei, who was struggling to put down a local revolt. Brooke helped suppress it. His reward, granted on 24 September 1841, was the governorship of Sarawak and the title of **Rajah** — a grant later confirmed by Sultan Omar Ali Saifuddin II around July 1842, per the historical record of the Raj of Sarawak.

That single grant founded a dynasty. Brooke and his successors — his nephew Charles Brooke and then Charles Vyner Brooke — ruled Sarawak as the **"White Rajahs"** from 1841 until the Second World War, and across a century they steadily enlarged their realm, acquiring river after river from a Brunei court in no position to refuse. After an 1846 conflict the Sultan also ceded the island of **Labuan** to Britain. The effect on Brunei was a slow amputation: each cession to Sarawak shrank the sultanate toward the two small enclaves it occupies today.

## North Borneo and the British protectorate (1878–1888)

While the Brookes expanded from the south-west, a commercial venture closed in from the north-east. In 1877–1878 the Sultan of Brunei — and, for overlapping territory, the Sultan of Sulu — signed concessions handing control of **North Borneo**, the land that is now Sabah, to European interests. Those rights were consolidated under the **British North Borneo Company**, which received its charter on 1 November 1881 and governed the territory as a chartered company, much as the Brookes governed Sarawak as a private dynasty.

By the 1880s the Sultan of Brunei, **Hashim Jalilul Alam Aqamaddin**, watched his realm being carved up on both flanks and appealed to Britain to halt the erosion. The outcome was the **Protectorate Agreement of 1888**: Brunei's agreement was signed on 17 September 1888, placing the sultanate's foreign affairs in British hands while leaving its internal government to the Sultan. Sarawak and North Borneo became British protectorates in the same year. From 1906 a British Resident was installed in Brunei whose advice was binding on the Sultan in all matters except the Islamic religion and Malay custom. The three Borneo territories — Brunei, Sarawak and North Borneo — were now distinct British-protected entities on a single island, a division that would shape the map of modern Malaysia.

## Brunei's separate path — and why it did not join Malaysia

When Britain began withdrawing from the region after the Second World War, the three Borneo territories took different roads. Sarawak and North Borneo became Crown Colonies and were then invited into the proposed Federation of Malaysia alongside Malaya and Singapore. The **Cobbold Commission** of 1962 assessed opinion in Sarawak and North Borneo, the Malaysia Agreement was signed in London on 9 July 1963, and on 16 September 1963 **Malaysia was proclaimed** — North Borneo entering as the state of Sabah.

Brunei was invited too, and for a time its inclusion was expected. In the end the sultanate **did not join**. Negotiations faltered over the terms — among them the status of the Sultan and the sharing of Brunei's oil revenues — and the territory remained a British protectorate outside the new federation. Brunei continued on its own, achieving full independence as **Brunei Darussalam on 1 January 1984**. The sultanate that had once claimed Sarawak and Sabah thus ended the twentieth century as their independent neighbour, not their fellow state.

## Why it still matters for Malaysia

The borders of East Malaysia are, in a real sense, the fossil record of Brunei's decline. Sarawak exists as a distinct Malaysian state because of a grant to James Brooke in 1841; Sabah exists in its modern shape because of concessions to a chartered company confirmed in the 1880s — and both were drawn from territory a Sultan of Brunei had the standing to cede only because of the sixteenth-century empire of Bolkiah. The small modern footprint of Brunei, nearly surrounded by Sarawak, is the visible scar of that long contraction. Understanding why Malaysia's two largest states sit across the South China Sea from the peninsula, and why a separate sovereign country sits wedged between them, means understanding the Brunei Sultanate — the Borneo empire whose losses became part of Malaysia's gains.
