In the space of a few turbulent years, Tun Abdul Razak Hussein moved from constitutional caretaker of a nation under emergency rule to the chief architect of its modern economic order. Malaysia's second Prime Minister governed for barely five years, yet the framework he built — an affirmative-action economy, a dominant coalition and a pragmatic foreign policy — shaped the country for decades after his sudden death in office. To many Malaysians he remains simply **Bapa Pembangunan**, the Father of Development.

## Early life and education

Abdul Razak bin Hussein was born on 11 March 1922 in Pulau Keladi, Pekan, in the state of Pahang, into an aristocratic family connected to the Pahang court (Perdana Leadership Foundation). Selected for the elite Malay College Kuala Kangsar in 1934, he went on to Raffles College in Singapore in 1940, where his studies were cut short by the Japanese occupation. After the war he read law in Britain and was called to the Bar at Lincoln's Inn, qualifying as a barrister in 1950 (Perdana Leadership Foundation).

Returning home, Razak entered the Malayan civil service and then politics through the United Malays National Organisation (UMNO), rising quickly to become one of its leading figures alongside [Tunku Abdul Rahman](/malaysia/tunku-abdul-rahman), the country's founding Prime Minister.

## The deputy years

When Malaya achieved independence in 1957, Abdul Razak became its first **Deputy Prime Minister**, a post he held from 31 August 1957 until he succeeded to the top office in 1970 (Encyclopædia Britannica). He combined the deputy premiership with the portfolio for national and rural development, and it was here that his reputation was made.

Razak drove a succession of rural and agricultural programmes, using tools such as the district-planning "Red Book" and expanding land-settlement schemes including the Federal Land Development Authority (FELDA), which resettled landless families onto cleared estates. His hands-on, data-driven approach to village development became a hallmark of his career and the root of the "Father of Development" title.

## The National Operations Council and the 1969 emergency

The defining crisis of Razak's career came on **13 May 1969**, when communal violence broke out in Kuala Lumpur in the aftermath of a tense general election. A state of emergency was declared, Parliament was suspended, and executive power passed to a **National Operations Council (MAGERAN)**. Abdul Razak was appointed its Director of Operations, and in that role he effectively governed the country while the normal constitutional order was in abeyance (Encyclopædia Britannica; Malaysian Bar).

The council ran Malaysia until parliamentary government was restored in 1971. Britannica records that he "steered the country through that year's violent disturbances." The episode is among the most sensitive in Malaysian history, and this article summarises it in neutral terms; the causes, conduct and consequences of 13 May remain the subject of ongoing scholarship and debate.

## The New Economic Policy

Out of the emergency came the policy most closely associated with Razak's name. Launched in 1971 as part of the Second Malaysia Plan, the **New Economic Policy (NEP)** set two overarching goals: to eradicate poverty irrespective of race, and to "restructure" society so that economic function was no longer identified with ethnicity (Perdana Leadership Foundation; Malaysian Bar). In practice this meant a broad programme of affirmative action intended to raise the economic share of Malays and other Bumiputera groups.

The NEP reshaped the Malaysian state. Razak's government created or expanded institutions to carry it out — among them the national oil company Petronas, the standards body SIRIM, the development bank Bank Pembangunan, and new universities such as Universiti Kebangsaan Malaysia. Its twenty-year horizon meant the policy long outlived its author, and its legacy continues to be debated.

## Barisan Nasional and the opening to China

Politically, Razak's answer to the fragility exposed in 1969 was consensus-building on a grand scale. He drew former opposition parties into government, replacing the old three-party Alliance with a far broader coalition. This grouping took shape in 1973 and was formally registered as **Barisan Nasional** (the National Front) in 1974 (Malaysian Bar). It would go on to govern Malaysia continuously until 2018.

Abroad, Razak pursued a strikingly pragmatic course. In late May 1974 he travelled to Beijing, where he met Chairman Mao Zedong, and on 31 May 1974 the two governments signed a communiqué establishing diplomatic relations — making **Malaysia the first ASEAN country to recognise the People's Republic of China** (New Straits Times; The Star). The move, made at the height of the Cold War, reflected his broader policy of regional neutrality and non-alignment.

## Death in office and legacy

Abdul Razak had been diagnosed with leukaemia years earlier, and the illness caught up with him at the peak of his power. He died in London on **14 January 1976** while receiving treatment, becoming the first Malaysian Prime Minister to die in office (Perdana Leadership Foundation; Encyclopædia Britannica). He was buried at the Makam Pahlawan (Heroes' Mausoleum) beside the National Mosque in Kuala Lumpur. He was succeeded by his cousin and brother-in-law, [Hussein Onn](/malaysia/hussein-onn).

His family remained central to Malaysian public life: his eldest son, [Najib Razak](/malaysia/najib-razak), became the country's sixth Prime Minister in 2009.

## Why he still matters

Few leaders have left so deep an imprint in so short a tenure. The institutional architecture Razak built — the NEP's affirmative-action settlement, the Barisan Nasional coalition and an independent, China-engaging foreign policy — defined the terms of Malaysian politics long after 1976. For that reason he is studied not only as a biography but as a turning point: the moment Malaysia's post-independence order was recast. Understanding him is essential to understanding the debates over race, economy and governance that continue in Malaysia today, and his place among [the Prime Ministers of Malaysia](/government/prime-ministers-of-malaysia).
