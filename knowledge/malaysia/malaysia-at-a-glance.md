This page exists to fix that: every figure below comes from a named official release, dated, so you can see exactly how current it is and check it yourself.

## Population: 34.2 million, and DOSM's own caveats

DOSM's *Current Population Estimates, Malaysia, 2025* (released 31 July 2025) put the national total at 34.2 million, up from 34.1 million in 2024 — a 0.5 per cent annual growth rate. Of that total, 90.1 per cent (30.9 million) were citizens and 9.9 per cent (3.38 million) were non-citizens.

Two things worth knowing before quoting this number elsewhere:

- It is an **estimate**, not a headcount. DOSM derives it with the cohort-component method, using the 2020 Population and Housing Census as a base and adjusting for births, deaths and migration each year.
- The **sex ratio** differs sharply by citizenship: 102 males per 100 females among citizens, versus 228 males per 100 females among non-citizens — a gap DOSM attributes to the high number of male foreign workers.

For the full ethnic and citizenship breakdown DOSM actually publishes — Malay, Other Bumiputera, Chinese, Indian and Others, plus the Sabah/Sarawak Bumiputera sub-groups — see [Malaysia's population composition](/en/malaysia/population-composition), which tracks the release-by-release detail this page deliberately keeps out.

## Land area: 330,345 km², a figure that moves rarely and slowly

DOSM's *Compendium of Environment Statistics, 2016* records Malaysia's total land area as 330,345 km². Land area is one of the few national statistics that barely changes year to year (reclamation and gazetting aside), so this remains the figure DOSM's own environment statistics still reference. Malaysia sits in two separate landmasses: Peninsular Malaysia on the Asian mainland, and Sabah and Sarawak on the island of Borneo — a geographic split that shapes almost everything else on this page, from time zone uniformity to the state list below.

## Capital and seat of government — two different things

Kuala Lumpur is Malaysia's constitutional and royal capital: it is where the Federal Territory of Kuala Lumpur sits, and where key national institutions, including the Istana Negara (National Palace), are located.

Putrajaya, a separate Federal Territory since 1 February 2001, is the administrative centre of the federal government — most ministries and the Prime Minister's Department relocated there starting in 1999, according to Perbadanan Putrajaya (Putrajaya Corporation), the statutory body that administers it. A handful of ministries, including those for Works, International Trade and Industry, and Defence, remain in Kuala Lumpur.

## States and federal territories

Malaysia's 13 states and 3 federal territories are set out in Article 1 of the Federal Constitution:

- **11 states in Peninsular Malaysia**: Johor, Kedah, Kelantan, Melaka, Negeri Sembilan, Pahang, Penang, Perak, Perlis, Selangor, Terengganu
- **2 states in East Malaysia (Borneo)**: Sabah, Sarawak
- **3 federal territories**: Kuala Lumpur, Labuan, Putrajaya

Nine of the peninsular states retain hereditary Malay rulers (sultans, or a Raja in Perlis, or the Yang di-Pertuan Besar in Negeri Sembilan); Melaka, Penang, Sabah and Sarawak instead have an appointed Yang di-Pertuan Negeri as head of state. Every five years, the Conference of Rulers elects the Yang di-Pertuan Agong — the federal Head of State — from among the nine hereditary rulers.

## Currency: the ringgit, not the "dollar"

The Malaysian ringgit (ISO 4217 code **MYR**, symbol **RM**) is issued by Bank Negara Malaysia (BNM), the central bank, and is subdivided into 100 sen. Banknotes in common circulation are RM1, RM5, RM10, RM20, RM50 and RM100; coins in common circulation are 5, 10, 20 and 50 sen. (RM2 notes and 1-sen coins were discontinued from active circulation but remain legal tender, per BNM.)

The currency was introduced in 1967 as the Malaysian dollar and formally renamed "ringgit" in 1975 — a naming detail that outlives most conversations about it, since older English usage sometimes still slips into calling it "the Malaysian dollar."

## Language: one national language, constitutionally limited in scope

Article 152 of the Federal Constitution names Bahasa Melayu as Malaysia's sole national language, required for official purposes — defined narrowly as the business of the Federal Government, a State Government, or a public authority. The same Article protects the right to use, teach or learn any other language outside that official scope, which is why English, Mandarin, Tamil and a long list of indigenous languages remain in everyday, business and educational use across the country.

For the fuller picture — including how the constitutional position sits alongside actual language use by ethnic group, and the scale of linguistic diversity in Sabah and Sarawak — see [Malaysia's national language, and the many others spoken beside it](/en/malaysia/languages-spoken).

## Economy: RM2.03 trillion nominal, 5.2 per cent growth

DOSM's 2025 Gross Domestic Product release (15 May 2026) puts nominal GDP at RM2.03 trillion for the year, a 5.2 per cent expansion, with the Services and Manufacturing sectors together contributing 82.5 per cent of total output. GNI per capita rose to RM57,200 in 2025, from RM54,870 in 2024.

These are DOSM's own headline figures for the full year; quarterly releases (available on the same DOSM portal) break the picture down further by sector and by quarter for readers who need more granularity than a fact sheet provides.

## Common mistakes

**Quoting Kuala Lumpur as "the seat of the federal government."** It's the constitutional and royal capital; Putrajaya has been the administrative seat since 1999. Both are correct, for different functions — conflating them makes both facts wrong.

**Calling the currency "the Malaysian dollar."** It was renamed ringgit in 1975; MYR and RM are the current codes.

**Treating population estimates as a census count.** DOSM's annual population figures are modelled estimates built on the 2020 census as a base, not a fresh headcount — useful to know before treating any single year's number as exact.

**Citing total area figures that mix land and water differently.** International sources vary depending on whether they include inland water bodies and maritime claims; this page uses DOSM's own published land-area figure rather than reconciling competing external totals.
