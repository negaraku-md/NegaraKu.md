When Melaka fell to Portuguese cannon in 1511, the dynasty that had made the Strait famous did not die with the city. Its last sultan, Mahmud Shah, withdrew up the archipelago and left two sons; one would refound the royal line downriver in Johor, the other would carry the old Melaka crown north, to a quiet valley threaded by a river whose sandbanks glittered with tin. That northern kingdom became Perak, and nearly five centuries later it is still ruled by the same bloodline, now by a sultan whose throne is passed not from father to son but along a moving ladder of princes. This is the story of how a defeated dynasty survived, grew rich on tin, lost its sovereignty to Britain over a single treaty, and endured into the present.

## From Melaka's ashes: the founding of 1528

Perak's royal house begins with the collapse of [the Melaka Sultanate](/malaysia/melaka-sultanate). After the Portuguese conquest, Sultan Mahmud Shah retreated through the region and died at Kampar in Sumatra in 1528. According to the genealogy maintained by Perak's own royal records and reflected in standard reference accounts, local Perak chiefs — led by figures such as Tun Saban — sought a prince of the Melaka house to rule over them. Mahmud Shah's son Muzaffar Shah was installed as the first Sultan of Perak, traditionally dated to around 1528, while his brother Alauddin Riayat Shah II founded what became [the Johor-Riau Sultanate](/malaysia/johor-riau-sultanate).

That shared parentage matters. Perak and Johor are sister dynasties, both descended directly from the sovereigns of Melaka, which is why Perak is often described as the only peninsular state whose ruling house traces an unbroken line back to the Melaka court. The *New Straits Times* has called it "Malaysia's second oldest sultanate." For readers tracing [the Malay sultanate system](/malaysia/malay-sultanate-system), Perak is the clearest surviving thread connecting today's monarchy to the pre-colonial Malay world.

## The kingdom that tin built

What made Perak worth ruling was tin. The alluvial deposits of the Kinta and Larut valleys were among the richest in the world, and for centuries tin ore mined, panned, and smelted along the Perak River was the state's economic engine, traded to Indian, Chinese, and later European merchants. The metal gave the sultanate both its wealth and its vulnerability: whoever controlled the mining districts controlled the revenue, and the mining districts lay at the edges of royal authority, in the hands of territorial chiefs.

By the mid-19th century the tinfields had drawn an enormous immigrant workforce. Around 1870 the Larut district alone held roughly 40,000 Hakka and Cantonese miners, organised into rival Chinese secret societies — chiefly the Ghee Hin and the Hai San — whose networks ran from the mines back to the merchant houses of Penang. Tin had turned Perak into a frontier boomland, and boomlands are hard to govern.

## The Larut Wars: tin, triads, and a contested throne

Between 1861 and 1874 the Larut tinfields erupted into a series of four wars. On the surface they were fights between the Ghee Hin and Hai San societies over mining ground and water rights; underneath, they fused with a bitter contest for the Perak throne. The Menteri of Larut, Ngah Ibrahim, backed the Hai San, while a rival claimant, Raja Abdullah, aligned himself with the Ghee Hin. Gambling quarrels, murders, and reprisals escalated into pitched battles: in the third war of 1872, some 10,000 defeated Hai San miners fled to Penang, and that September a group of Chinese leaders petitioned the colonial authorities to intervene.

The fighting wrecked the tin economy and threatened Penang's merchants, whose capital was sunk in the mines. Their appeals for order gave the British — until then formally committed to non-intervention in the Malay states — the opening they had been looking for.

## Pangkor 1874 and the Resident who went too far

On 20 January 1874, aboard the colonial steamer *Pluto* anchored off Pangkor Island, the Governor of the Straits Settlements, Sir Andrew Clarke, convened the parties to what became the Pangkor Engagement (or Pangkor Treaty). The agreement recognised Raja Abdullah as Sultan of Perak in preference to his rival Sultan Ismail, who refused to attend and kept the royal regalia. It settled the Larut disputes by appointing a Chinese Kapitan and renaming the war-torn district Taiping — "everlasting peace."

Its most consequential clause was quieter. A British Resident would be stationed at the Perak court, and the sultan undertook that the Resident's advice "must be asked and acted upon on all questions other than those touching Malay religion and custom." In a single sentence, Perak had traded away most of its sovereignty. The Pangkor model — indirect rule through a Resident who "advised" but effectively governed — would be exported across the peninsula and became the template for British control of the Malay States.

Perak's first Resident, James Wheeler Woodford Birch, took up the post on 4 November 1874. Energetic and, by most accounts, tactless, Birch moved to abolish slavery and to centralise tax collection — directly attacking the revenues and standing of the Malay chiefs. Resentment hardened into conspiracy. At a gathering of chiefs on 21 July 1875 an offer by the local chief Maharaja Lela to kill Birch was accepted, and on 2 November 1875 Birch was speared to death at the bath-house on his boat on the Perak River at Pasir Salak. A scholarly reassessment published in 2026 in the *MINDEN Journal of History and Archaeology* re-examines Ngah Ibrahim's role in the uprising that followed.

The assassination triggered the Perak War of 1875–1876. British forces suppressed the revolt; Maharaja Lela and others were executed, Sultan Abdullah was deposed and exiled to the Seychelles, and Ngah Ibrahim was banished. Raja Yusuf eventually took the throne, and a more conciliatory Resident, Hugh Low, rebuilt the administration. The lesson colonial officials drew was not to retreat but to tread more softly — the Residency survived and spread.

## The modern house and its moving crown

Through the Residency, the Federated Malay States, the Japanese occupation, and independence in 1957, the royal house of Perak endured. What makes it distinctive is how its throne passes. Instead of strict primogeniture, Perak uses a ladder of ranked royal titles. Under a ruling of 25 February 1953 that fixed the modern order, the senior title is Raja Muda, followed by Raja di-Hilir, then Raja Kecil Besar and a descending series of lesser Raja Kecil titles. When the sultan dies, the Raja Muda normally ascends, the Raja di-Hilir moves up to Raja Muda, the Raja Kecil Besar becomes Raja di-Hilir, and so on down the rungs — so the crown rotates among the royal family's branches rather than staying in one line. The two most senior titleholders are confirmed by a majority vote of the Perak State Legislative Assembly, and the Royal Council retains discretion to adjust the order.

The reigning sultan, Nazrin Muizzuddin Shah, is counted as Perak's 35th sultan and has reigned since 29 May 2014, the day after the death of his father, Sultan Azlan Muhibbuddin Shah.

## Why it still matters

Perak's history is woven through the biggest turning points in the Malaysian story. Its founding is the living proof that the Melaka line did not end in 1511 but survives on the throne in Kuala Kangsar today. Its tin built the economy that drew the immigrant communities who reshaped the peninsula's society. And its Pangkor Engagement is, more than any battle, the document that opened the door to British rule across the Malay States — which is why Pasir Salak is now a national historical site and Birch's killing is remembered as an early act of resistance. Perak is one of [the nine royal houses](/malaysia/nine-royal-houses) whose rulers take turns as Malaysia's king, and its unusual rotating succession remains one of the most studied features of the country's constitutional monarchy. To understand modern [Perak](/states/perak) — and modern Malaysia — is to start with this sultanate.
