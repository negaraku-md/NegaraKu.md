Sabah's state government sums up its own population this way: 33 indigenous groups, communicating in more than 50 languages and roughly 80 dialects. That is one state, out of thirteen, plus three federal territories. Zoom out to the whole country and the picture gets only more layered — yet the Federal Constitution names exactly one national language.

Both facts are true at once, and neither cancels the other out. This article separates the law from the demographics: what Bahasa Melayu's constitutional status actually says, and then what the official population data says about who speaks what, from Chinese dialects and Tamil to the indigenous languages of Sabah, Sarawak and Peninsular Malaysia.

## One clause, one national language

Article 152(1) of the Federal Constitution states that the national language is Bahasa Melayu (the Malay language). The National Language Act 1963/67 (Act 32) gives that provision statutory teeth, and Dewan Bahasa dan Pustaka (DBP) — the statutory body established to develop and standardise the language — is the institution tasked with carrying that mandate forward.

That much is table stakes; most overviews mention it in passing. What tends to get skipped is what Article 152 does *not* do.

The same clause carries its own limits, in Article 152(1)(a) and (b):

- No person may be prohibited or prevented from using, teaching or learning any other language, **except for official purposes**.
- Nothing in Article 152 prejudices the right of the Federal Government or a State Government to preserve and sustain the use and study of any other community's language.

Article 152(6) then defines "official purposes" narrowly: any purpose of the Federal Government, a State Government, or a public authority. Outside that federal/state-government scope — at home, in business, in places of worship, in vernacular schools — the Constitution is explicit that other languages are protected, not merely tolerated.

## Where English fits

English is not Malaysia's national language, and it never has been since independence. But the Constitution itself built in a bridge: it allowed the continued use of English for official purposes for a period after Merdeka Day, extendable until Parliament decided otherwise.

Decades on, Bahasa Melayu is firmly the language of official government business, but English's practical footprint remains large — in private-sector business, in much of higher education and professional practice, and in parts of the legal system, where English-language case law and legal drafting still carry significant weight. The two languages occupy different, largely non-competing lanes rather than one having simply replaced the other.

## Who the citizen population actually is

Language distribution in Malaysia tracks closely with ethnic composition, so the official demographic breakdown is the most solid starting point available. The Department of Statistics Malaysia (DOSM) counts citizens and non-citizens separately, and splits the citizen population into five named ethnic categories: Malay, Other Bumiputera, Chinese, Indian and Others. Malay is the largest of the five; Chinese is the second largest, Other Bumiputera third and Indian fourth.

The published percentages behind those categories move every release, so this article does not restate them — see [Malaysia's population composition](/en/malaysia/population-composition), where each figure is quoted against the specific DOSM release it comes from.

What matters for language is the shape of the categories rather than their exact size. "Other Bumiputera" is doing a lot of work in that list — it is not one group with one language, but a category DOSM itself splits further into Sabah Bumiputera, Sarawak Bumiputera and other Peninsular Bumiputera communities, each with their own language landscape. That is where most of Malaysia's linguistic diversity actually sits, and it's the part most short overviews compress into a single line.

## Chinese dialects: one lingua franca, several mother tongues

Mandarin functions as a common language among Malaysian Chinese — it is the medium of instruction in national-type Chinese schools (SJKC) — but it is rarely the only language spoken at home. Depending on ancestral origin and region, Malaysian Chinese communities also speak Hokkien, Cantonese, Hakka, Teochew, Hainanese and other dialect groups, often alongside Mandarin and Bahasa Melayu rather than instead of them.

The SJKC stream sits within the national education system under the Ministry of Education, following the same national curriculum as national schools, with Mandarin as the medium of instruction rather than a subject.

## Tamil, and other South Asian languages

Tamil is by far the predominant language of the Malaysian Indian community, carried through generations of migration from South India during the British colonial period. It functions in homes, in national-type Tamil schools (SJKT), in religious contexts and in Tamil-language media.

Malaysian Indians are not linguistically uniform either — smaller communities speak other South Asian languages such as Malayalam, Telugu or Punjabi — but Tamil is the language with an institutional footprint of its own, most visibly through the SJKT school stream, which sits alongside SJKC and SK (national schools using Bahasa Melayu) as one of three government-funded primary school types under the Ministry of Education.

## Sabah and Sarawak: where "indigenous" means dozens of languages

East Malaysia is where the country's linguistic range is widest, and where the "Other Bumiputera" line in the national table breaks open into detail that official sources do track.

**Sabah's** own government describes the state's population as 33 indigenous groups using more than 50 languages and around 80 dialects. The largest indigenous groups are Kadazan-Dusun, Bajau and Murut — the same names DOSM carries in its Sabah Bumiputera breakdown — with the remainder spread across smaller groups such as the Bisaya, Rungus, Lundayeh and others.

**Sarawak's** Bumiputera population divides differently again. DOSM's Sarawak Bumiputera breakdown names Iban — the largest indigenous group in the state — followed by Bidayuh and Melanau, alongside Orang Ulu communities and other smaller groups, each associated with its own distinct language.

Neither state's linguistic map reduces cleanly to a short list; the scale reported by Sabah's own government — dozens of languages, not a handful — is itself the more accurate takeaway than any single named language "representing" East Malaysia.

## Peninsular Malaysia's Orang Asli

Peninsular Malaysia has its own indigenous population, the Orang Asli, administered by the Department of Orang Asli Development (JAKOA). JAKOA classifies the Orang Asli into three main groups — Negrito, Senoi and Proto-Malay — comprising 18 subgroups in total, each historically associated with its own language, distributed mainly across Pahang, Perak and Selangor.

The Orang Asli are numerically a small share of the national population, but they are linguistically distinct from both the Malay-speaking majority and from the indigenous groups of Sabah and Sarawak — three separate indigenous language traditions inside one country, not one.

## Common mistakes

A few habits distort this topic more than the facts themselves do:

- **Treating "Bumiputera" as one language group.** It is a legal and administrative category covering Malay speakers, Sabah Bumiputera (Kadazan-Dusun, Bajau, Murut and others), Sarawak Bumiputera (Iban, Bidayuh, Melanau and others) and the Orang Asli — communities with genuinely distinct languages, not dialects of one another.
- **Assuming "national language" means "only language permitted."** Article 152 itself draws the opposite conclusion: the requirement applies to official purposes only, and the same clause protects the use, teaching and learning of other languages outside that scope.
- **Assuming Malaysian Chinese all speak the same dialect.** Mandarin is a shared second language for many; the home dialect a family actually grew up with — Hokkien, Cantonese, Hakka or another — depends heavily on ancestral origin and region.
- **Confusing East Malaysia's indigenous languages with Peninsular Malaysia's Orang Asli languages.** These are separate indigenous traditions, administered separately (state governments and agencies in Sabah/Sarawak; JAKOA for Peninsular Malaysia), not one indigenous-language category.
