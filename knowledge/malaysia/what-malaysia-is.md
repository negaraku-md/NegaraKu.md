Malaysia is not one place with one government. It is a federation — a single country stitched together from thirteen states and three federal territories, sitting on two land masses that are separated by several hundred kilometres of the South China Sea. Understanding that structure first makes almost everything else about the country easier to follow.

## The short answer

Constitutionally, Malaysia is a **federal, elective constitutional monarchy**. Three ideas are doing the work in that phrase:

- **Federal** — power is shared between one central (federal) government and thirteen state governments, each with its own assembly.
- **Elective** — the head of state, the king, is not one dynasty's inheritance but is *elected* for a fixed term.
- **Constitutional monarchy** — the king reigns under a written constitution and acts, in most matters, on the advice of an elected government.

The document that establishes all of this is the **Federal Constitution**, which Article 4 declares to be "the supreme law of the Federation" — meaning any ordinary law that conflicts with it is void to the extent of the conflict.

## Thirteen states and three federal territories

Article 1 of the Federal Constitution names the thirteen states directly: Johor, Kedah, Kelantan, Malacca (Melaka), Negeri Sembilan, Pahang, Penang (Pulau Pinang), Perak, Perlis, Sabah, Sarawak, Selangor and Terengganu.

They fall into two groups divided by the sea:

| Region | What it contains |
| --- | --- |
| Peninsular Malaysia (West) | 11 states on the Malay Peninsula, plus the federal territories of Kuala Lumpur and Putrajaya |
| East Malaysia (Borneo) | The two large states of [Sabah](/en/states/sabah) and [Sarawak](/en/states/sarawak), plus the federal territory of Labuan |

On top of the states sit three **federal territories** — pieces of land governed directly by the federal government rather than by a state:

- **[Kuala Lumpur](/en/states/kuala-lumpur)** — the national capital, carved out of Selangor in 1974.
- **[Labuan](/en/states/labuan)** — an island off Borneo, separated from Sabah in 1984.
- **[Putrajaya](/en/states/putrajaya)** — the federal administrative centre, created from Selangor in 2001, where most ministries and the Prime Minister's office are based.

So the capital and the seat of administration are, deliberately, not the same place — a distinction that trips up a lot of newcomers.

## A king who is elected

The most unusual feature of Malaysia's structure is its monarchy. The head of state is the **Yang di-Pertuan Agong** (often translated "the King" or "Supreme Head"), established by Article 32 of the Constitution.

He is not born into the national throne. Under Article 32, the Yang di-Pertuan Agong is **elected for a term of five years** by the **Conference of Rulers** (Majlis Raja-Raja, Article 38). Nine of the thirteen states are hereditary Malay monarchies, each with its own Ruler (a Sultan, or in some states a differently titled Ruler); those nine Rulers elect one of their number to serve as the national king, in practice rotating the office among the states. The remaining four states — Penang, Malacca, Sabah and Sarawak — have no hereditary ruler and are instead headed by an appointed **Yang di-Pertua Negeri** (Governor).

This makes Malaysia one of very few elective monarchies anywhere in the world. The [Yang di-Pertuan Agong](/en/government/yang-di-pertuan-agong) is head of state, symbol of the federation, and — in a limited set of situations — a holder of genuine constitutional discretion, while day-to-day executive power runs through an elected government.

> Nine hereditary Rulers choose one king, for five years at a time. It is a monarchy and an election at once — a design that exists almost nowhere else.

## Who actually governs: the three branches

Federal power is organised the way most constitutional democracies organise it — into a legislature, an executive, and a judiciary.

**The legislature — Parliament.** Article 44 vests federal law-making authority in a Parliament made up of the Yang di-Pertuan Agong and *two* Houses:

- the **Dewan Rakyat** (House of Representatives), whose members are elected from constituencies at a general election; and
- the **Dewan Negara** (Senate), whose members are, under Article 45, partly elected by the state legislatures (two per state) and partly appointed by the Yang di-Pertuan Agong.

A bill generally has to pass both Houses and receive royal assent before it becomes an Act. There is more on how this works in the article on [Parliament](/en/government/parliament).

**The executive.** Article 39 vests the executive authority of the federation in the Yang di-Pertuan Agong, but it is exercised in practice by the **Cabinet**, led by the **Prime Minister**, who commands the confidence of the Dewan Rakyat. This is the government of the day.

**The judiciary.** A separate system of courts interprets and applies the law, headed by the Federal Court. Because the Constitution is supreme, the courts can hold that a law or an act of government is unconstitutional.

## How power is split between the federation and the states

This is the part that makes Malaysia a *federation* rather than a single centralised state. The Constitution does not let the federal government legislate on everything. The **Ninth Schedule** divides subjects into three lists:

| List | Who legislates | Typical subjects |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Federal List | Federal Parliament | Defence, foreign affairs, citizenship, finance, trade, education, national policy |
| State List | State Legislative Assemblies | Land, agriculture and forestry, local government, and Islamic (Syariah) law and Malay custom |
| Concurrent List | Both | Certain shared subjects, such as some aspects of public health and drainage |

Each state has its **own written constitution** and its own **State Legislative Assembly** (Dewan Undangan Negeri) that legislates on the matters reserved to it. Article 71 requires the federation to guarantee each Ruler's constitutional position and the operation of the state constitutions.

Two states matter especially here. When Sabah and Sarawak joined in 1963, they entered on specific terms that gave them additional powers and safeguards not held by the peninsular states — over matters such as immigration and native law. That distinct constitutional position is the reason "Sabah and Sarawak" appear as a special case again and again in Malaysian public life.

## Why the structure looks the way it does

None of this was a single clean design. Malaysia as it exists today was **formed in 1963**, when the Federation of Malaya (independent since 1957) joined with Singapore, North Borneo (Sabah) and Sarawak. Singapore then left in 1965. So the present map — eleven peninsular states plus Sabah and Sarawak, without Singapore — is the *result* of that history, not a blueprint drawn up in advance. The story is told in [How Malaysia was formed in 1963](/en/malaysia/formation-of-malaysia-1963).

## Common points of confusion

- **"Malaysia" vs "Malaya."** *Malaya* was the peninsular federation that became independent in 1957. *Malaysia* is the larger federation formed in 1963 that added the Borneo states. They are not interchangeable.
- **The capital isn't where the government sits.** Kuala Lumpur is the capital and seat of Parliament; Putrajaya is the administrative centre where the executive is based.
- **The king changes.** Because the Yang di-Pertuan Agong serves five-year terms and the office rotates among the Rulers, the person who is king changes over time — the institution is permanent, the holder is not.
- **States are not just administrative regions.** They have their own constitutions, assemblies and law-making powers, and (for nine of them) their own Rulers. A Malaysian state is closer to a US state than to a mere province.
