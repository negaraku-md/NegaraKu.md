A company spends seven months and an alliance with a foreign sultanate to conquer a port. Then it spends the next century and a half making sure that port never gets rich.

That is the plain shape of Dutch rule in Melaka. It is also the part most retellings skip, because "the VOC took over, then the British took over" is a much tidier sentence than the truth: the Dutch East India Company won Melaka, and then treated it as a problem to be managed rather than a prize to be developed.

## Taking it: a joint siege, not a solo conquest

The Dutch did not take Melaka alone.

Arkib Negara Malaysia's own account is blunt about it: Melaka and its fortress "was defeated by the Dutch united with Johor, after seven months of siege conducted against it from sea and land." The Johor Sultanate, which had its own long score to settle with the Portuguese occupiers of Melaka, supplied the land forces and local knowledge that made a naval blockade into an actual capture.

When the fortress fell in 1641, the Dutch found what the Portuguese had been defending it with: 68 cannons and 74 rifles, according to the same archival record. Portuguese Melaka, which had held the Malacca Strait for 130 years, was over.

Dutch rule that followed lasted 154 years — 1641 to 1795, per the Melaka state government's own history. On paper, that is a longer tenure than either the Portuguese before them or the British after. In practice, it was the quietest of the three.

## The real capital was never Melaka

Here is the detail that explains almost everything else in this article: by the time the VOC took Melaka, it already had a capital.

Batavia — modern Jakarta — had been built up by the company well before 1641 as its administrative and warehousing hub for the entire Asian trade. Melaka, conquered more than twenty years later, was never going to unseat it. The VOC's own priority was denying Melaka and its strait to rival European powers, not competing with its own headquarters for trade.

So the company didn't. Tin quotas that Melaka's traders were supposed to meet were increasingly rerouted to Batavia. Governors in Melaka pushed for more latitude to trade; Batavia consistently said no. The pattern shows up in the record as decline, but it was closer to policy.

The result was a port kept deliberately in Batavia's shadow. Rival Malay ports drew away trade the VOC had no interest in suppressing, because propping up a port it had already decided not to prioritise was not worth the cost. Melaka stayed a garrison town and an administrative seat rather than the thriving entrepôt it had been under earlier rulers.

## Even Melaka's hinterland ran through Batavia

The clearest evidence of where real authority sat isn't in Melaka town at all. It's on Pangkor Island, off the coast of Perak.

In 1670 the VOC built Kota Belanda there — a warehouse to store tin bought up under a monopoly the company had forced on Perak's rulers. According to Arkib Negara, the order to build it was "issued from Batavia, the Administrative Centre of the Dutch East India Company" — not from the Melaka governor next door, whose own harbour was the obvious place to route Perak's tin through.

| --- | --- |
| 1651 | An earlier VOC tin post is destroyed by Perak leaders opposing the monopoly |
| 1670 | Kota Belanda built (initially timber), on an order from Batavia |

A tin monopoly extracted from a Malay state next door to Melaka, administered from an island fort, on orders from a headquarters far away across the sea in Java — and Melaka itself barely appears in the chain. That is what "subordinate to Batavia" looked like in practice.

## What Dutch Melaka did build

None of this means the Dutch left Melaka untouched. They built what a colonial administrative seat needs, just not what a thriving trading port needs.

The Stadthuys — the Dutch governor's residence and administrative centre, completed around 1650 — still stands in Melaka's Dutch Square today, along with Christ Church, completed in 1753 to mark a century of Dutch rule, and St. Peter's Church, from 1710. These were buildings for governing and worshipping, not warehouses for a booming entrepôt.

The Dutch also inherited and repaired the Portuguese fortress of A Famosa after the siege damage. It would not survive the century that followed — but that story belongs to the next occupier.

## Losing it, twice

Dutch rule in Melaka did not end once. It ended, was reversed, and ended again — twice within thirty years, for reasons that had nothing to do with Melaka itself.

**1795 — Britain moves in without a fight.** The French Revolutionary Wars had reached the Netherlands, and the exiled Dutch stadtholder, writing from Kew Palace in England, instructed Dutch colonial governors to admit British forces as allies rather than let French-aligned authorities in the Netherlands claim the colonies. Melaka's governor complied. Britain occupied the port to keep it out of French hands, not because it wanted Melaka for itself.

**1806 — the fortress comes down.** Fearing the Dutch might one day retake Melaka and use A Famosa against them, the British ordered the fortress demolished. Arkib Negara records that William Farquhar, acting on orders from Penang, "detonated the fortress until most of it was completely destroyed." Only the gate and wall fragments survive today — the same fragments visitors to Melaka see now.

**1818 — the Dutch come back, briefly.** An Anglo-Dutch treaty settlement following the Napoleonic Wars restored Dutch colonies seized during the conflict, and Melaka reverted to Dutch administration. But the restoration was administrative, not a vote of confidence — nobody was rebuilding Melaka into a rival to Singapore or Batavia at this point.

**1819 — Britain stops waiting.** Rather than bet on what would happen to a restored-to-Dutch Melaka, the British East India Company's Stamford Raffles went looking for an alternative. He left Calcutta in December 1818 specifically in search of a new British settlement to replace Melaka, landed at Singapore in January 1819, and signed a treaty with the Sultanate of Johor on 6 February 1819 that gave Britain its foothold there. Singapore's founding is, in part, a direct consequence of Britain no longer trusting Melaka's future to Dutch hands.

## Handing it over for good

The final transfer was negotiated far from Melaka, in London.

The Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824 — signed 17 March 1824 — settled a long list of overlapping British and Dutch claims across the Malay Archipelago. Under its terms, the Netherlands ceded the city and fort of Melaka, and all its dependencies, to Britain outright, and agreed not to seek any further foothold on the Malay Peninsula. In exchange, Britain handed its own settlement at Bencoolen, on the west coast of Sumatra, to the Dutch — plus its remaining possessions on that island.

The paperwork was signed in 1824. The actual handover took effect on 1 March 1825, the date fixed by the treaty for the transfer of property and establishments on both sides.

Melaka never went back to Dutch rule again. Within two years it was folded, alongside Penang and the new settlement of Singapore, into a single British administrative unit — the beginning of what would become the Straits Settlements.

## Common mistakes

**"The Dutch developed Melaka into a great trading city, like the Portuguese and British did."** The opposite happened. The VOC's own capital, Batavia, competed with Melaka for the same trade, and company policy consistently favoured Batavia. Melaka was kept subordinate to that headquarters and was not built up as a rival entrepôt.

**"Melaka fell to Britain in one clean handover."** It changed hands administratively four times in 30 years: British occupation in 1795, restoration to the Dutch in 1818, permanent cession to Britain by treaty in 1824, and the actual transfer in 1825.

**"A Famosa is a Portuguese ruin."** What survives is a fragment. The fortress the Dutch inherited and repaired was demolished by British order in 1806 to keep it out of Dutch hands during a war the Dutch were losing to France, not the Portuguese.

**"Dutch Melaka and Batavia were roughly equal partners."** They were never equals. Melaka was a VOC possession that answered to Batavia, down to decisions about a tin warehouse on an island off Perak's coast, ordered from Java rather than from Melaka's own governor.
