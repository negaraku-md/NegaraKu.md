On the west coast of the Malay Peninsula, where the Klang and Selangor rivers drain into the Strait of Malacca, a seafaring people from the far side of the archipelago built one of the peninsula's wealthiest states. Their fortune was not built on spice or gold but on tin — the grey ore buried in the valleys that would, within a century, drag the young sultanate into a war over its own riches and, eventually, under the shadow of a British Resident. This is the story of the Selangor Sultanate, a Bugis dynasty whose line still sits on the throne today.

This article sets out the historical record descriptively and respectfully. It concerns a living royal institution, and it aims to state the verifiable facts rather than take any side.

## Who were the Bugis, and how did they reach Selangor?

The Bugis were a maritime and warrior people from southern Sulawesi, in what is now eastern Indonesia. The royal house of Selangor traces its descent to Bugis nobles who claimed ancestry from the rulers of Luwu in Sulawesi. From the late seventeenth century onward, Bugis migrants and fighters spread across the western archipelago, settling along the coasts of the Malay Peninsula and becoming deeply involved in the politics of the Johor-Riau empire.

By the early eighteenth century the Bugis had become kingmakers in Johor, where their leaders held the powerful office of Yang di-Pertuan Muda (effectively the under-king or chief executive). Among the most prominent was Daeng Chelak, one of the famous "five Bugis brothers," who served as Yang di-Pertuan Muda of Riau. It was his son who would carry the family's fortunes north to the tin rivers of Selangor.

## How was the sultanate founded under Raja Lumu?

Daeng Chelak's son, Raja Lumu, established himself as the leading Bugis authority in Selangor and was recognised as Raja Selangor in 1743. For a generation the territory was governed as a Bugis chieftainship rather than a formal sultanate in its own right.

That changed in 1766. According to accounts summarised by historians, Raja Lumu sought the dignity and legitimacy of a formal sultan's title. He sailed to Perak, where — at a place recorded as Kota Lumut — he was installed and recognised by Sultan Mahmud Shah of [the Perak Sultanate](/malaysia/perak-sultanate), one of the peninsula's most senior Malay royal houses. Only then did Raja Lumu take the regnal name **Sultan Salehuddin Shah**, becoming the first Sultan of Selangor. He established his seat at Kuala Selangor. The borrowing of legitimacy from an older house is a recurring feature of [the Malay sultanate system](/malaysia/malay-sultanate-system), in which a ruler's standing rested on recognition by established peers.

Salehuddin was succeeded by his son, who reigned as Sultan Ibrahim Shah, and the dynasty continued down the generations — each ruler drawn from the same Bugis-descended line.

## Why did tin make Selangor both rich and unstable?

Selangor's rivers gave it access to some of the richest tin deposits in the Malay world. Mining districts clustered around **Klang**, the coastal settlement of **Lukut** to the south, and — from the 1850s — the valleys of Ampang that gave rise to **Kuala Lumpur**. Chinese labourers poured in to work the mines, and the ore flowed down the rivers to the coast for export.

Tin brought wealth, but it also raised the stakes of every political quarrel. Control of a mining district meant control of its revenue, and the right to tax the ore and the opium consumed by the miners. By the mid-nineteenth century the Sultan's authority over his own chiefs had weakened, and the territorial rulers who held the tin lands behaved as near-independent lords. The stage was set for a struggle over who would reap the profits.

## What was the Selangor Civil War?

The conflict known as the **Selangor Civil War** or the **Klang War** broke out around 1867 and ran until 1874. Encyclopaedia Britannica describes it as a series of conflicts among Malay chiefs that drew in Chinese mining factions. At its core was a dispute over Klang and the tin revenues of the Klang Valley.

In 1854 the Sultan had appointed **Raja Abdullah** to govern the Klang district. **Raja Mahdi**, who felt he had been deprived of that territory and its income, seized the fort of Klang in 1867, igniting open war. A Kedah prince, **Tengku Kudin** (also recorded as Tengku Dhiauddin or Ziauddin), who had married into the Selangor royal family, was brought in to mediate; when mediation failed, he took up arms against Mahdi.

The war became entangled with the rivalries of Chinese secret societies, who supplied fighters and money to opposing sides:

| Faction | Malay ally | Approximate stance |
|---|---|---|
| Ghee Hin society | Raja Mahdi | Held early control of mining revenue |
| Hai San society | Tengku Kudin / Yap Ah Loy | Backed the eventual victors |

**Yap Ah Loy**, the Kapitan Cina of Kuala Lumpur, aligned with Tengku Kudin, whose side also drew fighters from Pahang and, increasingly, support from the British. Kuala Selangor fell to Kudin's forces in November 1873, and Raja Mahdi went into exile in 1874. The war had wrecked the economy, scattered mining investment, and — critically — given Britain a reason to step in.

## How did the British Residency and the FMS reshape Selangor?

Britain had officially held to a policy of non-intervention in the Malay states, but the disruption to trade and acts of piracy off the Selangor coast changed London's calculation. In 1874 the reigning ruler, **Sultan Abdul Samad** — the fourth Sultan, who reigned from 1857 to 1898 — accepted a British Resident on the model established that same year in Perak under the Pangkor Engagement. **James Guthrie Davidson** became the first British Resident of Selangor, with the young **Frank Swettenham** sent as his assistant; Swettenham would return in 1884 as the state's third Resident.

Under the Residential system, the Sultan reigned but was bound to accept the Resident's "advice" on all matters except Malay religion and custom. In 1896 Selangor joined with Perak, Pahang and Negeri Sembilan to form the **Federated Malay States (FMS)**, a centralised British administration coordinated by Swettenham, who became its first Resident-General. Kuala Lumpur, grown rich on tin, was chosen as the federal capital — binding Selangor's fortunes to the wider colonial economy.

## Who rules Selangor today?

The Bugis line endured through colonial rule, the Japanese occupation and independence. Modern Selangor is one of Malaysia's nine states with a hereditary ruler — one of [the nine royal houses](/malaysia/nine-royal-houses) whose sultans elect the national king (the Yang di-Pertuan Agong).

The reigning ruler is **Sultan Sharafuddin Idris Shah**, the ninth Sultan of Selangor, who succeeded his father in November 2001 and was crowned in 2003. The state capital is Shah Alam, while Klang remains the royal town, home to the official residence Istana Alam Shah. The modern state of [Selangor](/states/selangor) is Malaysia's most populous and industrialised, encircling the federal territories of Kuala Lumpur and Putrajaya.

## Why it still matters

Selangor's story compresses much of Malaysia's modern history into a single state: the Bugis diaspora that reshaped peninsular politics, the tin economy that drew in Chinese migration and built Kuala Lumpur, the civil war that opened the door to colonial rule, and the royal institution that survived all of it. Understanding how a Bugis warrior's son became a sultan in 1766 helps explain the layered society — Malay, Chinese, Indian — and the constitutional monarchy that govern Selangor and Malaysia today.
