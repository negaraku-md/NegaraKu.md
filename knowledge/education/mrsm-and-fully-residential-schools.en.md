Two names often come up when Malaysian families talk about the "best" boarding schools for their children: MRSM and SBP. Both are selective fully residential colleges, both fiercely competitive to get into — but they are run by two different bodies, under two different policy mandates.

## What sets MRSM apart from SBP?

MRSM — Maktab Rendah Sains MARA (MARA Junior Science College) — is run by the **Council of Trust for the People (MARA)**, a statutory body established on 1 March 1966 as an outcome of the first Bumiputera Economic Congress in 1965. MARA now sits under the ministry responsible for rural and regional development — a portfolio that has been known by several names, including the Ministry of Rural Development and now the Ministry of Rural and Regional Development. MARA states there are **57 MRSM** across the country, offering the **STREAM** programme (Science, Technology, Religion, Engineering, Arts and Mathematics).

Fully Residential Schools (SBP), by contrast, are run directly by the **Ministry of Education Malaysia (MOE)**. The MOE states that the emphasis on SBP is tied to the New Economic Policy, which stressed the need for a workforce in science and technology. The MOE also states that its goal is to develop the country's top talent and to democratise access to quality education.

## How do students get into each system?

The admission pathway is one of the most striking differences.

For **SBP**, students are screened through the **Special School Admission Assessment (PKSK)** — an MOE assessment that also serves as the entry route to National Religious Secondary Schools (SMKA), Technical Secondary Schools and the Royal Military College. Applications are made entirely online through the MOE portal, and selection takes into account the PKSK assessment along with academic achievement and co-curricular involvement. The detailed academic requirements for Form 1 and Form 4 admission are set by the MOE and may change from one intake to the next — candidates should refer to the MOE's PKSK portal for the current requirements.

For **MRSM**, MARA uses its own **MRSM Entrance Test (UKKM)**. According to MARA Director-General Datuk Zulfikri Osman on 7 February 2026, candidates must achieve a minimum score of **60 percent** in the UKKM to be offered a place — intended to ensure that students can cope with the academic demands of the institution. He also stated that places are allocated by income group: **60 percent for the B40 group, 30 percent M40 and 10 percent T20**. For that intake, around 7,300 Form 1 places were contested by 118,000 applicants, while 2,700 Form 4 places were contested by 96,000 applicants; MARA received around 45,000 appeals.

## Why does MRSM focus on Bumiputera students?

MARA was established specifically to advance the socioeconomic development of the Bumiputera community, a mandate expressed directly in MRSM's goals. MARA states that MRSM aims to produce holistic Bumiputera students who excel in science and technology and possess entrepreneurial qualities.

Following that mandate, MRSM maintains a **10 percent quota for non-Bumiputera students** — meaning the majority of places are reserved for Bumiputera students. On 5 September 2021, the then Minister of Rural Development, Datuk Seri Mahdzir Khalid, confirmed that the 10 percent non-Bumiputera quota was being maintained, alongside a 60 percent quota for high-achieving students from B40 families. On the same date, the government announced that MRSM was returning to its original policy allowing students from religious schools, National-type Chinese Schools and National-type Tamil Schools to apply.

This differs from SBP, which does not set a racial quota and is open to all Malaysian citizens who meet the academic requirements and sit the PKSK.

## What are the differing views on this policy?

MRSM's quota policy is part of a broader public discussion about affirmative action in Malaysia, and differing views exist.

Those who support the MRSM approach point to MARA's origins and statutory mandate: the institution was established specifically to close the socioeconomic gap for the Bumiputera community, and its science schools are one of the tools of that policy. From this viewpoint, the Bumiputera focus is not a policy exception but the founding purpose of MARA.

One issue frequently raised is the actual take-up under the non-Bumiputera quota. On 20 February 2025, MARA Chairman Datuk Dr Asyraf Wajdi Dusuki stated that MARA was not abolishing the 10 percent quota even though the trend in the non-Bumiputera admission rate was only around **1 percent**; he said the quota (introduced in 2002) was being maintained to encourage interaction and integration among students. This figure raises questions in public discourse about whether the places could be opened up more widely, and some also compare MARA's Bumiputera-focused model with SBP's national intake model.

This article takes no position in that debate. What is clear from the official policy record is that MRSM and SBP exist as two separate tiers within Malaysia's selective boarding-school system, each with its own governing body, entrance test and intake philosophy.
