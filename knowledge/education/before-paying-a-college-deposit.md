The deposit is usually the first money a family hands a college — and, once it is
paid, it is often the hardest to get back. It is also paid at exactly the moment
when the pressure to commit is highest: an offer in hand, a deadline on the
letter, and a counter clerk ready to take payment. This guide is the set of
checks worth doing **before** that money leaves your account, because almost all
of them are far easier to do now than to unwind later.

None of this requires distrusting the institution. It is simply that the things
that matter most — whether the exact programme is accredited, who awards the
final certificate, and what the refund clause actually says — are not always the
things a brochure leads with.

## 1. Check the specific programme on the MQA register — not just the institution

In Malaysia, accreditation attaches to the **programme**, not the institution.
The national quality-assurance body is the **Malaysian Qualifications Agency
(MQA)**, which operates under the Malaysian Qualifications Agency Act 2007 (Act
679) and maintains the **Malaysian Qualifications Register (MQR)**. An institution
can hold several accredited programmes while a particular course is only
provisionally accredited, or not yet listed at all.

So the check is specific: search the MQR at **mqr.gov.my** for the exact
programme name, at the exact level, delivered at the exact campus or branch you
are enrolling in. Save or screenshot the listing. If the programme is not there,
that is a question to put to the institution in writing — not necessarily a
dealbreaker, but something to understand before paying.

**Provisional vs Full Accreditation.** Programme accreditation runs in stages.
*Provisional Accreditation* is an earlier, design-stage approval that a
programme's structure meets the minimum standards — typically granted before the
programme has graduates and for a limited validity period. *Full Accreditation*
is the later recognition that the programme as delivered meets the standards, and
fully accredited programmes are the ones listed on the MQR. A provisional status
is not automatically a problem, but you should know which one your programme holds.

Accreditation is also what underpins the recognition you may be counting on: since
2017 MQA is the reference point for the Government's recognition of qualifications
for public-service appointment, and accredited status generally underpins credit
transfer and admission to further study. Which of those matters to you is worth
deciding deliberately — see section 5.

## 2. Confirm the institution is registered — registration and accreditation differ

These are two different approvals, and it is easy to conflate them. **Registration**
means the institution is lawfully allowed to operate: private higher educational
institutions (IPTS) are registered with the **Ministry of Higher Education (MOHE)**
under the **Private Higher Educational Institutions Act 1996 (Act 555)**; public
universities and recognised branch campuses operate under their own arrangements.
**Accreditation** is the separate, programme-level quality recognition by MQA
described above.

The practical point: a properly registered institution can still offer a
programme that is only provisionally accredited, or not yet accredited. Confirm
both — that the institution is a registered provider, and that your specific
programme is on the MQR.

## 3. For twinning / 3+0 / franchise programmes, confirm who awards the degree

Many Malaysian programmes are delivered in partnership with a foreign university —
a *2+1 twinning* (part of the study here, the rest at the partner abroad), a *3+0*
(the whole foreign degree delivered in Malaysia), a *franchise*, or a
*credit-transfer* arrangement. The labels are used inconsistently across the
sector, so do not rely on the term alone.

The question that actually matters is simple: **who issues the final
certificate?** In a twinning or 3+0 arrangement with a foreign partner, the
*foreign* university is usually the awarding body; in a local programme, the
Malaysian institution awards it. Get the answer in writing, naming the awarding
institution. While you are at it, confirm the Malaysian portion is registered and
accredited, since these arrangements also require the foreign partner's consent
and MOHE approval.

## 4. Get the full written fee schedule — the deposit is only a fraction

A deposit is a small part of the total. The full cost usually also includes a
registration or enrolment fee, tuition billed per semester or per year across the
whole programme, and other charges — resource and facility fees, examination fees,
insurance, international-student fees, and for a foreign-partner programme a
separate certification or awarding-body fee.

Ask for the **complete written fee schedule for the entire programme**, not just
the first year, and confirm what the deposit is credited against. Fee levels are
institution-specific and change, so the institution's own official written
schedule — not a verbal figure or an old brochure — is the thing to rely on.

## 5. Read the refund clause — there is no national cooling-off right to fall back on

This is the check most often skipped, and the one that most often causes regret.
Based on our research, Malaysia has **no national statutory cooling-off period and
no law guaranteeing a deposit refund** for private higher-education students. In
other words, there is no default safety net: what you can get back is governed
entirely by **the institution's own written refund policy**, which varies widely.

In practice, deposits are commonly non-refundable, and where a refund is offered
it often scales with how close to — or after — the start date you withdraw;
international-student visa or security deposits have their own cancellation terms.
So read the refund and withdrawal clause in the offer letter **before** you pay,
confirm in writing what is refundable and on what timeline, and keep every record.

If an institution's marketing was misleading, or a term is grossly one-sided, the
**[Consumer Protection Act 1999](/law/consumer-protection-act/) (Act 599)** — which covers misleading conduct and
unfair contract terms — *may* offer an avenue, and legal commentary argues that
students can be "consumers" under it. But that is a possible route, not a
guaranteed refund, so the written policy you agree to up front is what counts.

## 6. Decide which recognition you actually need — and verify that one

"Recognised" is not one thing. The dimensions a family might care about are
separate, and each is verified with a different body:

- **MQA accreditation** — the baseline programme-quality recognition, on the MQR.
- **Professional-body recognition / exemptions** — licensed professions keep their
  own lists. Engineering programmes, for example, are recognised for the
  profession through the **Engineering Accreditation Council (EAC)** under the
  **Board of Engineers Malaysia (BEM)**; accountancy bodies grant their own
  exemptions. This is additional to MQA recognition, not a substitute for it.
- **Public-service (JPA) recognition** — for government employment, based on MQA
  accreditation of the specific programme.
- **Overseas recognition** — for working, licensure or further study abroad, which
  depends on the foreign awarding university and the destination country's own
  regulators.

Decide which of these your child's goal actually depends on — a government job, a
professional licence, study abroad, a particular employer — and verify **that**
dimension with **that** body, in writing. Do not assume MQA accreditation alone
covers all of them.

## Common mistakes

- **Checking the institution, not the programme.** Accreditation is per programme;
  a trusted college can still have an unaccredited course.
- **Treating the deposit as refundable by default.** There is no national refund
  right; read the clause first.
- **Taking an articulation promise at face value.** "Guaranteed transfer to Year 3"
  is the partner university's decision — verify it with the partner, in writing.
- **Paying before seeing the full programme fee schedule.** Year-one figures hide
  the real total.
- **Assuming MQA accreditation equals every kind of recognition.** Professional,
  government and overseas recognition are each verified separately.
