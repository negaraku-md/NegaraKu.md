In any corner of Malaysia — on the edge of an old rubber plantation, or on the
fringe of a town — you can find a small Tamil school. Many of these schools have
a history spanning more than a century; they are not merely buildings, but
witnesses to a community's migration, labour and language.

Tamil schools (Sekolah Jenis Kebangsaan Tamil, **SJKT**) are part of Malaysia's
national education system. They are primary schools that use Tamil as the medium
of instruction. This article describes their origins, administration, funding,
present-day challenges and legal background in a neutral way — without arguing in
favour of any side, and presenting differing viewpoints alongside one another.

## What is a Tamil school?

SJKT are **national-type primary schools** that use Tamil as the main medium of
instruction. They follow the government's national curriculum; **Malay (Bahasa
Melayu) and English** are compulsory subjects. Pupils therefore receive their
basic education in Tamil while also developing the Malay-language skills needed
for national secondary education.

Tamil schools exist only at the primary level (Year 1 to Year 6). A similar
arrangement exists for the Chinese language; for details see
[Chinese education in Malaysia](/en/education/chinese-education-in-malaysia).
[The languages spoken in Malaysia](/en/malaysia/languages-spoken) explains the
overall picture of the various languages spoken in the country.

## Plantation-era origins

The roots of today's Tamil schools lie in the rubber and coconut plantations of
the **British colonial era**. From the late 19th century, large numbers of
labourers were brought from South India — mainly from Tamil Nadu — to work on
the estates. [The estate Tamil community](/en/arts-culture/estate-tamil-community)
describes this history in detail.

Colonial **labour legislation** required estates with a certain number of
workers' children to run a school. When the labour code was enforced in 1924,
the number of estates with schools in the Federated Malay States rose from 227 to
303. But these schools were largely kept **basic in nature**, with poor
facilities.

There is also a critical scholarly perspective on these origins. One historical
study argues that the colonial government and plantation companies set up these
schools in order to retain workers on the estates and provide them with a limited
education, and that the advancement of the Tamil community was not the primary
aim. This is one informed view; there are also alternative opinions. After
independence, these schools accepted government funding and were integrated as
**national-type schools**.

## Government schools and government-aided schools

Tamil schools fall into two tiers on the basis of administration and ownership:

| Category | Land/building | Government's role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Government school (fully-aided, SK-level) | Generally owned by the government | Government bears operating + capital costs |
| Government-aided school (partially-aided) | Mostly owned by the community/trust | Government bears teachers' salaries and operating costs; building upgrades/maintenance historically the burden of the board of management |

**Most** SJKT belong to the aided (partially-aided) category. As a result many
schools, especially those on old estate land, historically faced a funding gap
for building upgrades and maintenance.

Funding equity is a continually debated issue. One side says Tamil schools
receive a proportionately small share of education funding and that the
infrastructure of aided schools has lagged behind. On the other hand, the
government has provided maintenance allocations and dedicated funds; every annual
budget includes allocations for Tamil schools. Current operating funding is
largely allocated **on the basis of enrolment** — which, a university study has
pointed out, is not sufficient for small rural schools, recommending that
location and socio-economic status also be taken into account.

## Enrolment and school relocation

In recent years enrolment in Tamil schools has gradually declined. The Year 1
enrolment data released by the Tamil School Managers' Board (LPS) show this
trend:

| Year | Year 1 enrolment (nationwide) |
| --- | --- |
| 2023 | 11,712 |
| 2024 | 11,568 |
| 2025 | 11,021 |
| 2026 | 10,330 |

Urbanisation, family migration and the community's falling birth rate are cited
as reasons. According to 2026 parliamentary sources, around **528 SJKT** operate
nationwide; total enrolment stands at around 78,000 (the figures vary somewhat
depending on source and time).

A major challenge is **geographical mismatch**. About two-thirds (~67%) of Tamil
schools are in rural areas, but much of the Indian community has moved to urban
areas. As a result, some estate schools have very few pupils — the number of
schools with 30 or fewer pupils rose from 132 in May 2024 to 155 in 2025. As a
remedy, efforts are under way to **relocate** low-enrolment schools to urban
areas with large Indian populations; **multi-grade classes** have been introduced
in many schools.

## The absence of Tamil-medium secondary education

Government-funded Tamil-medium instruction exists **only at the primary level**.
Pupils who finish SJKT move on to **national secondary schools** (Sekolah
Menengah Kebangsaan), where the medium of instruction is Malay.

To ease this language transition, pupils who need it are given a one-year
**transition class** (Remove / Peralihan). At the secondary level, Tamil may be
taught as a **Pupils' Own Language** (POL) class — not as a medium of
instruction, only as a subject. The absence of dedicated government Tamil-medium
secondary schools is a subject of continuing debate about the continuity of
Tamil education.

## Legal protection and debates

The legal background of Tamil schools rests on two pillars. The first is
**Article 152** of the Constitution: this declares Malay the country's official
language; at the same time, it does not bar the right to **preserve, learn and
teach** the languages of other communities. The second is the **Education Act
1996 (Act 550)**: this defines national-type schools and provides for their
establishment and maintenance within the national education system.

Some parties sued, arguing that vernacular schools contravene Article 152. The
**High Court in 2021** and the **Court of Appeal in 2023** ruled that these
schools are constitutional; the basis of the courts' reasoning was that
vernacular schools are not public authorities and that Article 152 allows for the
teaching of other languages. In **February 2024, the Federal Court** denied leave
to appeal.

## Common misconceptions

- **Tamil schools are unconstitutional.** No — the courts have affirmed their
  constitutionality.
- **Only Tamil is taught in Tamil schools.** No — Malay and English are
  compulsory subjects; the national curriculum is followed.
- **There are Tamil-medium secondary schools too.** No — government Tamil-medium
  instruction exists only at the primary level; at secondary level Tamil can come
  only as a mother-tongue (POL) subject.
- **The government fully funds all Tamil schools.** In reality most are the aided
  (partially-aided) type; ownership and financial responsibility vary from school
  to school.
