Three letters — UEC — are enough to set off a debate that has run for more than half a century in Malaysian education politics. For some, it is an internationally recognised certificate that opens the door to the world's leading universities; for others, it touches on the standing of the national language and the country's education policy. Both views exist side by side, and both need to be understood to see the full picture.

## What is the UEC?

The Unified Examination Certificate (UEC), or Sijil Peperiksaan Bersama, is the school-leaving examination for students of Chinese Independent High Schools (SMPC) in Malaysia. It was first introduced in 1975 by Dong Zong — the United Chinese School Committees' Association of Malaysia (UCSCAM) — a non-governmental body that represents and safeguards Chinese education.

Its background stems from the country's early education policy. According to Free Malaysia Today reports, Chinese schools that chose to keep Mandarin as their medium of instruction left the national curriculum in 1961 and became private schools. Because these schools no longer had a government-standardised examination to assess their leavers, Dong Zong introduced the UEC as a unified examination to standardise assessment.

The main medium of instruction for the UEC is Mandarin. Yet its curriculum is not merely Chinese-focused subjects — students also take Mathematics, Science, History, Geography, as well as Malay and English. Malay is a compulsory subject. Many UEC students also sit for the SPM examination as private candidates; Dong Zong reports that the SPM Malay pass rate among SMPC students has exceeded 96% in recent years — 97.38% (2022), 97.11% (2023) and 96.65% (2024) — using the same SPM paper as other candidates. These figures were released by Dong Zong, a party that supports UEC recognition, and have not been independently verified against official Malaysian Examinations Board data.

## How widely is it accepted?

Acceptance of the UEC varies by place and institution. Internationally, the UEC senior-level certificate is regarded as academically equivalent to the STPM and A-level, and is reported to be accepted by more than 1,000 universities worldwide, including institutions in Singapore, Taiwan, China, the United Kingdom and Australia. Domestically, it is accepted by most private institutions of higher learning (IPTS).

However, at the Federal level, the UEC is **not** recognised as an entry qualification for most public universities (IPTA) or for the federal public service. This means UEC students who wish to enter an IPTA generally have to rely on other qualifications, such as the SPM followed by matriculation or the STPM.

According to Free Malaysia Today reports, the UEC is accepted in Sabah and Sarawak — the report describes these two states as the only states in Malaysia that accept the UEC for admission to state-owned universities and the state public service. For example, Sarawak reportedly recognised the UEC in 2016 for admission to state-owned colleges and universities as well as positions in the state public service.

## Why has its recognition become a long-running issue?

The issue of UEC recognition has surfaced repeatedly in policy discourse and election politics over the years. According to Free Malaysia Today reports, in 2018 Barisan Nasional made a related pledge when Najib Razak, then BN chairman and Prime Minister, offered UEC recognition if BN secured a strong mandate.

In the same year, the Pakatan Harapan manifesto also promised UEC recognition — allowing certificate holders to access public universities without an equivalence barrier — subject to the condition that applicants obtain at least a credit in Malay at SPM level. While in government, PH set up a task force to gather views, but that effort stalled after the change of government in 2020, and the task force was dissolved on 29 February that year.

In 2023, Education Minister Fadhlina Sidek was reported as stating that the ministry had no plans to recognise the UEC, citing the National Education Policy and the Education Act 1996. Prime Minister Anwar Ibrahim, meanwhile, was reported to have signalled that discussions on the UEC could only take place after the standing of Malay as the national language had been clarified.

## What are the arguments on different sides?

This debate involves several differing positions. It is important to state them in a balanced way, because it touches on matters of language, identity and policy that are sensitive in Malaysia.

**Those who support recognition** (including Dong Zong) generally put forward arguments such as:

- The UEC meets international academic standards and is accepted by the world's leading universities.
- UEC students maintain a high command of Malay, and are ready to meet a Malay credit requirement if one is set.
- Recognition opens wider access to educational and career opportunities, and helps retain local talent.
- The right to learn and use languages other than the national language is protected under Article 152 of the Federal Constitution.

**Those who raise concerns or oppose it** generally put forward arguments such as:

- Concern that recognition could undermine the standing of Malay as the national language and the main medium of instruction in the education system.
- The view that the UEC is not conducted in the national language and lies outside the framework of the National Education Policy.
- Concern over the impact on national identity and racial integration, as well as the uniformity of the country's education standards.

It should be stressed that this article does not take any position in the debate. Its aim is to convey the facts and the range of views that exist, not to judge any side.

## How many students are involved?

The scale of this system gives context to the debate. According to a written parliamentary reply by Education Minister Fadhlina Sidek, as reported by Free Malaysia Today, in 2026 a total of 75,144 students were in the Chinese private secondary school system — that is, 19.4% of all 387,406 Chinese students at secondary level nationwide. The remainder were in Ministry of Education schools (260,699, or 67.3%) and other private education institutions (51,563, or 13.3%).

The same report stated that the total number of students in Chinese private secondary schools has fallen below 80,000, after peaking at around 81,000 students.

## What comes next

As of this draft, the official position remains the same: the UEC is not recognised by the Federal Government for admission to most IPTA and the federal public service, while state-level acceptance (Sabah and Sarawak) and international acceptance continue to exist in parallel. Dong Zong has stated that it will continue to fight for UEC students' access to academic programmes at public universities.

For students and parents, the common practical step is to understand the entry requirements of each pathway early — including whether an SPM or STPM is needed as an additional qualification for the IPTA — and to check the specific requirements of each university or state. Since policy can change with future government decisions, referring to official announcements from the Ministry of Education and the relevant state governments is the most accurate source of information.
