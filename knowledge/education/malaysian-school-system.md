For a child growing up in Malaysia, the question "which school?" carries more meaning than in many countries. It is partly about distance and reputation, as everywhere — but it is also a choice between a national school that teaches mainly in Malay and a national-type school that teaches in Chinese or Tamil. Both are part of the same public system, follow the same national curriculum, and lead to the same national examinations. Understanding how that system fits together is the first step to navigating it.

This guide sets out the structure of Malaysian schooling — the stages, the school types, the examinations and the governing law — in neutral, descriptive terms.

## Who runs the system?

School education in Malaysia is the responsibility of the **Ministry of Education (MOE)**, with each state and federal territory also running its own education department to coordinate local matters. The system is governed principally by the **Education Act 1996**, which replaced the earlier Education Act 1961 and sets out the structure of national education, the curriculum and the categories of school.

Public education in the national system is provided free to Malaysians. Alongside it sit private schools, international schools and homeschooling, which operate under their own arrangements within the law.

## The five stages

Malaysian education is organised into five broad stages:

| Stage | Typical ages | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Preschool | 4–6 | Kindergarten; offered by government, private and community providers |
| Primary | 7–12 | Six years (Years 1–6); compulsory |
| Secondary | 13–17 | Five years (Forms 1–5); ends with SPM |
| Post-secondary | 18–19 | Form Six (STPM), matriculation, foundation, diplomas |
| Tertiary | 18+ | Colleges, polytechnics and universities |

Primary education is **compulsory**. Under the Education Act 1996 as amended in 2002, a Malaysian child must be enrolled in primary school on reaching six years of age by the start of the school year.

## National schools and national-type schools

At the primary level, Malaysia's public system has two categories of school, a structure that dates back to the **Razak Report** of the 1950s, which designed a unified national education system with a common curriculum taught through different mediums of instruction:

- **National schools (Sekolah Kebangsaan):** Government primary and secondary schools in which the main medium of instruction is Malay, the national language.
- **National-type schools (Sekolah Jenis Kebangsaan):** Government or government-aided primary schools in which the main medium of instruction is **Chinese** (SJKC) or **Tamil** (SJKT). In these schools, Malay and English are compulsory subjects.

The national-type schools are commonly referred to as **vernacular schools**. The defining feature of the design is that, whatever the medium of instruction, all these schools teach the **same national curriculum** and lead to the same national examinations. At secondary level, some schools are known as national-type secondary schools (SMJK), reflecting the historical conversion of certain Chinese-medium secondary schools into the national system.

This is a structural description, not an evaluation: the place of vernacular education is a long-standing and sometimes contested public policy topic in Malaysia, and this page does not take a position on it.

## Primary and secondary in more detail

**Primary school** runs for six years (Years 1 to 6). Historically, pupils sat a national examination, the Primary School Achievement Test (UPSR), near the end of primary school; this assessment has since been reformed in favour of school-based assessment (see the verification note on this page for the current arrangement).

**Secondary school** runs for five years, divided into:

- **Lower secondary (Forms 1–3):** a three-year foundation phase.
- **Upper secondary (Forms 4–5):** a two-year, more specialised phase that ends with the **Sijil Pelajaran Malaysia (SPM)** — the Malaysian Certificate of Education, the system's major school-leaving examination.

Results in SPM largely determine a student's post-secondary pathway.

## After school: the post-secondary routes

SPM is a fork in the road rather than the end of it. Common next steps include:

- **Form Six**, a two-year pre-university programme ending in the **Sijil Tinggi Persekolahan Malaysia (STPM)**, broadly comparable to A-levels.
- **Matriculation**, a pre-university programme run by the MOE.
- **Foundation and diploma programmes** at colleges and universities.

From there, students progress to **tertiary education** at polytechnics, colleges and universities, public or private.

## How the school year works

The academic calendar, term structure and school holidays are set nationally and interact with Malaysia's public holidays. Because these dates shift from year to year — and differ slightly between two regional groupings of states — they are covered in their own guide: the [academic calendar and school holidays](/en/education/academic-calendar-and-school-holidays/).

## Common misconceptions

- **"Vernacular schools teach a different curriculum."** No — national and national-type schools follow the same national curriculum; the difference is the main medium of instruction, with Malay and English compulsory in national-type schools.
- **"Secondary school ends with UPSR."** No — UPSR was a primary-level test (since reformed); the major secondary school-leaving examination is SPM, at the end of Form 5.
- **"Education is only compulsory to the end of secondary school."** The explicit compulsory stage under the Education Act 1996 is primary education; confirm any later extensions with the MOE.
- **"All schools are run directly by the federal ministry."** The MOE sets national policy and curriculum, but each state also has an education department, and private, international and home schooling operate alongside the national system.

## What's next

Malaysia's school system reflects the country it serves: a shared national framework delivered through more than one language of instruction, holding a common curriculum and common examinations across different communities.
