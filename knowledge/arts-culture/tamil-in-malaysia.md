On the old rubber estates of Malaya, some of the earliest classrooms were sheds and huts, and the lessons were in Tamil. According to a historical study published in the journal of the Malaysian Historical Society, a basic primary education in Tamil was offered on plantations from the early twentieth century, provided for under labour codes of the day and, for a long time, taught by clerks and part-timers rather than trained teachers.

That estate schoolroom is where the Tamil language enters the story of modern Malaysian schooling. More than a century later, Tamil is still taught as a medium of instruction in a network of government-supported primary schools, and its right to be used, taught and learned sits inside the country's constitutional framework.

This article describes three things plainly: who speaks Tamil in Malaysia, how the Tamil-medium school system is organised, and where the language stands in law. It reports what the figures and the provisions say, without evaluating policy.

## The community

Tamil in Malaysia is, above all, the mother tongue of much of the Indian community. According to the Department of Statistics Malaysia (DOSM), Malaysia's population in 2024 was estimated at 34.1 million, of whom 30.7 million were citizens. Among citizens, DOSM reported the ethnic composition as Bumiputera 70.4 percent, Chinese 22.4 percent, Indians 6.5 percent, and Others.

The census records ethnicity rather than a separate tally of Tamil speakers. The large majority of Malaysian Indians, however, trace their origins to Tamil-speaking regions of southern India, so Tamil is the most widely used Indian-community language in the country. It is concentrated in the states where the Indian population has historically been largest, including Selangor, Perak and Penang.

## The schools: SJKT

The most visible institution of the Tamil language in Malaysia is the Tamil national-type primary school, known in Malay as **Sekolah Jenis Kebangsaan Tamil (SJKT)**. These are government or government-aided schools within the national education system that use Tamil as the medium of instruction, while Bahasa Melayu and English are compulsory subjects for every pupil.

Answering a question in the Dewan Rakyat in June 2026, the Ministry of Education reported the current scale of the system:

| Measure | Figure (as at 31 May 2026) |
| --- | --- |
| SJKT operating nationwide | 528 |
| Total enrolment | 78,501 pupils |

The Ministry also noted that registrations to sit the Tamil language paper in the Sijil Pelajaran Malaysia (SPM) examination had eased gradually — from 8,023 candidates in 2023 to 7,494 registered for 2026 — a trend the Ministry linked to urbanisation and the closure of estates, while stating that it remained committed to strengthening Tamil-language education.

Because many SJKT began as estate schools serving small, dispersed communities, a number today have low enrolment. The Ministry has described managing this through combined classes and, in some cases, relocating school sites to areas of higher demand.

## Where the language stands in law

The starting point is **Article 152 of the Federal Constitution**. Its basic rule is that the national language is the Malay language, to be used for official purposes. Read alongside that rule are two provisos that bear directly on Tamil:

- **Article 152(1)(a)** provides that no person shall be prohibited or prevented from using (other than for official purposes), or from teaching or learning, any other language.
- **Article 152(1)(b)** provides that nothing in the clause shall prejudice the right of the Federal Government or of any State Government to preserve and sustain the use and study of the language of any other community in the Federation.

The Government's own MyGovernment portal states the same point in plain terms: while Malay holds official status, Malaysia's communities are free to use other languages, "such as Mandarin by the Chinese and Tamil by the Indians."

Within the education system, Tamil-medium national-type schools operate under the **Education Act 1996 (Act 550)**, which recognises national-type primary schools that use Chinese or Tamil as the medium of instruction.

The constitutionality of that arrangement was tested in court. In November 2023 the **Court of Appeal** unanimously dismissed challenges brought by several organisations and held that vernacular (national-type) schools are not public authorities, that the use of Tamil or Chinese as a medium of instruction in them is therefore not for an "official purpose", and that it is protected under Article 152(1)(a). The court also held that the relevant sections of the Education Act 1996 are not inconsistent with the Federal Constitution.

## The historical roots

The presence of Tamil in Malaysia is tied to labour migration under British colonial rule. From the late nineteenth century into the first decades of the twentieth, workers were recruited from southern India — largely through the *kangani* system — to work on rubber, coffee and other plantations, as well as on railways, roads and public works.

Schooling followed the workforce. Estate Tamil schools grew up on the plantations themselves, initially informal and under-resourced, before state governments progressively took over their organisation and, from the 1930s, trained teachers began to appear. After independence, these estate and community schools were drawn into the national education framework as national-type schools, the lineage that today's SJKT continue.

## The wider language landscape

Beyond the classroom, Tamil has a place in Malaysia's broadcasting and public life. The national broadcaster, Radio Televisyen Malaysia (RTM), operates a national Tamil-language radio service, Minnal FM, carried across Peninsular Malaysia on its transmitter network; Tamil-language television and a Tamil-language press also serve the community. Tamil is likewise one of the languages offered as an examinable subject in national assessments, including the SPM.

Taken together, these are the ordinary channels through which a community language is sustained: home and community use, a school system, examinations, and broadcasting.

## Common misconceptions

**"Article 152 bans other languages."** It does the opposite for non-official use: Article 152(1)(a) and (b) expressly protect the use, teaching and learning of other languages, and the preservation of community languages, outside official purposes.

**"Tamil-medium schools sit outside the national system."** SJKT are national-type schools within the education system under the Education Act 1996; Bahasa Melayu and English are compulsory subjects in them.

**"Every Malaysian Indian speaks Tamil, and only Indians do."** The census records ethnicity, not language. The large majority of Malaysian Indians have Tamil roots, but the community also includes speakers of other Indian languages, and Tamil is learned by some outside the community as well.
