Around Qingming, before the sky is fully light, the foot of Bukit Cina in Melaka is already lined with parked cars. Families carry fresh flowers, incense, candles and offerings as they walk up the rolling slopes toward their ancestors' graves. Some first offer incense at a small stone niche beside the grave inscribed with the characters for "Houtu", then turn to tend the ancestor's headstone — pulling weeds, repainting faded carved characters, and laying out offerings. The densely packed graves along the slope, all facing the same way, are the resting place of generation after generation of Chinese who came south.

In Malaysia, a Chinese public cemetery like this has a distinctive name — **yishan**. It is not merely a place of burial, but a site of memory sustained together by clan associations, temples and an entire community. This article only describes the origins, customs and history of the yishan, without passing judgment on any religious belief.

## What a "Yishan" Is

"Yishan" is the term used across Malaysia, Singapore and the wider Nanyang region for a Chinese public cemetery, and is not commonly used elsewhere. The "yi" is taken from the sense of a righteous act, of charitable help: many of the Chinese labourers who came south in earlier generations were single men who had gone abroad to make a living, and once they died far from home, if there were no kin to handle their affairs there might be no one to bury them or sweep their graves. So communities of fellow-townsmen would fund and set aside a hillside specifically to bury their own — and such charitable, mutual-aid public burial hills came to be called yishan. The character "shan" (hill) comes from graves mostly being built against the hillsides.

A yishan was therefore never a commercial cemetery from the outset, but part of community welfare. It provided fellow-townsmen with a place for burial and worship, and eased the fear of "dying far from home with no one to pay respects". To this day, many old yishan still retain this public, non-profit character.

## Clan Associations, Houtu and Tua Pek Kong: Who Guards the Yishan

The management of a yishan often falls to **dialect-based clan associations, native-place bodies or temple committees**. Clan associations are formed by place of origin or dialect group (such as Cantonese, Hokkien, Hakka and others), handling burial affairs for their fellow-townsmen on one hand, and on the other holding collective worship for departed kinsmen at festivals such as Qingming and the spring and autumn rites. Headstones often carry the deceased's ancestral place name, so that the origin of a person's native soil endures with the stone.

Within the yishan there are also two common "guardians". One is **Houtu** — the earth deity of the burial ground, regarded as the god who governs the whole domain of the burial hill; when sweeping tombs, many families first pay respects to the Houtu shrine beside the grave before honouring their ancestors. The Houtu niche is often right at one side of the headstone, sometimes just a stone slab inscribed with the two characters for "Houtu". The other is **Tua Pek Kong (Fu De Zheng Shen)** — a deity widely worshipped by Malaysia's Chinese, with temples found everywhere; some land held under a Fu De temple also contains a yishan, with the same committee caring for both the burial hill and the temple's incense. These arrangements vary from place to place.

## Feng Shui and the Siting of Yishan

Chinese tradition attends to the feng shui of the "yin dwelling" (the grave), so the distribution and orientation of yishan follow discernible patterns. Grave sites are mostly chosen on sloping ground that is **backed by hills and facing water**, and the orientation, height and arrangement of each grave are all considered, believed to bear on the blessings that flow to one's descendants. This is one reason many yishan are set on hills and carry "shan" (hill) in their names.

In old yishan you can often see rows of graves facing roughly the same way, rising tier upon tier with the lie of the hill, with space left in front for offerings and for family members to kneel; the more elaborate ones even have stone-built "tomb arms" embracing the grave body. These forms and refinements make the yishan not only a place of burial but an open-air display of folk craft and custom.

## Bukit Cina: The Largest Old Chinese Burial Hill Overseas

Melaka's **Bukit Cina (Sam Poh Shan, also rendered as "China Hill")** is Malaysia's most representative Chinese burial hill. Together with the adjoining hills it covers roughly twenty-five hectares and holds **over twelve thousand graves**, some dating back to the Ming dynasty, and is often described by travel and reference sources as **the largest traditional Chinese burial hill outside China** (this "largest" claim is mostly widely-repeated rather than a strictly verified conclusion).

Bukit Cina is tied to a long-told legend: it is said that in the fifteenth century the Ming princess Hang Li Po was married to the Sultan of Melaka, and that this hill was land the Sultan gave; at its foot remain relics such as the Seven Wells of Zheng He, and beside it stands the Poh San Teng temple. A development plan last century once threatened this burial hill, but through sustained advocacy by the Chinese community and the public, Bukit Cina was ultimately preserved, becoming the cultural landscape it is today, at once cemetery and park.

## Kuala Lumpur's Kwong Tong Cemetery and Burial Hills in the City

In the capital, **Kwong Tong Cemetery** is one of the best-known Chinese burial hills. It was designated in **1895**, when Cantonese overseas Chinese applied to the Sultan of Selangor and the colonial government; the main grounds are at Bukit Seputeh, with additional sections elsewhere. The founding pioneer **Kapitan Yap Ah Loy** rests here (he died in 1885 and was later reinterred here). The grounds also hold a memorial to civilians who died in the Second World War and memorials related to the Nanyang Volunteer Drivers and the war of resistance, making it not only a cemetery but a site of Chinese history and collective memory. In 2006, Kwong Tong Cemetery was listed as a Heritage Park, and in recent years it has joined with the Hokkien cemetery and others to pursue heritage-listing and academic research.

Similar stories play out elsewhere: as cities expand, many old urban yishan have faced pressure to relocate and exhume, and have in turn been preserved or resettled by negotiation in various ways through the efforts of the public and communities.

## Feng Shui, Exhumation and the Pressure on Land

Increasingly scarce urban land is the biggest reality facing yishan today. Traditional burial takes up a large area, many old yishan have long been close to full, and some must also face the relocation brought by development and **exhumation** (moving graves, gathering the bones for reburial). So a shift from burial toward placement in a columbarium or a garden cemetery is under way.

| Form of burial | Common practice | Characteristics |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Traditional earth burial | Site chosen by feng shui, interment for peace, a headstone set up with a Houtu shrine | Takes up much space, attentive to orientation; most old yishan are of this kind |
| Exhumation and reburial | When the original grave's term ends or it must be ceded, the bones are gathered and reburied or cremated | Mostly driven by land or development pressure |
| Columbarium (ash niche) | After cremation, the ashes are placed in a niche within the tower | Small footprint, easy to manage and to visit collectively |
| Memorial garden | A planned cemetery, replacing dense grave mounds with greenery and landscaping | High use of space, a tidier setting |

Cremation and placement in a niche, or moving to a planned memorial garden, has become the choice of more and more families. Practices are not uniform across families and communities, however, and many people still value ancestral graves and the tradition of earth burial.

## Qingming: The Busiest Day of the Year at the Yishan

The busiest time of year at the yishan is **Qingming**. In the roughly ten days around Qingming, Chinese families often go as a whole household to sweep tombs and honour their ancestors — pulling weeds, cleaning the headstone, repainting faded carved characters, and laying out offerings, by custom paying respects to the Houtu first and the ancestors after. In Guangdong, tomb-sweeping is colloquially called "bai shan" (paying respects at the hill), an expression that travelled to the Nanyang region with the migrants.

In recent years, some yishan managements have promoted a simpler, eco-friendly approach and the proper handling of rubbish after tomb-sweeping, and some families now remember their forebears through niche visits or even online remembrance instead. Whatever the changes in form, a visit to the yishan at Qingming remains, for many Malaysian Chinese families, a way of sustaining the memory of their bloodline.

## Common Misconceptions

**Treating a yishan as an ordinary commercial cemetery.** Yishan arose from native-place mutual aid and charitable burial, are mostly managed by clan associations or temples, and carry a public, welfare character quite unlike a purely commercially run cemetery.

**Assuming "Houtu" is some major deity.** In the burial-ground context, Houtu refers to the earth deity guarding that particular burial hill; at tomb-sweeping people usually pay respects to the Houtu first and the ancestors after, and it cannot simply be equated with other Houtu beliefs found elsewhere.

**Assuming Bukit Cina's being "the largest" is a settled conclusion.** "The largest Chinese burial hill outside China" is a widely-repeated claim, and the exact number of graves differs between sources; what is certain is that it is very large and long-established.

**Assuming yishan never change.** Under land pressure and urban development, exhumation and reburial, columbaria and memorial gardens are gradually changing the way the Chinese bury their dead and tend their graves.
