The gamelan set moved from one court to another not because of a sale or a seizure — but because of marriage. When a princess moved to her husband's palace, the instruments, dancers and songs moved with her. That is how gamelan became the property of the Malay courts.

## How did gamelan reach the Malay courts?

According to JKKN cultural mapping records, joget gamelan was first performed before the public in **Pekan, Pahang in 1811**, at the wedding of Tengku Hussain. It was here that this art form took root among the Pahang royal family.

It then crossed over to Terengganu through the family ties between the two courts. The figure most often mentioned is **Tengku Ampuan Mariam**, a Pahang princess who married Sultan Sulaiman Badrul Alam Syah of Terengganu in **1913**. She brought a gamelan set from Pahang, trained local musicians, and organised the dance and its costumes. JKKN records note a repertoire that originally spanned 77 pieces and now has only **12 pieces** still performed — among them Timang Burung, Lambang Sari, Togok, Topeng and Perang Manggung.

## What sets Malay gamelan apart from Javanese gamelan?

Malay gamelan shares its roots with the gamelan tradition of the Malay archipelago. But when it was absorbed into the Pahang and Terengganu courts, its instruments were **redesigned to suit Malay design and sound** — an adaptation that, according to the Warisan KL portal, makes it unique and different from other gamelan. Its focus is an orchestra of metal keys and gongs accompanying a court dance, not a large arrangement with vocals like Javanese gamelan.

The Malay court ensemble usually consists of seven types of instrument:

| Instrument | Role |
| --- | --- |
| Keromong | A row of small gongs on a wooden frame; carries the main melody |
| Saron (large & small) | Struck metal keys; carry the melodic line |
| Gambang | Wooden keys struck with a mallet |
| Kanong | A knobbed gong of medium pitch; marks the rhythm |
| Gong (a pair) | Emphasises phrases and rhythmic endings |
| Gendang | A double-headed drum; controls the overall tempo |

This arrangement underscores Malay gamelan's character as a metallic percussion ensemble — its emphasis is on the interweaving of melody and rhythm, not on singing.

## Why did it almost disappear — and how was it rediscovered?

Gamelan activity halted around **1942** because of the Second World War, and its set of instruments lay silent at Istana Kolam, Terengganu. According to Warisan KL, it was only "rediscovered" in **1966**, when Tan Sri Mubin Sheppard traced this nearly-extinct Malay tradition and found the stored gamelan set.

The original gamelan set was then **returned to Pahang in 1973**, and is now displayed at the Pahang Museum in Pekan — back in the state where it was first performed before the public more than a century earlier.

## Is Malay gamelan official heritage?

Yes. In the 2009 declaration, the Department of National Heritage listed **"Gamelan"** as a National Heritage under the category of **Intangible Heritage Object — Performing Arts (Arts)**. Recognition of this kind is carried out under the National Heritage Act 2005 (Act 645).
