When we read a Malay word today — on a road sign, in a newspaper, on a phone screen — we read it in the same letters as the English alphabet: a, b, c, d and so on. That script is called Rumi script. It is so ordinary that many are not aware that Rumi is not the original script of the Malay language, and that its neat and uniform form today is the result of a standardisation effort that took more than a hundred years.

This article explains Rumi script descriptively: what Rumi is, where it came from, how its spelling system developed through several major reforms, who coordinates it, and what its relationship is with [Jawi script](/en/arts-culture/jawi-script) today.

## Origins of the romanisation of Malay

Rumi script is a writing system that uses the Latin alphabet to write [Malay](/en/arts-culture/bahasa-melayu). Unlike Jawi, which is based on the Arabic letters and written from right to left, Rumi uses Latin letters and is written from left to right.

The process of converting Malay into Latin letters — called *romanisation* — began long before British colonisation. According to studies in the history of language planning, records of Malay in the Latin alphabet can be traced as early as the 16th century, when European traders, travellers and missionaries recorded Malay words using the letters they knew (Ismail Dahaman, *Perancangan Bahasa Malaysia*, 1987). At this early stage, spelling was highly inconsistent: each writer spelled according to the pronunciation and conventions of his own mother tongue, whether Portuguese, Dutch or English.

This situation changed when the British administration in Malaya needed a consistent way of writing Malay for the purposes of governance and education. It was from here that the era of officially coordinated Rumi spelling systems began.

## The Wilkinson system and the Za'ba system

The first official standardisation effort is associated with **R.J. Wilkinson**, a British officer and scholar. In 1904, a committee led by him produced a Rumi spelling system under the government of the Federated Malay States. That system — often called the Wilkinson Spelling — became the official Rumi spelling in administration and schools throughout Malaya and some other British territories.

About two decades later, the Malay language figure **Zainal Abidin Ahmad**, better known by his pen name **Za'ba**, introduced yet another spelling system. Introduced around 1924 while he was serving at the Sultan Idris Training College, the Za'ba system refined the Wilkinson spelling and became the official spelling in schools and administration in Malaya, Singapore and Brunei by the 1930s. Because of its widespread use in schools, it was also known as the *School Spelling*.

Among the features that distinguished the Za'ba system from Wilkinson was the way vowels were marked. Za'ba introduced the mark ⟨ĕ⟩ to distinguish the *e pepet* (schwa) sound from the *e taling* sound, and adjusted the spelling of vowels in closed syllables to come closer to pronunciation — for example, *buruk* was spelled *burok*. He also used the hyphen to separate certain affixes from the root word, such as *di-jual*.

## The New Rumi Spelling of 1972

Throughout the mid-20th century, Rumi spelling in Malaya and in Indonesia developed separately, producing two different conventions for the same sounds. Several attempts to unify them — including the Congress Spelling proposal that emerged from the Congress of Malay Language and Letters in 1956 — did not gain wide acceptance.

Real unification came in 1972. On **16 August 1972**, Malaysia and Indonesia simultaneously proclaimed a single common Rumi spelling system. In Malaysia, this system was proclaimed by Prime Minister Tun Abdul Razak and was known as the **New Rumi Spelling**; in Indonesia it was known as *Ejaan Yang Disempurnakan* (EYD) and was proclaimed by President Suharto. In Malaysia, this system replaced the Za'ba spelling, while in Indonesia it replaced the Republican (Soewandi) spelling system.

The 1972 reform reconciled many differences between the two countries. Among its main changes, the letters for certain sounds were unified: *ch* in Malay spelling and *tj* in Indonesian spelling both became **c**; the Indonesian *dj* became **j**; and *sj* became **sy**. The diacritic mark on the *e pepet* (⟨ĕ⟩) inherited from the Za'ba system was also dropped, so that both the *e pepet* and *e taling* sounds were spelled with the same letter **e**.

This coordination did not stop in 1972. Language cooperation between the two countries was later institutionalised through the Indonesian-Malaysian Language Council (MBIM), founded at the end of 1972, and became **MABBIM** (Majlis Bahasa Brunei-Indonesia-Malaysia) when Brunei joined it in 1985. Through MABBIM, Rumi spelling and terminology continue to be coordinated on an ongoing basis across the three countries.

## The role of Dewan Bahasa dan Pustaka

In Malaysia, the institution responsible for coordinating, maintaining and developing Rumi spelling is [Dewan Bahasa dan Pustaka](/en/arts-culture/dewan-bahasa-dan-pustaka) (DBP), a statutory body under the Federal Government. DBP manages the implementation of the New Rumi Spelling and publishes official guidelines documenting the methods of spelling, term formation and standard pronunciation of Malay.

This role means that Rumi spelling is not something frozen. When new words enter the Malay language — from the fields of science, technology or international borrowings — DBP determines how to spell them in Rumi according to the existing guidelines, and publishes those decisions through dictionaries, word registers and online reference resources such as the Malay Literary Reference Centre (PRPM). In this way, a consistent Rumi spelling can be maintained across publishing, education and administration.

## Rumi and Jawi today

The official position of Rumi script is established in law. **Section 9 of the National Language Act 1963/67 (Act 32)** provides that the script for the national language is the Rumi script — with the same proviso expressly stating that the provision does not prohibit the use of the Jawi script. In other words, the law makes Rumi the official script, but allows Jawi to continue to be used.

Today, Rumi is the principal script of Malay in almost all everyday contexts: mainstream education, media, business, law and administration. Jawi script, meanwhile, is preserved as heritage and continues to be used in Islamic education, calligraphy, religious and cultural materials, and on certain official signs and emblems. These two scripts — one Latin, one Arabic — remain two ways of writing the same Malay language, each carrying its own history and role.
