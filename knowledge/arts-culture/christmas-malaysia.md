On the evening of 24 December, a church in Kota Kinabalu or Kuching fills for a late service, while a few hundred kilometres away on the Peninsula the same date is an ordinary working Thursday. The next morning, 25 December, the whole country is off work. That split — one date, two very different statuses — is the neatest way to understand how Christmas sits in Malaysia: a religious festival for one community, a public holiday for everyone, and a season of open doors that many take part in regardless of faith.

## A public holiday in every state

Christmas Day, 25 December, is a public holiday observed across all of Malaysia — every state and federal territory.

The statutory route differs by region. In Peninsular Malaysia, public holidays are set out in the First Schedule of the **Holidays Act 1951** (Act 369), and Christmas Day appears among them. Sabah and Sarawak, which retained their own holiday laws on joining the federation, provide for public holidays under their own ordinances. Each year the Federal Government also publishes a consolidated national holiday list through the Cabinet Office of the Prime Minister's Department.

The practical result is uniform: whatever the legal instrument, 25 December is a non-working public holiday nationwide, in the same way that Hari Raya Aidilfitri, Deepavali, Wesak and Chinese New Year are.

## The one date that is not nationwide: Christmas Eve

Sabah goes one step further. **Christmas Eve, 24 December, is a state public holiday in Sabah**, gazetted under the Sabah Holidays Ordinance (Cap. 56). It is not a holiday in the rest of the country.

So in Sabah the season carries two consecutive days off; elsewhere, 24 December is a normal working day even though the following day is a holiday. This is a common point of confusion for visitors and for employers scheduling across states.

## Who observes it religiously

Christmas is, in the first place, a Christian religious festival commemorating the birth of Jesus Christ. Malaysia's Christian community is a minority: the Department of Statistics Malaysia's 2020 census recorded about **2.9 million Christians** in a national population of 32.4 million.

That community is not evenly spread. Among the states, **Sarawak recorded the largest number of Christians**, and Sabah also has a substantial Christian population; on the Peninsula, Christian congregations are concentrated in the larger cities. This is why the day's religious character is most visible in East Malaysia, where in some districts it is a major community occasion.

Observance commonly centres on church services. A **Christmas Eve Midnight Mass** or evening service is a widespread practice, followed by services on Christmas morning, with carols, readings and gatherings. Different denominations and congregations keep their own forms; the census releases used here do not break the Christian count into denominations, and this article does not attempt to.

## How it is marked more widely

Beyond the churches, Christmas is also marked as a broad seasonal and social occasion — and this is where participation reaches well past the Christian community.

- **The open house.** Malaysia's open-house custom — hosting friends, neighbours and colleagues of any background for a shared meal — is applied to Christmas as it is to Hari Raya, Deepavali and Chinese New Year. Christian families who host an open house commonly welcome guests of other faiths, and the visiting runs in both directions across the festival calendar.
- **Public and retail decorations.** Shopping malls, hotels and city centres put up trees, lights and displays, and run seasonal sales, from around late November.
- **Seasonal events.** Carol performances, markets and community events appear in the weeks before the day, often organised by malls, hotels and community groups rather than only by churches.

The two layers coexist without merging: the religious observance is a matter for the Christian community, while the decorative and social season is something many Malaysians engage with as part of a shared festival calendar.

## Common points of confusion

- **"Christmas Eve is a national holiday."** It is not. Only Sabah gazettes 24 December as a public holiday; nationwide, the holiday is Christmas Day itself.
- **"It is only a commercial or Western season."** The decorations and sales are real, but for the Christian community — most numerous in Sarawak and Sabah — 25 December is a religious observance, not merely a retail event.
- **"Christmas is celebrated the same way everywhere in Malaysia."** Its visibility varies a great deal by region. It is a prominent community occasion in parts of East Malaysia and a more low-key one in much of the Peninsula.
