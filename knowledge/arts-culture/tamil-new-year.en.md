The first dawn of the month of Chithirai. In many Tamil homes a tray was
already prepared the night before — mango, banana, betel leaf, a little gold
jewellery, a mirror. On waking in the morning, before looking at anything
else, this tray should be the first thing seen. This is **kanni** — an
expression of the belief that the first sight on New Year's Day sets the tone
for the whole year.

Tamil New Year — or **Chithirai Puthandu** — falls on the first day of the
month of Chithirai in the Tamil solar calendar, usually on **14 April**. It is
a cultural and seasonal occasion; this article neither explains nor evaluates
the religious meaning of any ritual — it describes the common customs of
Malaysia's Tamil community in a neutral way.

## The Day and the Calendar

The Tamil calendar is a solar calendar, and Chithirai is its first month. The
day Chithirai begins is the start of the new year — in the Gregorian calendar
this falls around 14 April. On the same day come **Vishu**, the regional new
year of Kerala's Malayalees, and **Vaisakhi**, the new-year/harvest occasion of
the Sikhs and Punjabis; in Malaysia these three communities generally celebrate
their respective festivals on the same day. This article focuses on the Tamil
tradition.

> In 2008 the Tamil Nadu government tried to move the new year to the first day
> of Thai; in 2011 that change was reversed and the Chithirai day was
> reinstated. Malaysian Tamils generally follow the Chithirai day.

## Kanni: The First Sight

At the heart of the New Year customs is the kanni. The night before, auspicious
items are arranged on a tray — usually three fruits (mango, banana, jackfruit),
betel leaf and areca nut, gold or silver jewellery, a coin, flowers. Many place
the tray before a mirror in the prayer room. Seeing this sight first thing on
waking at dawn rests on the belief that it makes a good start to the coming
year.

## Home, Kolam and Worship

- **Kolam** — patterns drawn at the doorway of the house with coloured rice
  flour; at its centre a **brass lamp (kuthuvilakku / maa vilakku)** is often lit.
- **New clothes** — wearing new garments and decorating the home with flowers
  and festoons.
- **Temple worship** — going to the temple with the family to worship, and
  seeking the blessings of elders, is customary; many take an oil bath before dawn.
- **Greetings** — people wish one another "Puthaandu Vaazhthukkal" (Happy New Year).

## Listening to the Panchangam

On New Year's Day many listen to, or read, the **panchangam** (the annual
almanac-cum-astrological text). It contains notes on the important days and
auspicious times of the new Tamil year. In many families this is a regular part
of the start of the year.

## Mango-and-Neem-Flower Pachadi: Life's Mixture

The signature dish of the New Year feast is **mango-and-neem-flower pachadi**.
The sourness of raw mango, the sweetness of jaggery, the bitterness of neem
flower, and tamarind — these all blend in a single dish. The combining of sweet
and bitter is spoken of as a reminder to accept that the coming year will hold a
mix of joy and sorrow. Pongal (the sweet rice dish) and other sweets also
feature in the feast.

## Difference from Pongal and Deepavali

Confusing Tamil New Year with other Tamil festivals is a common mistake:

| Festival | Time | Nature |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tamil New Year | Chithirai 1 (~14 April) | Cultural-seasonal new year |
| Thai Pongal | Thai 1 (~14 January) | Harvest festival |
| Deepavali | Aippasi/Karthigai (Oct–Nov) | Festival of lights |

[Thai Pongal](/en/arts-culture/pongal) is an occasion to give thanks for the
harvest of the month of Thai; [Deepavali](/en/arts-culture/deepavali) is the
festival of lights. The new year is a day distinct from both.

## The Malaysian Context

In Malaysia, Tamil New Year is **not a federal public holiday**. Unlike
Deepavali (nationwide, almost everywhere) or [Thaipusam](/en/arts-culture/thaipusam)
(in some states), it is generally not gazetted as a holiday in any state. In
2018 the president of the Malaysia Hindu Sangam petitioned for it to be declared
a holiday; some parties argued instead that Thai Pongal should be made a
holiday. So it remains largely a day celebrated at home and in temples.

Even so, on the Chithirai day a festive air can be seen in Kuala Lumpur's Little
India (Brickfields), Pulau Pinang and other Tamil community areas, with temple
worship, community events and special markets. The Chetti (Chetti Melaka)
community of Melaka also celebrates the new year.

## Common Misconceptions

- **"Tamil New Year and Pongal are the same."** No — the new year is Chithirai 1;
  Pongal is the harvest festival that falls on Thai 1.
- **"It is a public holiday in Malaysia."** No — it is not a federal holiday.
- **"It is wholly a religious ritual."** It is primarily a cultural-seasonal new
  year; although worship takes place in many families, the way it is celebrated
  varies from home to home.
