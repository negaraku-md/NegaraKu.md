As the drumbeat tightens, the lion springs into the air, its front paws landing firmly on a steel pole no thicker than the rim of a bowl, the tail following at once — two performers leaping and posing among a row of poles set at varying heights, two or three metres off the ground, the crowd holding its breath, until the lion, now seemingly "alive", steadies itself on the highest pole of all and the applause breaks out. This is not the festive lion dance on the ground, but a sport with scores, champions and world rankings — high pole lion dance.

High pole lion dance is a highly demanding competitive form developed from the southern lion dance (southern awakening lion). It keeps the southern lion's head design, its gong-and-drum accompaniment and its expressive performance, but moves the stage from the ground onto a row of steel "plum blossom poles". In Malaysia, this is both a sport that has repeatedly won international titles and a festive performance commonly seen at weddings, business openings and Chinese New Year. This article only describes its equipment, movements, music and local development, and does not pass judgment.

## From the Ground to the High Poles: A Competitive Southern Lion

The traditional southern lion is mostly performed on the ground, on tables or on long benches, with 'cai qing' at its core — the lion works to pluck a head of lettuce (a homophone for 'generating wealth') hung up high, often with an ang pow attached, then "spits" the leaves out toward the host, symbolising the delivery of fortune to the door. Such performances centre on interaction with the audience and the host, and on the lion's expressions of "probing, startlement and joy".

From the 1980s and 1990s onward, troupes around Malaysia and Singapore pushed the lion dance toward a standardised competitive sport: fixed pole arrays, prescribed movement difficulty, a uniform scoring system, and routines to be completed within a time limit. Thus "high pole lion dance" took shape — no longer just door-to-door New Year greetings, but a contest of fitness, skill and choreography before judges and a scoreboard.

## Equipment: "Plum Blossom Poles" Made of a Row of Steel Poles

The stage of high pole lion dance is a set of poles known as "jong". They are set at varying heights into an array, and because the layout resembles plum blossom, they are called "plum blossom poles". The lion must walk, leap and turn between the poles, and complete various posing shapes on the pole tops.

Modern competition poles are mostly detachable steel posts, usually around 1 to 3 metres tall, with pole surfaces barely a hand's span wide, demanding a very high level of balance and nerve from the performers. Early troupes mostly used wooden poles, later gradually switching to more stable metal poles that are also easier to dismantle and transport — an improvement that let high-pole arrays be carried from place to place for competitions, and in effect also helped the sport go international. The exact heights, spacing and number of poles in an array are set by each competition's rules.

## The Lion's Head, the Lion's Tail and a Set of Gongs and Drums

A southern lion is performed by two people working together: the first lifts the lion's head with both hands, controlling the opening and closing of its eyes, ears and mouth to convey expression; the second bends over as the lion's tail, responsible for a great deal of the support, exertion and coordination. On the high poles, the coordination between the two is especially crucial — one leaps, the other catches the pole, and the slightest discrepancy can mean a miss. One of the most classic movements is for the lion's head and tail to "fly" onto the same pole at once and then steady themselves firmly.

The accompaniment consists of gongs, drums and cymbals, with the drum at its heart. The drummer does more than keep the beat: the speed, force and pauses of the drumbeat direct the performers' advance and retreat, rise and fall like a command, while also setting off the lion's emotional shifts as it "scouts the path, takes fright, rejoices and shows its might". The gongs and cymbals reinforce the layers of rhythm. A high pole performance is often a narrative completed jointly by the performers and the drummer.

## Kun Seng Keng in Muar and Malaysia's High-Pole Tradition

Any discussion of Malaysia's high pole lion dance cannot bypass the **Kun Seng Keng Lion & Dragon Dance Association** in Muar, Johor. This troupe was founded in 1988 in Muar, named after a nearby temple, and later became one of the most successful high-pole troupes in the world, accumulating dozens of world championship titles and long leading in both local and international arenas.

Kun Seng Keng is credited with driving the standardisation of high-pole equipment and competition specifications: it is reported that in the 1990s the troupe was among the first to adopt detachable metal poles, and took part in setting the dimensions of competition poles and the scoring rules, an influence that continues to this day. It also led a number of Malaysian troupes onto the world stage. For this reason, Malaysia is widely regarded as one of the global powerhouses of high pole lion dance; local troupes have repeatedly taken the titles at international competitions such as the **Genting World Lion Dance Championship** — an event hosted by Genting World that has long drawn top teams from China, Australia, Indonesia, the Philippines, Hong Kong and elsewhere to compete on the same stage. In 2007, high pole lion dance was listed as a National Heritage of Malaysia.

## A Competitive Sport, and a Festive Performance Too

High pole lion dance carries a dual identity in Malaysia. On one hand, it is a competitive sport through and through: there are national teams and clubs, youth training pipelines, judges and points, and the movements are continually standardised and raised in difficulty from one competition to the next. On the other hand, it also enters ordinary occasions of celebration — a new shop's opening, a company anniversary, a wedding and even Chinese New Year, when the host may invite a troupe to mount the poles and display its skill for the sake of liveliness and a good omen.

As with other communities' major festivals, the lion dance in Malaysia also often crosses ethnic lines: it appears in cross-ethnic celebrations and public performances, becoming a widely recognised piece of cultural scenery in the local multi-ethnic society. The scale, difficulty and choreography of a performance vary with the occasion, and what this article describes is the most common and most representative face of the sport.

## Common Misconceptions

**Confusing high pole lion dance with the dragon dance.** The lion dance has one or two people holding up a single lion; the dragon dance has a team each holding one segment, moving a long dragon together. The two often appear on the same occasion, but are different performances.

**Assuming all lion dances take place on high poles.** High pole lion dance is only one competitive form of the southern lion. More common still is the traditional lion dance on the ground or on tables and benches, centred on cai qing and door-to-door New Year greetings.

**Assuming high pole lion dance comes from ancient China and is the same as the northern lion.** High pole lion dance belongs to the southern lion, a competitive event that took shape around Malaysia and Singapore in the late 20th century on the foundation of the traditional awakening lion; it is a separate system from the "northern lion", which differs in both design and footwork.

**Assuming the lion's head is the most strenuous.** Much of the support, exertion and pole-catching on the high poles actually falls to the bent-over lion's tail; the lion's head and tail must coordinate closely, and neither can do without the other.
