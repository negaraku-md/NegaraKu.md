Lift up an old kampung house, take apart its joints, and reassemble it on a new site — largely without any fixed concrete foundation. That is the wisdom of traditional Malay architecture: a house that "reads" the tropical climate long before air conditioning existed.

## Why are Malay houses built on stilts?

The traditional Malay house is a **stilt house** — its floor raised high off the ground by a row of timber posts. This design solves several problems at once in a hot and humid environment.

According to the science behind the house, a raised floor "promotes the movement of air" beneath the house, drying out damp soil and protecting the occupants during seasonal floods. Long window openings — from near floor level almost to the ceiling — allow cross-ventilation at body height, while a tiered, pitched roof traps and expels heat through the stack effect. Materials such as nipah thatch, meanwhile, have a low thermal capacity, so they cool quickly after sunset.

The traditional Terengganu house, for instance, is "built high off the ground to avoid moisture, floods and animal intrusion", and is constructed from cengal timber, renowned for its durability.

## How are the interior spaces of a Malay house arranged?

The Malay house is not a single space but a chain of connected functional spaces — usually running from front to back:

- **Anjung / serambi** — the front veranda where guests are received and conversations are held.
- **Rumah ibu** — the core of the house; the largest space and highest in standing, where sleeping and family affairs take place.
- **Selang** — the connecting passage between the core house and the kitchen.
- **Dapur** — usually at the rear for privacy.
- **Loft and under-floor space** — storage above and a multipurpose area below the floor.

The Negeri Sembilan nobleman's house, now turned into a museum at UPM, for example, is divided into the anjung, serambi, rumah ibu, loft and kitchen — complete with tiered *tebar layar* (gable-end) decoration at the peak of the roof.

## What variations are there by region?

The basic form is similar, but each state developed its own distinctive style.

| Style | Features | Region |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bumbung panjang (long roof) | Steep gable roof with tiered *tebar layar* for ventilation | Melaka (Rumah Serambi), Terengganu |
| Curved gable | Roof ends curve slightly upward | Negeri Sembilan |
| Rumah Kutai | Perak's oldest still-inhabited Malay house; "kutai" means old or ancient | Perak |

## How does the Malay house differ from the Borneo longhouse?

Both are timber stilt houses adapted to the tropical climate, but their social logic is the opposite. The Malay house is a **single-family** dwelling. The Sarawak longhouse (*rumah panjang*), by contrast, is **one structure for many families** — from as small as four doors to, historically, hundreds of families under a single roof.

Inside, each family owns its own room (*bilek*) for sleeping and cooking, but they all share the **ruai**, a communal gallery that stretches the length of the house for conversation and gatherings. The *tempuan* connects the room to the ruai, while the *tanju* is an open platform for drying pepper or rice. It is built from belian (ironwood), bamboo, nibung and apung. In short: the Malay house arranges space for a single household, while the longhouse arranges an entire village under one roof.

## What's next

If you want to see these houses first-hand, heritage museums such as the traditional house at UPM and cultural villages in Sarawak offer well-preserved examples. For readers who want to go deeper, explore the terminology of construction techniques (pasak, tanggam, tiang seri) and compare roof styles across states to understand how a single climatic principle produced so many faces of architecture.
