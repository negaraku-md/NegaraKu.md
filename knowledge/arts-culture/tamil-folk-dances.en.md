On a temple festival day, as the beat of the thavil and the sound of the nadaswaram
fill the street, a person comes dancing, bearing on their head a karagam decorated
with flowers, without letting it tilt in the slightest. Nearby, youths standing in
rows waving colourful handkerchiefs, little girls dressed as peacocks, a person
raising hoofbeats on stilts with a dummy horse tied at the waist — these are all
scenes of the Malaysian Tamil **folk dance** tradition.

These dances were born in the temple, harvest and worship traditions of Tamil Nadu
villages; brought to Malaysia by migrant Tamils, they continue vibrantly today at
festivals and cultural events. This article describes those forms neutrally — not
the stage **classical arts** of Bharatanatyam and Carnatic music (described
separately), nor the **temple instrumental** tradition such as the nadaswaram. What
is discussed here are **people's arts** born at the community level and widely danced.

## The Main Folk Dance Forms

Malaysian Tamils maintain many dance forms; among the widely known are:

| Dance | Brief description |
| --- | --- |
| **Karagattam** (Karagattam) | A balancing dance, bearing a decorated karagam (brass pot) on the head without tilting; linked with Mariamman worship |
| **Oyilattam** (Oyilattam) | An elegant group dance performed waving colourful handkerchiefs while standing in rows (oyil = beauty) |
| **Kavadi Aattam** (Kavadi Aattam) | A Murugan-worship dance based on the kavadi carried at Thaipusam |
| **Mayilattam** (Mayilattam) | A dance performed in peacock costume, dedicated to Lord Murugan |
| **Poikkal Kuthirai Aattam** (Poikkal Kuthirai) | Dancing on stilts raising hoofbeats, bearing a dummy horse figure at the waist |
| **Kummi / Kolattam** (Kummi / Kolattam) | A group dance performed to rhythm by clapping (kummi) or tapping small sticks (kolattam) |
| **Therukkoothu** (Therukkoothu) | A folk theatre-dance form performed in the street |

Alongside these are music-dance forms set to the beat of leather instruments such as
the **udukkai and parai**, and forms such as **thattukkaal aattam** (dancing on
stilts). Karagattam is often performed with **nadaswaram and thavil** music;
Oyilattam relies on thavil music and folk songs.

## Roots: Village and Temple

These dances are rooted in the **village culture** of Tamil Nadu. Karagattam, Kavadi
Aattam and Mayilattam arose in the contexts of temple festivals, Amman worship and
harvest celebrations. Karagattam is especially tied to **Mariamman** (the rain/shakti
goddess) worship — it is esteemed as a form of giving thanks for good rain and
fertility. Because Mariamman worship was deeply rooted in the estate communities of
Malaysia, these dance traditions too continued here through those communities.
[Mariamman worship](/en/arts-culture/mariamman-worship) explains the Mariamman
worship traditions in detail.

Those who brought these arts were largely South Indian Tamils who came for estate
work during the British era; [the Estate Tamil community](/en/arts-culture/estate-tamil-community)
explains more about their social history.

## How They Are Kept Alive in Malaysia

These dances are kept alive in Malaysia in many ways:

- **Temple festivals:** During festivals such as Thaipusam and Chithirai Pournami,
  Karagattam, Kavadi Aattam and the like are performed. See:
  [Thaipusam](/en/arts-culture/thaipusam).
- **Cultural festivals:** At events such as **Tamilar Thirunal**, held in states like
  Perak alongside the Tamil New Year, these otherwise rarely staged dances are
  gathered and performed on stage. On the Tamil New Year, see
  [Tamil New Year](/en/arts-culture/tamil-new-year).
- **Tamil-school performances:** At the annual functions of Tamil-medium schools and
  in **inter-school competitions**, children learn and perform these dances. See:
  [Tamil vernacular schools](/en/education/tamil-vernacular-schools).
- **Festive events:** Cultural troupes also perform at Deepavali events, open-house
  (rumah terbuka) celebrations, and community association functions. See:
  [Deepavali](/en/arts-culture/deepavali).
- **Troupes and exchange:** Local cultural troupes train in these dances; touring
  performances by artist troupes from Tamil Nadu also strengthen this tradition.

## Distinct from Classical Art and Temple Music

Folk dances should not be confused with Malaysia's other Tamil art traditions:

- **Not classical art:** Bharatanatyam, Carnatic music and the like are **classical
  arts** with taught rules, set in a stage format; these are described separately in
  the [Indian classical arts](/en/arts-culture/indian-classical-arts-in-malaysia)
  article. Folk dances, by contrast, are people's arts born at the village level.
- **Not the temple instrumental tradition:** The tradition of temple instruments such
  as the nadaswaram and thavil is explained in the
  [Temple music — nadaswaram](/en/arts-culture/temple-music-nadaswaram) article. These
  provide **background music** for these dances; but dance and the instrumental
  tradition are separate art forms.
- **Not a martial art:** Tamil martial arts such as silambam are different; see:
  [Silambam](/en/arts-culture/silambam).
