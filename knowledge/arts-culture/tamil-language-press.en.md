A Tamil newspaper alongside the morning coffee or tea at the corner shop — for a
generation of Malaysia's Tamil community, this was a familiar sight. Those pages
carried not only the day's news, but temple festival notices, Tamil school news,
wedding greetings, obituaries and Tamil film reviews — the everyday life of a
community, set in print.

The Tamil-language press in Malaysia has a history of more than a century. It is
interwoven with the Tamil community's history of migration, with the plantation
economy and with Tamil-language education. This article describes the sector's
history, major dailies, community role, wider media landscape and the changes of
today in a neutral way — neither praising nor disparaging any title.

## History: Tamil journalism in colonial Malaya

The roots of the Tamil press lie in British colonial Malaya, particularly in the
**Straits Settlements**. According to researchers, **Penang** was the birthplace
of many Tamil publications in the colonial era; from the late 19th century into
the early 20th century, Tamil literary and journalistic efforts grew there.

As an extension of this growth, **Tamil Nesan** appeared as a first issue on 24
September 1924. It brought the Tamil community a wide range of coverage —
politics, religion, national and world affairs, education and Tamil cinema — and
until it closed was regarded as the country's oldest Tamil daily.

At a time when Tamil workers lived largely in isolated plantation communities,
print media was an important bridge carrying the language and the news to the
community.

## Major Tamil dailies

Malaysia's Tamil newspapers began in various periods; some endured, some closed.
A summary of the main titles:

| Newspaper | Founded | Status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tamil Nesan | 1924 | Ceased on 1 February 2019 |
| Malaysia Nanban | 1 October 1986 | In operation |
| Makkal Osai | roots 1981; a daily from 2005 | In operation |
| Tamil Malar | — | Mentioned |

**Tamil Nesan** closed in 2019, citing long-running financial difficulties; it
had been under the family ownership of the former Malaysian Indian Congress (MIC)
leader Datuk Seri S. Samy Vellu.

**Malaysia Nanban** was launched in Kuala Lumpur on 1 October 1986; its name
means 'Malaysia's friend'.

**Makkal Osai** began in 1981 as Tamil Osai, stopped in 1990 owing to management
problems, then revived from the same roots and became a daily under the name
Makkal Osai in 2005. This title has a history of some controversies and temporary
suspensions (for example in 2007 and 2008).

## Its role in the community, Tamil education and identity

Tamil newspapers were not merely news organisations. For the estate
[Tamil community](/en/arts-culture/estate-tamil-community), they were:

- **A space for the language** — keeping Tamil writing and reading alive within
  the community every day.
- **A voice for Tamil education** — a platform for news about
  [Tamil-medium schools](/en/education/tamil-vernacular-schools), examination
  results and community demands.
- **Cultural identity** — sustaining community identity through festivals such as
  Deepavali and Thaipusam, temple events, and Tamil cinema and literary news.

In this way, print media was an important pillar of the continuity of
[Tamil in Malaysia](/en/arts-culture/tamil-in-malaysia).

## The broader Tamil broadcast media landscape

The print press did not operate alone; it was part of a broad Tamil media
landscape:

- **Minnal FM** — the primary Tamil radio run by the state broadcaster Radio
  Televisyen Malaysia (RTM); most programmes are in Tamil, with some songs in
  Malayalam, Hindi and Telugu. It is described as the world's first 24-hour
  Indian-language broadcasting service.
- **Astro Vaanavil** — a pay-TV channel aimed at the Indian community, launched
  in 1996; primarily Tamil, with programming that also includes Telugu and
  Malayalam.
- **Astro Vinmeen** — a sister channel of Vaanavil; introduced as a
  high-definition (HD) entertainment channel.

These deliver news, culture and entertainment in Tamil in a way that complements
one another.

## Declining circulation and the shift toward digital

Malaysia's Tamil newspapers face the same challenge that print media meets around
the world. As readers — especially the young — have begun accessing news through
the internet, mobile apps and social media, the circulation of print copies has
been falling. Declining advertising revenue and the costs of printing and
distribution add further to this pressure.

In response, Tamil media are moving toward digital platforms such as **online
editions, news portals, YouTube, Facebook and WhatsApp news channels**. Titles
that continue in print are also trying to reach young readers through online
video and social media pages. This is the same trend seen across Malaysia's other
language media, including the
[Chinese-language press](/en/arts-culture/chinese-language-press).

## Common misconceptions

- **The Tamil press has vanished entirely.** No — although Tamil Nesan has
  closed, dailies such as Malaysia Nanban and Makkal Osai continue to operate
  both in print and online.
- **Tamil media means only newspapers.** Together with the print press, Minnal FM
  radio, the Astro Vaanavil and Vinmeen television channels and online platforms
  together make up the full Tamil media landscape.
- **All titles operate under the same ownership.** Ownership differs; some titles
  have had party or political ties in their history, while some are under private
  company ownership.
