On the evening of the fifteenth day of the eighth lunar month, the atrium of the shopping mall is hung with glowing red lanterns, and in the park of the residential area children wander about in twos and threes carrying lit lanterns. On the dining table at home, a box of mooncakes cut into small pieces sits beside a pot of hot tea, with a peeled pomelo next to it. The family carries chairs out of the house and looks up at the roundest moon of the year — this is a typical Mid-Autumn night in a Malaysian Chinese household.

The Mid-Autumn Festival (also called the Mooncake Festival or the Lantern Festival) is a traditional festival celebrated by Malaysia's Chinese community on the fifteenth day of the eighth lunar month. The Chinese make up about a fifth of the country's citizen population (DOSM data puts it at 22.2% in 2025). Unlike Chinese New Year, the Mid-Autumn Festival is not a public holiday in Malaysia, and businesses and offices operate as usual, but this does not stop it from being a festival familiar to all and lively year after year. It is, first and foremost, a seasonal and cultural festival based on the lunar calendar — this article only describes its customs and how they are presented locally.

## Mooncakes: The Taste of the Festival and the Gift-Giving Season

The mooncake is the most emblematic food of the Mid-Autumn Festival. The most common is the Cantonese mooncake: filled with lotus-seed or red-bean paste, usually with one or more salted egg yolks in the centre, symbolising a full moon. Cut open, the round yolk sits right against the round body of the cake — a classic image on the festive table.

In Malaysia, mooncake flavours have long gone beyond the traditional. Besides the baked Cantonese mooncake, the **snow-skin mooncake** — a kind of mooncake whose skin is not baked and which must be eaten chilled — is especially popular in Malaysia and around Singapore; localised flavours such as durian, white coffee, chocolate and green tea also keep appearing.

About one to two months before the Mid-Autumn Festival, the major shopping malls set up mooncake stalls, entering what is called the 'mooncake season'. During this period, mooncakes are not just a snack to enjoy at home but a favoured gift for companies to present to clients and for relatives and friends to give one another, with box after box of beautifully packaged mooncakes changing hands frequently before the festival.

## Chang'e Flying to the Moon: A Legend About the Moon

Moon-gazing at Mid-Autumn is never complete without that story about the moon. The most widely told is **Chang'e flying to the moon**: it is said that the master archer Hou Yi obtained the elixir of immortality, and his wife Chang'e, in particular circumstances, swallowed the elixir, whereupon her body grew light and she floated up, finally dwelling on the moon, separated from the human world ever after.

The story has many versions with differing details, and is generally regarded as a folk legend explaining 'moon-gazing at Mid-Autumn and someone living on the moon', rather than historical fact. It adds to this festival, with its theme of the full moon and reunion, a poetic colouring of longing and separation.

## Lanterns: The Children's Mid-Autumn

If mooncakes are the taste of Mid-Autumn, then lanterns are its look. After nightfall, children carry lit lanterns — some traditional paper lanterns, some cellophane lanterns in all sorts of cartoon shapes — moving and parading between residential areas, parks and temples.

Shopping malls and temples are also hung with rows of lanterns as decoration. Precisely because lanterns are so visually striking, many Malaysians simply call the Mid-Autumn Festival the **'Lantern Festival'**. More than a few communities, clan associations and temples also hold 'lantern-carrying evenings', organising children to parade with lanterns together, making it one of the most emblematic sights of the local Mid-Autumn.

## Pomelos, Tea and a Table of Reunion

The heart of Mid-Autumn, in the end, lies in the word 'reunion'. On this day the moon is at its roundest and brightest, regarded as a time for the family to gather and share in togetherness. The family sits together, eating mooncakes, peeling pomelos and drinking tea while gazing at the moon.

The **pomelo** is the seasonal fruit of Mid-Autumn — large, thick-skinned and keeps well, ripening just in time for the season; the thick pomelo peel, once removed, is sometimes worn by children as a hat. Hot tea is used to cut the sweetness of the mooncake — the sweet mooncake with the clean tea is a widely acknowledged pairing. A family chatting idly under the moon is the plainest and most central image of this festival.

## Open Celebrations by Communities and Clan Associations

The Mid-Autumn Festival in Malaysia does not take place only within the family. Chinese **clan associations** (huiguan), temples and community organisations often hold open Mid-Autumn celebrations: lantern evenings, arts performances, mooncake charity sales, lantern riddles, dragon and lion dances, and more.

The Thean Hou Temple in Kuala Lumpur is one of the common venues for such celebrations, hung with large numbers of lanterns and holding lantern displays during the festival period; places such as George Town, Penang have community activities such as lantern processions. These open celebrations often also welcome residents of other ethnic groups to join in and watch, giving the Mid-Autumn Festival — like Malaysia's other major community festivals — a side that is shared across ethnic lines.

## Common Misconceptions

**Assuming the Mid-Autumn Festival is a Malaysian public holiday.** It is not. The Mid-Autumn Festival has no statutory holiday in Malaysia, and businesses operate as usual; this differs from Chinese New Year, whose first two days are a public holiday.

**Assuming the snow-skin mooncake is an ancient tradition.** The snow-skin mooncake is a mooncake that is not baked and is eaten chilled, popular in places such as Malaysia and Singapore, and is generally regarded as a relatively modern development, coexisting with the traditional baked Cantonese mooncake rather than existing since antiquity.

**Taking Chang'e flying to the moon as historical fact.** It is a folk legend explaining Mid-Autumn moon-gazing, with many versions and differing details, and is not a historical record.

**Assuming it is celebrated the same way nationwide.** From mooncake flavours to dialect names to community activities in various places (such as temple lantern displays and the George Town lantern procession), the practices of Chinese communities differ from place to place; what this article describes is the most widespread and common practice.
