Near almost any old rubber estate in Malaysia, and along the time-worn streets of the town centres, you can find a Mariamman temple. The Amman statue with its ochre-yellow face, and the festivals held before the rainy season, are not accidental — they carry more than a century and a half of a Tamil community's history and tradition of faith.

**Mariamman worship** is the worship of the mother goddess (Amman) rooted in the South Indian Tamil folk tradition. Traditionally associated with rain, protection and the curing of disease, this worship came to Malaysia through South Indian migrant labourers and spread especially deeply in the estate Tamil communities. This article describes the origins of this worship, its temples and its customs neutrally — it neither explains nor evaluates any belief.

## Mariamman — the Mother Goddess

Mariamman is regarded as the principal mother goddess of the South Indian Tamil tradition. The word **maari** means "rain" in Sangam Tamil; **amman** is a Dravidian word meaning "mother". She is therefore traditionally associated with rain, fertility and a good harvest.

She is also revered as a deity who cures disease — in earlier times she was worshipped as the deity who protected against epidemics such as smallpox and fever. The custom of carrying pots of turmeric-mixed water and neem leaves during the hot season is connected with this. In many villages she is regarded as the **guardian deity** (grama devata) who protects the settlement, and as a family deity (kuladeivam). In the Hindu tradition she is counted among the forms of **Shakti** (the Goddess).

## In the Estates and the Tamil Community

Mariamman worship in Malaysia today is largely a consequence of the **British colonial era**. In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, labourers who came from South India — mainly from Tamil Nadu — to work on the rubber estates and in the railways, ports and public works departments brought their folk tradition of worship with them.

In the isolated estate communities, Amman temples were not merely places of worship — they were also centres that preserved language, culture and social identity. Amman was revered as the guardian deity of the estate; Amman festivals were arranged around sowing, harvest and the rainy season. As a continuation of this history, small Mariamman temples can still be found on many old estate lands today. The broader history of Malaysia's estate Tamil community is described in [the estate Tamil community](/en/arts-culture/estate-tamil-community).

## Forms of Worship

Mariamman worship comprises a variety of forms of worship, which vary from temple to temple and community to community. Some of the commonly seen forms are given below:

| Form of worship | Brief description |
| --- | --- |
| Abhishekam | A sacred bathing of the deity with milk, tender-coconut water, turmeric, sacred ash and the like |
| Kavadi | Worship by carrying a decorated kavadi as a vow (most widespread at Thaipusam) |
| Theemithi | A firewalking vow carried out at certain temples — not everywhere |
| Chariot festival | A festival in which Amman is taken in procession through the streets on a decorated chariot |
| Amman festival | The annual Amman festival, including abhishekam, annadanam (free feeding) and more |

At these festivals, traditional musical instruments such as the **nadaswaram and thavil** take pride of place; [temple music: the nadaswaram](/en/arts-culture/temple-music-nadaswaram) describes them further. Note that practices such as **theemithi** take place only at certain Amman temples — no practice is common to all temples.

## Major Temples

There are several hundred Mariamman / Amman temples across Malaysia. Two temples of historical significance:

| Temple | Location | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sri Mahamariamman Temple | Kuala Lumpur | Founded in 1873; regarded as the oldest functioning Hindu temple in the country |
| Queen Street Sri Mahamariamman Temple | George Town, Penang | Built in 1833; Penang's oldest Hindu temple (a small shrine in 1801) |

The Kuala Lumpur Sri Mahamariamman Temple was founded in 1873 by K. Thamboosamy Pillai; at first it was the Pillai family's private temple, and it was opened to the public in the late 1920s. It was moved to its present site on Jalan Tun H. S. Lee (the former High Street) in 1885. It is this temple that, during Thaipusam, sends out the **silver chariot** — introduced in 1983 and made of about 350 kg of silver — to Batu Caves; it carries the images of Lord Murugan in procession through the city streets. For a detailed account of Thaipusam, see [Thaipusam](/en/arts-culture/thaipusam).

The Queen Street Sri Mahamariamman Temple in Penang was built in 1833 (a small shrine is said to have stood there in 1801). It is that state's oldest Hindu temple; since 1906 it has been managed by a committee appointed by the **Hindu Endowments Board Penang**.

## Connection to the Broader Hindu Tradition

Mariamman worship is not a separate religion — it is part of the broader Hindu religion, and in particular of the **Shakta (Shakti) tradition**. In the Hindu philosophical view, Amman is regarded as a form of the Goddess (Shakti); in diaspora communities she is often venerated alongside the forms **Kali and Durga**.

In Malaysian Hindu worship, Amman worship operates together with the worship of Shiva and Murugan; many temples have shrines for other deities alongside Amman. For the overall background of the Hindu religion in Malaysia, see [Hinduism in Malaysia](/en/arts-culture/hinduism-in-malaysia).

## Common Misconceptions

- **"Theemithi takes place at every Amman temple."** No — theemithi (firewalking) is a vow-based practice carried out only at certain temples; practices vary from temple to temple.
- **"Mariamman worship is a separate religion."** No — it is part of the broader Hindu religion, and in particular of the Shakti tradition of worship.
- **"Amman worship belongs only to Tamils."** Although it is very widespread in the Tamil community, Amman worship is part of the broader Hindu tradition; worship is not defined by ethnicity.
