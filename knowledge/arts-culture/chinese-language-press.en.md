In the morning kopitiam, an open Chinese-language newspaper often sits beside a cup of coffee. Readers flip past the front-page headlines from home and abroad and usually head straight for the pages they know best — some turn first to local news about which Chinese primary school has held a fundraising fair, some go to the literary supplement and opinion pages, and some are only after the obituaries, classified ads and the lottery results. For many Malaysian Chinese, the Chinese press is not just news but a daily habit of reading Chinese.

The Chinese-language press is one of the oldest modern institutions in the history of Malaysia's Chinese community. Written and edited in Chinese and aimed at Chinese-community readers, it existed well before the independence of Malaya, with origins that can be traced all the way back to the old papers of the Nanyang era. This article traces, in a descriptive and neutral way, the origins and present-day situation of several major Chinese dailies, and sets out the background to press regulation and digital transformation, without making political judgments about any institution or policy.

## Several Major Chinese-Language Dailies

The major Chinese dailies still distributed nationwide in Malaysia today fall broadly into two camps: the four old papers that come under **Media Chinese International Limited (MCIL)**, and Oriental Daily News, founded by Sarawak's KTS Group and standing outside that group.

| Paper | Founded | Brief description |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nanyang Siang Pau (南洋商报) | 1923 (Singapore) | Founded by the entrepreneur and philanthropist Tan Kah Kee (陈嘉庚); listed in 1989, regarded as one of the earliest Chinese dailies to be listed on the Malaysian exchange |
| Sin Chew Daily (星洲日报) | 1929 (Singapore) | Founded by the brothers Aw Boon Haw (胡文虎) and Aw Boon Par (胡文豹); long among the top-selling Chinese papers in the Peninsula |
| China Press (中国报) | 1946 | A popular daily known for its social and local news |
| Guang Ming Daily (光明日报) | 1987 | Took over the staff of the discontinued Sin Pin Jih Pao, with its base in Penang and northern Peninsular Malaysia |
| Oriental Daily News (东方日报) | 2003 | Founded by Sarawak's KTS Group, a major Chinese daily outside the MCIL group |

Beyond these papers, various regional and specialised Chinese papers and magazines still exist in different parts of the country, making up a wider Chinese-language publishing ecosystem.

## From Nanyang to Independence: The Beginnings of a Century of Press History

The two oldest roots of Malaysia's Chinese press both lie in Singapore — at that time Malaya and Singapore were both part of the British Straits Settlements, and the readership and distribution range of the papers spanned both territories.

**Nanyang Siang Pau** was founded by Tan Kah Kee in September 1923, with a strong commercial and overseas-Chinese flavour; **Sin Chew Daily** was founded in January 1929 by the brothers Aw Boon Haw and Aw Boon Par, famous for Tiger Balm. Both papers went through a suspension of publication during the Japanese Occupation (1942–1945), resumed after the war, and, with the separation of Malaya and Singapore, each developed editions and printing operations aimed at local Malaysian readers. Sin Chew Daily built plants around Kuala Lumpur from the 1950s and later placed its headquarters in Petaling Jaya, Selangor.

**China Press**, founded after the war (1946), took a popular approach, while **Guang Ming Daily** (1987) took over the staff of the discontinued old paper Sin Pin Jih Pao, with its base in Penang and the northern Peninsula. Together these papers formed the everyday information network of the Chinese community around the time of independence.

## Consolidation: The Formation of Media Chinese International

Entering the 21st century, the Chinese press underwent a notable round of ownership consolidation.

In 2001, Huaren Holdings, associated with the MCA, acquired Nanyang Press, a transaction that at the time triggered protests and boycotts from some Chinese-community groups, with the related controversy continuing for a period. After that, Nanyang Press and Sin Chew Media gradually moved toward being held by the same group.

In 2008, Malaysia's **Sin Chew Media**, **Nanyang Press** and Hong Kong's **Ming Pao Enterprise** merged to form **Media Chinese International (MCIL)**. MCIL was the first company to be listed simultaneously on the main boards of both the Malaysian and Hong Kong exchanges, bringing together Sin Chew Daily, China Press, Nanyang Siang Pau, Guang Ming Daily, and Hong Kong's Ming Pao, among other papers. This merger placed four Chinese dailies, each with its own history, under a single multinational listed group.

By contrast, **Oriental Daily News** was founded by Sarawak's KTS timber group. Its first issue appeared in September 2002 but was briefly suspended over a permit issue; the permit was restored in December of the same year, and the paper formally began regular publication on New Year's Day 2003. It has long remained outside the MCIL system, forming another force in the Chinese press.

## A Pillar of Chinese Education, Community and Culture

The significance of the Chinese-language press in Malaysia goes beyond delivering news.

Written and edited in Chinese and aimed at the Chinese community, the Chinese press is itself one of the most widespread domains in which written Chinese is used in daily life; it is also the main reporting platform for Chinese education (Chinese primary schools, Chinese independent high schools), Chinese community associations (clan associations, native-place associations, chambers of commerce) and all kinds of cultural activities. Much of the fundraising for Chinese schools, the bulletins of Chinese associations and news of cultural festivals is conveyed to the community through the Chinese press.

In addition, the major Chinese papers have long organised charity performances, education funds and other activities, raising considerable funds for Chinese education and disaster relief. It is for this reason that, in much Chinese-community discourse, the Chinese press is placed alongside Chinese education and Chinese community associations as one of the important pillars sustaining Chinese literacy and the transmission of Chinese culture.

## The Background to Press Regulation

Like all newspapers in Malaysia, the publication of the Chinese press is regulated by law. Under the **Printing Presses and Publications Act 1984**, the use of a printing press and the printing and publishing of newspapers all require a permit from the Ministry of Home Affairs.

Before the 2012 amendment, newspaper companies had to reapply to the Ministry of Home Affairs **each year** to renew their permit, an arrangement that historically prompted discussion about press freedom. The amending Act passed in 2012 (Act A1436) abolished the requirement to reapply each year, changing it so that a permit remains valid until it is revoked, and restored a measure of judicial review over related decisions.

Historically, the Chinese press has also been affected by this system: for example, during Operasi Lalang (茅草行动, Operation Lalang) in 1987, Sin Chew Daily's publishing permit was suspended for a time, and it only resumed publication months later. This article merely states these institutional backgrounds and factual sequences objectively, without passing judgment on the position of any party.

## Declining Circulation and Digital Transformation

In line with the trend facing the global newspaper industry, the print circulation of Malaysia's Chinese press has generally declined over the past two decades. Readers — especially the younger generation — increasingly get their information via mobile phones, social media and news websites, and advertising revenue has shifted accordingly from print to online.

In response to this change, the major Chinese papers have each developed their own **news websites, e-papers and mobile apps**, and spread content on social platforms; some reporting is presented in formats such as graphics and short videos. In this process, the Chinese press has on the one hand worked to hold on to its existing readers and credibility, and on the other hand felt its way toward a business model for the digital age. Whether and when print will disappear entirely remains uncertain, and this article makes no prediction.

## Common Misconceptions

**Thinking the Chinese press is purely a news medium.** In the Malaysian context, the Chinese press also carries the role of reporting on Chinese education, Chinese associations and cultural activities, and of raising funds for Chinese-community welfare, and is seen by many as one of the vehicles sustaining Chinese literacy and culture.

**Treating all the papers under the group as the same paper.** Although Sin Chew, China Press, Nanyang and Guang Ming all belong to MCIL, each has its own long history, positioning and readership; Oriental Daily News, meanwhile, belongs to another group and is not under MCIL.

**Assuming the Chinese press only appeared after independence.** Its origins can be traced to the pre-independence Nanyang era — Nanyang Siang Pau and Sin Chew Daily were founded in Singapore as early as the 1920s, and only later developed local Malaysian editions.

**Conflating the history and the present state of press regulation.** The 1984 Act once required annual renewal of the permit, but the 2012 amendment has abolished this requirement; in discussing the relevant system, one should distinguish between the situation before and after the amendment.
