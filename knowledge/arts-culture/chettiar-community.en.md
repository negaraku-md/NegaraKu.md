On the day of Thaipusam in Penang, a magnificent chariot made of silver sets off from Lebuh Penang Street toward the Murugan temple up on the hill. Neither that chariot nor the community that has run it for more than a century is incidental — they tell the story of a merchant-banking tradition that travelled from Chettinad to Malaya.

**Chettiar** — in full, **Nattukottai Nagarathar** — is a Tamil merchant community whose homeland is Chettinad in Tamil Nadu. Within Malaysia's various Tamil communities, this is a distinct group. This article describes their roots, history, cultural heritage and present-day situation in a neutral way.

## Who are the Nagarathar?

The homeland of the Nagarathar is **Chettinad** — a triangular region lying between the Sivaganga, Ramanathapuram and Pudukkottai districts of Tamil Nadu, with Karaikudi as its central town. Traditionally they were merchants, bankers and money-lenders. Having started out trading in salt, cotton and paddy, they later developed a sophisticated indigenous banking system.

The Nagarathar community is organised around nine traditional temples; which temple a family belongs to plays an important part in their social identity. Marriage customs too follow this temple-based structure.

## Merchant-banking history in Malaya

In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, as tin mining, rubber estates, trade and agriculture expanded in British Malaya, a large **demand for credit** arose. The principal community that met that demand was the Nagarathar. Beginning from a small town in Ramanathapuram, their trading network spread to Myanmar (Burma), Thailand, Indochina and the Malay States.

Their customers and partners were diverse — Chinese merchant magnates, colonial officials, lawyers, sultans, penghulus (village heads), ship captains, shopkeepers and farmers. Their lending methods included mortgages, pawn loans and insurance bonds; there was also a system of collecting wages and pensions through a power of attorney. The securities accepted expanded over time to include land, property, ships, lodging houses, wages and even bullock carts.

It is important to view this history in a balanced way. On the one hand, the Nagarathar financing system provided credit to segments the colonial banks ignored; on the other, these loans sometimes led to borrowers — particularly some Malay landowners — losing their land, as has been recorded by researchers.

## The kittangi system

At the heart of Nagarathar business was the room-office known as the **kittangi**. It was a warehouse-style shop-office; typically several lenders would stay in the one building and conduct business. Each had his own strongbox (cash chest) and a desk on the ground floor. Some ran their own business; others worked as salaried agents for firm-owners. These kittangis were not merely offices — they also served as hubs for exchanging information, trust and social ties.

## Chettinad cultural heritage

The Nagarathar turned the wealth that came from trade into distinctive cultural symbols in their homeland:

- **Mansions:** From the late 19th to the mid 20th century, thousands of large mansions were built in Chettinad. Built with materials such as Burmese teak, Italian marble and Belgian glass, they combine the Dravidian tradition with foreign influences.
- **Athangudi tiles:** Colourful floor tiles made by hand in the town of Athangudi using local soil and glass plates; they are one of the hallmarks of Chettinad mansions.
- **Cuisine:** Chettinad food is known for its blend of spices and deep flavour; serving on a banana leaf is a common tradition.
- **Jewellery:** Traditional gold jewellery also holds an important place in the community's heritage.

## Temples and philanthropy

**Temple-building and philanthropy** are an important trait in the Nagarathar tradition. It was their custom to raise a temple to Lord Murugan in the places where they settled. The clearest example of this in Malaysia is in Penang.

| Period / Year | Event |
| --- | --- |
| 19th century | Nagarathar arrive in British Malaya; money-lending business |
| 1854 | The Nattukottai Chettiar (Street) Temple is built in Penang city |
| 1894 | The silver chariot is made in Karaikudi and sent to Penang |
| 1915 | Penang's Murugan temple moves to its present Waterfall Hill site |

It was at the Murugan temple established by the Nagarathar in Penang that **Thaipusam was first celebrated in Malaysia**. The **silver chariot**, made in Karaikudi in 1894, has been maintained by this community for generations, and they run Penang's Thaipusam chariot procession; the celebration they hold on the eve of Thaipusam is called 'Chetti Pusam'. (The Thaipusam processions of places such as Batu Caves and Sungai Petani are run separately by their respective Murugan temples.)

## The community today

In the middle of the 20th century, monetary-law changes and the political-economic shifts in countries such as India and Myanmar shrank the traditional money-lending trade. Many Nagarathar families moved into business, the professions (such as law, medicine and accountancy) and education-related fields.

Today the Nagarathar community in Malaysia is relatively small in number; nevertheless it maintains a distinct cultural identity through temples, festivals, food and family-temple traditions.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Chettiar just means money-lender."** Although money-lending was an important occupation, they played a broad economic role as merchants and bankers; their financing system filled a credit demand the colonial banks ignored. At the same time, that some loans led to loss of land is part of history — it is right to view both sides in a balanced way.
- **"Chettiar = all Malaysian Tamils."** No. The Nagarathar are a distinct group within the Malaysian Tamil community; the majority of Malaysian Tamils are of a different lineage who came for estate work.
- **"Penang's silver chariot belongs to the government."** That chariot belongs to the Nagarathar community; it is this community that has run Penang's Thaipusam chariot procession for more than a century.
