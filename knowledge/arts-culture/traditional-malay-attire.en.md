Watch a man getting ready for Hari Raya or a wedding. The loose shirt is on, the trousers are tucked in — but he isn't finished yet. What remains are two things that decide whether he looks fully dressed or only halfway there: a length of samping cloth wrapped at the waist, and a songkok on his head. Without those two things, the baju Melayu isn't truly a baju Melayu yet.

That is the reality of traditional Malay attire — it is not merely a single garment, but a complete set with its own rules and etiquette. This guide breaks down the three core garments: the baju Melayu for men, and the baju kurung and kebaya for women, along with the samping, songkok and tudung that complete them.

## What is the difference between baju Melayu, baju kurung and kebaya?

All three are traditional Malay garments, but each has a different form, wearer and way of wearing. The easiest to tell apart is the baju Melayu (men) from the two women's garments — the baju kurung and kebaya — which are often confused with one another.

| Garment | Wearer | Basic form | Worn with |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Baju Melayu | Men | Loose long-sleeved shirt + trousers | Kain samping + songkok |
| Baju kurung | Women | Long, loose tunic covering the hips | Wrapped cloth (sarong) + tudung |
| Kebaya | Women | Front-opening blouse, more fitted to the body | Sarong + kerongsang/buttons |

The most obvious difference between the baju kurung and the kebaya is the cut. The baju kurung is loose and does not reveal the body's shape, while the kebaya opens at the front and is more fitted, fastened with a kerongsang (brooch) or buttons. UNESCO describes the kebaya as "a front-opening garment often adorned with fine embroidery and fastened with pins such as a kerongsang or buttons", and paired with a sarong.

## What makes a baju Melayu complete?

The baju Melayu is not a single garment, but four components that come together. The Department of Museums Malaysia describes the baju Melayu as the traditional attire of Malay men, consisting of a loose long-sleeved shirt, paired with long trousers or a cloth known as the samping, and usually completed with a songkok.

The four components are:

- **Baju** — a loose long-sleeved shirt, usually sewn with little shaping so that it is comfortable and loose.
- **Trousers** — loose long trousers. The official Johor royal website records that the wide trousers of the Teluk Belanga baju Melayu were in fact adapted from the "Chinese trousers" of Chinese workers of that era.
- **Kain samping** — a sarong cloth wrapped over the trousers, from the waist downwards.
- **Songkok** — the headwear that completes the outfit, especially for formal occasions.

### Cekak musang or Teluk Belanga?

The most important difference to understand about the baju Melayu is its collar style. There are two main styles, and their names are often heard but rarely understood.

| Collar style | Appearance | Context |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cekak musang | High standing collar, encircling the neck and buttoned | The official Johor royal website records that this high collar was once associated with royalty and dignitaries |
| Teluk Belanga | A lower round collar, with fine "tulang belut" stitching at the neck | The cut designated as the official dress of the Johor Malays |

The name "tulang belut" (eel bone) refers to the fine embroidery technique around the neckline of the Teluk Belanga shirt that resembles the bones of an eel. It is hand-stitching that marks the finishing of a carefully made Teluk Belanga garment.

Both styles are still worn today. The cekak musang tends to be seen as more formal and fully dressed because of its collar buttoned up to the neck, while the Teluk Belanga is seen as lighter and more relaxed, though equally suitable for formal occasions.

## Where does the Teluk Belanga cut come from?

The name "Teluk Belanga" is not merely a style — it is a place. The official Johor royal website records that the Teluk Belanga baju Melayu is associated with Sultan Abu Bakar, who ruled Johor from Teluk Belanga in Singapore. At that time Johor had long been a crossroads of trade, and this garment was born from the meeting of various influences — the dress of merchants and workers from India and China combining with Malay custom.

That is why its wide trousers originate from the trousers of Chinese workers, and its cut absorbs outside elements while remaining Malay. The Teluk Belanga garment is cut long, past the hips, with long sleeves.

One verifiable point of history: in 1982, Sultan Iskandar designated the Teluk Belanga cut as the official dress of the Johor Malays. This made the Teluk Belanga not merely a style, but the official dress of a state.

## What are the samping and songkok for?

For those unaccustomed to wearing the baju Melayu, these are the two components most often left out or worn incorrectly.

**The samping** is a length of sarong cloth wrapped over the trousers, from the waist downwards. It can be made of songket cloth woven with gold thread, or another patterned fabric. The official Johor royal website describes the fold of the samping as a "rolling wave" shape, with a length around the ankle. For more formal occasions such as weddings, a songket samping with gold thread is the usual choice because it gives a luxurious impression — which is why [songket](/arts-culture/songket) was once a marker of status.

**The songkok** is an oval-shaped headwear, usually made of velvet, felt or cotton, and typically black. For the Teluk Belanga baju Melayu of Johor, the official Johor royal website mentions a "black velvet songkok" as its pairing, along with black leather shoes. The songkok is a mark of full dress at formal occasions — wearing a baju Melayu without a songkok at a formal event is considered incomplete.

## Why is the baju kurung called the national dress?

The baju kurung is the most widely worn women's garment in Malaysia — from schoolgirls to civil servants and national formal occasions. The Department of Museums Malaysia describes the baju kurung as the traditional attire of the Malay community, recognised as the national dress of the country, and explains that its design "reflects the values of modesty, elegance and simplicity".

The cut of the baju kurung is indeed designed for comfort and modesty. The official Johor royal website describes the Teluk Belanga baju kurung as a garment cut long enough to cover the hips, with ample room to ease movement. Two stitching features that make it loose yet neat are:

- **Pesak** — cloth insets at the sides of the body that add width and lengthen the garment.
- **Kekek** — shaped cloth insets at the underside of the sleeve that give room for arm movement.

The women's Teluk Belanga baju kurung has a round neck that opens at the front, adorned with the same tulang belut stitching as the men's garment — making the men's and women's pairing uniform within a single family of style.

### The colours that were once forbidden

An interesting example of how dress is bound up with palace custom: the official Johor royal website records that before 2010, the colours blue, yellow and white were not permitted to be worn by commoners because they were royal colours. That prohibition was lifted by Sultan Ibrahim in 2010, giving women the freedom to choose their cloth. This reminds us that traditional attire is not static — its rules change according to royal decree and the times.

## Why is the kebaya listed by UNESCO?

Among these three garments, the kebaya is the only one to have received world heritage recognition. At the 19th session of the Intergovernmental Committee for the Safeguarding of the Intangible Cultural Heritage held in Asunción, Paraguay in December 2024, UNESCO inscribed the kebaya on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

What makes this inscription special is that it was a joint nomination by five countries: Brunei Darussalam, Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore and Thailand (reference 02090). UNESCO describes the kebaya as "a shared cultural element that transcends ethnicity, religion and borders, facilitating dialogue and uniting communities".

In terms of dress, UNESCO describes the kebaya as a front-opening garment often adorned with fine embroidery, fastened with a kerongsang or buttons, coming in various lengths, and paired with a sarong. Its making requires specialised skills — preparation, design, selection and cutting of the cloth, as well as various styles of stitching and embroidery. This knowledge is traditionally passed from mother to daughter, and is now also taught through schools and workshops.

The kebaya is worn in a variety of settings — from casual events to formal occasions, social gatherings and celebrations, and even in artistic performances such as dance, theatre and film.

## How do you choose the right attire for an occasion?

Choosing traditional attire depends on three questions: what occasion, how formal, and whether it is a royal or religious event. Here is a simple framework:

- **Formal or royal occasion (men)** — a full baju Melayu with a songket samping and songkok. The cekak musang gives the most formal impression.
- **Formal occasion (women)** — a baju kurung with a neat cloth, or a kebaya for a more elegant impression; the tudung is up to the wearer.
- **Hari Raya Aidilfitri** — the baju Melayu or baju kurung is the usual choice; families often coordinate their colours. See [Hari Raya Aidilfitri](/arts-culture/hari-raya-aidilfitri) for festive context.
- **Weddings** — a songket samping and a kebaya embroidered with gold thread give a special impression; couples often choose full songket.
- **School and office** — a simple baju kurung of cotton cloth is the most practical everyday wear.

If in doubt, the general principle is simple: the more formal the occasion, the more complete the set of attire (songket samping, songkok, embroidery), and the more relaxed the occasion, the simpler the cloth.

## Common mistakes

- **Wearing a baju Melayu without the samping or songkok at a formal occasion.** The baju Melayu is a set. At a formal event, the samping and songkok are not extra ornaments but part of the complete attire.
- **Confusing the baju kurung with the kebaya.** The baju kurung is loose and long; the kebaya opens at the front and is more fitted. The two are not different names for the same thing.
- **Thinking "Teluk Belanga" is just a type of cloth.** Teluk Belanga is a cutting style (especially the round collar with tulang belut embroidery), not a kind of fabric — and its name comes from the place in Singapore where Sultan Abu Bakar ruled.
- **Assuming the kebaya belongs to only one ethnic group or one country.** UNESCO listed it as the shared heritage of five countries, and describes it as an element that transcends ethnicity, religion and borders.
- **Thinking every samping must be songket.** Songket gives the most luxurious impression for formal occasions, but a samping can also be made of other patterned cloth for lighter wear.

## What next

Traditional Malay attire is most easily understood when seen as a system, not a collection of separate garments: the baju Melayu, baju kurung and kebaya, each with its cutting style (cekak musang or Teluk Belanga), completed with the samping, songkok and tudung according to the occasion.

To see these garments up close, the National Textile Museum on Jalan Sultan Hishamuddin, Kuala Lumpur — a heritage building completed in 1896 — displays textiles and the techniques of their making, including the Pohon Budi gallery that highlights [songket](/arts-culture/songket) and the Teluk Berantai gallery with its collection of gold-thread embroidery. If you want to delve deeper into the fabrics that form these garments, start with the [songket](/arts-culture/songket) and [batik](/arts-culture/batik) articles, the two textiles most often used as the material for the samping, kebaya and baju kurung.
