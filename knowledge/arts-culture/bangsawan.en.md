Before there were motion pictures, there were lit stages, hand-painted curtains, and an orchestra able to switch from the flowing melodies of a Malay court to a rhythm from the Middle East within the same night's performance. That was bangsawan — the stage opera that was once the liveliest urban entertainment on the Peninsula, and which later handed its actors and singers over to the newborn Malay film industry.

## Where did bangsawan come from?

Bangsawan did not grow out of the village, but out of the collision of a port city. Its origins lie in the theatre of the Parsi community — Indians of Parsi descent — known as **Wayang Parsi**, and this form was first staged in Penang in the 1870s.

Historical records note a more specific catalytic moment: in 1875, a Parsi theatre troupe from Bombay gave a performance to great acclaim in Penang, and it was from there that bangsawan began to take shape. In its early stages, these Wayang Parsi performances were staged in Hindi with actors brought in from India.

The great shift came when the form was taken over by local practitioners and performed in the **Malay language**. The Pushi (Indera) Bangsawan troupe of Penang is recorded as the first bangsawan troupe — or at least the first to use the term "bangsawan" — and became the pioneer of Malay dialogue on stage.

By the early 20th century, the form was established enough to be commented on in the press. A *Straits Times* report in 1903 described it as "an opera of Indian origin conducted in the Malay language."

## Why is it called "bangsawan"?

The word *bangsawan* in Malay means the nobility or people of rank. The name suits its content: many bangsawan stories revolve around kings, princes, princesses and palaces, filled with lavish costumes and grand settings.

But what made it "Malay" was not merely the language. Bangsawan absorbed and blended a variety of sources — stories, songs and styles — into a cosmopolitan form of urban entertainment. JKKN describes it as a **transitional theatre** that links traditional Malay theatre with modern Malaysian drama.

## What did a performance look like?

Unlike mak yong or wayang kulit, which are staged in open spaces or behind a screen, bangsawan used a **Western-style proscenium stage** with hand-painted backdrops. Common backdrops included curtains, street scenes, gardens, palace halls and forest views. These colourful painted backdrops also reflected a troupe's financial strength — the wealthier the troupe, the grander its backdrops.

Bangsawan acting was melodramatic and heavily improvised. The script was usually only a rough outline, allowing actors to adjust dialogue and scenes according to the audience's response. Battle scenes, meanwhile, incorporated silat movements.

A bangsawan performance typically followed a fixed sequence:

| Section | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| Gurindam | The story synopsis is delivered in poetic form before the acting begins |
| Opening song | Marks the start of the performance |
| Main performance | Drama, romance, silat action and comedy |
| Extra turn | An interlude during the break — comedy sketches, dance and singing |
| Closing song | A gesture of appreciation to the audience |

The **extra turn** was one of bangsawan's great attractions: an interlude space that allowed songs and dances from outside the main story, including foreign songs from the Americas, the Middle East and India.

## Music: one orchestra, two worlds

Bangsawan's accompaniment was provided by a live orchestra that combined traditional instruments with Western ones. Among the instruments used were the **piano, violin and tabla**. This combination allowed a troupe to move from a classical Malay song to a more modern or exotic rhythm within a single performance — something that made bangsawan feel "international" for its era.

## Stories and characters

The bangsawan repertoire was very broad because it borrowed from any source that would draw an audience. Its narratives were typically adapted from the *Sejarah Melayu* (Malay Annals), the two great Indian epics *Mahabharata* and *Ramayana*, Arab tales, Chinese classics, European stories, and even the plays of Shakespeare.

Its characters, meanwhile, were stock types — recurring figures from story to story:

- **Young hero** — the main warrior or prince
- **Leading lady** — the story's heroine
- **King and minister** — the powerful figures
- **Spirits, hermits and comedians** — the supporting characters

This fixed cast of characters made it easier for actors to improvise, because the audience already recognised the "role" of each character even though the plots differed.

## Troupes and theatres

At its peak, bangsawan was a serious entertainment business, with large troupes and dedicated theatres, especially in Penang and Singapore. Here are some of the recorded troupes and theatres:

| Troupe / theatre | Notes |
| --- | --- |
| Pushi (Indera) Bangsawan, Penang | The first bangsawan troupe; pioneer of Malay dialogue |
| Star Opera Company | Founded by Cheong Koon Seng in 1909 |
| Wayang Kassim (Indra Zanibar Royal Theatrical Company) | Among the leading troupes of the early 20th century |
| Nahar Opera | Around 90 actors and 28 musicians |
| Grand Nooran Opera | Founded by Pak Yem with a $5,000 loan from the Shaw brothers |
| Theatre Royal, North Bridge Road (Singapore) | Opened 1908; seated around 1,300 spectators |

Bangsawan troupes also crossed borders. Before the Second World War, bangsawan troupes toured as far as Sumatra and Java, and influenced the popular theatre there.

## Why did bangsawan decline?

Bangsawan's decline came from newer entertainment technologies. By the mid-20th century, competition from radio, film and television drew audiences out of the bangsawan halls.

That change could be seen in the buildings themselves. By the late 1930s, many bangsawan halls in Singapore were converted into cinemas. After the war, bangsawan lost almost all of its funding, and the industry — in its large-scale commercial form — all but vanished.

## How did bangsawan nourish Malay film?

The decline of bangsawan was not the end of its talent, but a transfer. When the opera industry collapsed, many of its practitioners were absorbed into film production studios. The Shaw brothers — Run Run and Run Me Shaw — who had originally employed bangsawan practitioners in their amusement parks, later used them in films.

This is bangsawan's most enduring legacy. Actors, singers, musicians and directors trained on the bangsawan stage carried their skills — melodramatic acting, singing, improvisation and performance style — straight into early Malay film. In that sense, the first generation of Malay film stars was really the last generation of bangsawan stage stars.

Bangsawan itself did not entirely disappear. It continued through the radio broadcast *Bangsawan di Udara* (from around 1958 to the 1980s) and various preservation efforts to this day.
