The festival is named after a single idea: the harvest. *Kaamatan* means exactly that in the Kadazandusun language, and everything else in the celebration — the ritual, the rice wine, the pageant crowned on the last evening — grows out of that one act of bringing in the rice.

For the Kadazan, Dusun, Murut, Rungus, Lundayeh and other indigenous communities of Sabah, Pesta Kaamatan is the year's great thanksgiving. It falls on 30 and 31 May, and on those two days it is a public holiday in the state of [Sabah](/en/states/sabah) and in the federal territory of Labuan.

## Three names for one festival

You will see the festival written three ways, and all three are correct:

| Name | Language |
| --- | --- |
| Tadau Kaamatan | Kadazandusun |
| Pesta Kaamatan | Malay |
| Harvest Festival | English |

The Sabah Tourism Board traces *Kaamatan* to the root meaning "harvest". It is not a festival that was invented for a calendar; the Sabah State Library describes it as rooted in the older belief-world of Sabah's indigenous peoples, and one of the surviving legacies of that way of life.

## The rice spirit, described as belief

At the centre of Kaamatan is a belief, and it should be read as belief rather than as a claim this article asserts as fact.

In Kadazandusun tradition, rice is not merely a crop. It carries a spirit — **Bambaazon**, also written **Bambarayon** — which the KDCA describes as responsible for the growth and well-being of the paddy and its protection from harm. Kaamatan is, at its core, the act of giving thanks to that spirit and to the Creator for a good harvest, and of renewing the relationship between the community and the rice it lives on.

The Sabah State Library puts the purpose plainly: the festival expresses gratitude for the harvest and reinforces unity, gratitude and respect for ancestral heritage.

## The legend of Huminodun

The rice spirit is tied to one of the most important legends of the Kadazandusun world, and again it is recorded here descriptively.

As the tradition is told, **Kinoingan**, the creator, faced a famine among his people. His daughter, **Huminodun**, was sacrificed so that food would grow, and from parts of her body came rice and other crops. In this telling she is the origin of the harvest itself, and her spirit is associated with Bambaazon.

That legend is why the festival's most visible modern event is not, despite appearances, an ordinary beauty contest.

## Unduk Ngadau: a pageant that is really a commemoration

The **Unduk Ngadau** is the cultural pageant held during Kaamatan, and its name repays a second look. The Sabah Tourism Board reads *unduk* as "to emerge and to spring out" — like a young plant pushing out of the soil — and *tadau* as the sun, giving "the emerging sun". A second reading, from the Dusun *runduk tadau*, is often given as "the woman crowned by the sunlight".

Whichever reading you take, the contest is held in honour of Huminodun. Each contestant is understood to represent her qualities — the loyalty, willingness and sacrifice of the legend — rather than appearance alone.

That framing shows up in the rules. The pageant has been staged since 1960 by the **Kadazan Dusun Cultural Association (KDCA)**, and it runs as a district-level qualifying structure that converges on a state-level final at Hongkod Koisaan in Penampang. Since 2012 the final has included a question-and-answer round conducted in an ancestral language, so command of language and cultural knowledge — not looks — decides the outcome. The KDCA also sets conduct standards, specifying, for example, that swimsuits and hot pants are not part of Unduk Ngadau attire.

## The shape of the celebration

Kaamatan is not a single day. The Sabah Tourism Board describes a month-long celebration: it opens at the start of May at a chosen launching district, other districts and the KDCA's coordinating committees — including those in Peninsular Malaysia and Sarawak — hold their own celebrations, and everything converges on the grand finale on 30 and 31 May.

Among its recurring elements:

- **Magavau** — a thanksgiving ritual to honour and call home the rice spirit, Bambaazon, traditionally led by a **bobohizan** (priestess, also spelled bobolian) reciting ritual chants.
- **Sumazau** — the signature dance of the Kadazandusun, performed to gong music.
- **Unduk Ngadau** — the cultural pageant described above.
- Food and drink of the harvest, including **tapai** (rice wine) and dishes such as **hinava**.

The state-level events are staged by the KDCA, whose hall — **Hongkod Koisaan**, on Penampang Road — becomes the centre of the celebration. The association itself was established in 1966, tracing its roots to the Society of Kadazan Penampang formed in 1953; it was formerly the Kadazan Cultural Association and took its present name in 1989.

## Common misunderstandings

**"Unduk Ngadau is just a beauty pageant."** It is a cultural pageant that commemorates the Huminodun legend, and since 2012 it has judged contestants partly on a question round in an ancestral language.

**"Kaamatan is only about Kadazan people."** It is celebrated by the Kadazan, Dusun, Murut, Rungus, Lundayeh and other indigenous communities of Sabah. The broader grouping is often written Kadazandusun, and Sabah's ethnic and linguistic diversity is wider still — see [Malaysia's population composition](/en/malaysia/population-composition).

**"It is a national public holiday."** It is a public holiday in Sabah and Labuan on 30 and 31 May, not a nationwide one, though Kaamatan celebrations are also held by Sabahan communities elsewhere in Malaysia.

**"The rice-spirit story is folklore Malaysia agrees on as history."** This article records the Huminodun legend and the Bambaazon belief as the tradition of the community that holds them, described respectfully — not as claims presented as established fact.
