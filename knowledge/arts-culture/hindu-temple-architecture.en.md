As you approach any part of a Malaysian town where a Tamil community has put down roots, a sculpture-covered tower reaching toward the sky catches the eye from a distance. That gateway tower, with its tiers of colourful deity figures, is no accident — it is the continuation of an architectural tradition a thousand years old; the mark of a cultural heritage that people of South Indian Tamil descent carried with them to Malaysia.

Most Hindu temples in Malaysia follow the **Dravidian** — that is, South Indian — architectural tradition. This article describes, in a neutral way and from a heritage and art perspective, the main elements of that architecture, the craft tradition that builds it, and Malaysia's notable examples. It does not explain or evaluate any belief of worship.

## What Dravidian Architecture Is

Dravidian architecture is the temple architectural tradition that developed in South India — especially in Tamil Nadu and neighbouring states — under lineages such as the Pallavas and Cholas. Unlike the curved **shikharas** of North Indian (Nagara) temples, the towers of Dravidian temples are built in a stepped, tapering pyramidal form.

A walled rectangular enclosure, tall sculpture-covered gateway towers and pillared halls are the hallmarks of this tradition. Because Malaysia's Hindu community is largely of South Indian Tamil descent, the temples here naturally reflect this tradition. (For the broader background of the Malaysian Hindu community, see [Hinduism in Malaysia](/en/arts-culture/hinduism-in-malaysia).)

## The Main Elements of a Temple

The layout of a traditional Dravidian temple can be understood through its elements:

| Element | Description |
| --- | --- |
| Gopuram | The tiered gateway tower, covered in deity sculptures, rising above the entrance to the temple's enclosure wall — the temple's clearest signature |
| Vimana | The tower-like structure above the sanctum; in the Dravidian tradition usually pyramidal in form |
| Garbhagriha | The inner sanctum housing the main deity; the central, most sacred place of the temple |
| Mandapam | Pillared halls; used for worshippers to gather and for festivals and arts performances |
| Dhvajasthambam | The flagstaff/pillar in front of the sanctum, often sheathed in metal |
| Sacred tank | A body of water found at some temples, used for rituals |

In later temples, especially in larger complexes, the entrance gopuram grew taller than the sanctum's vimana and became the temple's tallest, most striking feature. Some large temples have separate gopurams on all four sides.

## Craftsmen and the Agama Rules

Dravidian temples are not built arbitrarily; they are laid out according to the rules of the traditional texts known as the **Agama**. These texts prescribe the temple's dimensions, layout, the placement of the deities and the rituals.

Those who build them are the traditional craftsmen known as **shilpi** or **sthapati** — the skill of shaping deity sculptures and towers in stone, mortar and metal is customarily passed down from generation to generation. In Malaysia, the gopurams of many temples were carved by craftsmen brought over from Tamil Nadu.

When a temple is newly built, and after a major renovation, a consecration ceremony called **kumbhabhishekam** is performed. By Agama tradition, each temple is customarily given a reconsecration kumbhabhishekam roughly **once every twelve years** — at which the wear on the structure is assessed, repairs are made and the gopuram is renewed.

## Malaysia's Notable Temples

Malaysia has several fine examples of Dravidian architecture:

- **Sri Maha Mariamman Temple, Kuala Lumpur** — Malaysia's oldest continuously operating Hindu temple, founded in 1873 by K. Thamboosamy Pillai. In 1885 it was moved to its present location on Jalan Tun H.S. Lee. Its five-tiered gopuram was completed in 1972 and is carved with 228 sculptures of deities.
- **Batu Caves Murugan temple** — a temple set in a limestone cave at Gombak, Selangor. It was dedicated to Lord Murugan around 1891; Thaipusam has been held there since 1892. There are **272 steps** up to the cave temple; a golden Murugan statue roughly **42.7 metres** tall, unveiled in 2006, stands at the foot. (For details on Thaipusam, see [Thaipusam](/en/arts-culture/thaipusam).)
- **Arulmigu Balathandayuthapani Temple, Penang** — the "Waterfall Hilltop Temple" in George Town. Its seven-tiered gopuram (about 21.6 metres) is cited as the largest Murugan temple outside India; after Batu Caves, it is Malaysia's foremost Thaipusam centre.

Alongside these, cave temples such as the Kallumalai Murugan temple in the Perak (Ipoh) area, and the street temples of old cities such as Malacca and Penang, also reflect this tradition.

## From Estate Shrine to Gopuram

Not all Malaysian Hindu temples began as large gopuram temples. During the British period, Tamil labourers who settled on rubber estates and in mining areas often consecrated and worshipped Mariamman under a tree or at a simple shrine. These were plain, unadorned structures.

Over time, as the community grew in economic standing, many of these simple shrines took shape as stone-and-mortar structures, as full temples crowned with a gopuram. So a temple's architecture often reflects the history and economic journey of that community. Even today many estate temples face relocation and land-rights difficulties during urban redevelopment.

## Temples of Other Indian Traditions

Although most Malaysian Hindu temples belong to the South Indian Shaiva and Shakti traditions, they are not the only kind. The architectural details of Vaishnava temples dedicated to Vishnu, of temples of the Malayali tradition, and of temples of people of North Indian descent differ somewhat. The Sikh community's gurdwaras are also present in Malaysia; they follow a different architectural tradition. This diversity reflects the various regional roots of the Malaysian Indian community.

## Common Misconceptions

- **"The gopuram and the vimana are the same."** No — the vimana is above the sanctum; the gopuram is at the entrance. In later temples the entrance gopuram grew taller than the vimana.
- **"All Malaysian Hindu temples are Murugan/Mariamman temples."** Although the majority are, there are also Vaishnava temples and temples of the Malayali and North Indian traditions.
- **"Temples are built arbitrarily."** Traditional temples are laid out according to the Agama rules, by shilpi/sthapati craftsmen.
- **"Once a temple is built, it stays as it is forever."** By Agama tradition it is renewed through a kumbhabhishekam roughly once every 12 years.
