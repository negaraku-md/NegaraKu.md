On the seashore at Tanjung Tokong in Penang stands a temple of no great size, quiet for most of the year. But around the Lantern Festival things are wholly different: incense-pole baskets, gongs and drums, and long lines of people come all the way from George Town; at night every lamp and candle in the temple is put out, and a crowd gathers around a brazier, fanning the fire with held breath — how high the flames leap is taken as a "forecast" for the coming year. This temple is, in many people's minds, the fountainhead of Tua Pek Kong worship.

Tua Pek Kong is one of the most widespread deities among the Chinese communities of Malaysia and the wider Nanyang region. The three characters "大伯公" may be seen in old temples in the cities, small shrines in the countryside, and even a little niche by a shop's front door. He is often called a "god of fortune" (福神) presiding over the wealth and peace of a locality. This article only describes the origins, identity and associated customs of this deity, and the way he is presented locally, without passing judgment on any religious belief.

## Who Is Tua Pek Kong: A Name With Multiple Identities

Taken literally, "Tua Pek Kong" (大伯公) is a respectful term for a "tua pek" (大伯, a male elder of the grandfather generation). But exactly whom it refers to varies from community to community and temple to temple, and there are broadly two parallel understandings.

One is the thread of the **Earth God**. Hokkien, Teochew and Hakka speakers all habitually call the Earth God "Pek Kong" (伯公), so many people understand Tua Pek Kong as an honorific for **Fude Zhengshen** (福德正神, that is, the Earth God, 土地公), taking him to preside over the land of a locality and to bless its peace and livelihood. On this understanding, Tua Pek Kong shares a common source with the Earth God worship found throughout Chinese society.

The other is the thread of **deifying forebears**, with a distinctly local character: early Chinese pioneers who crossed the sea southward and did meritorious work opening up new settlements were, after death, revered as deities and honoured as Tua Pek Kong. On this understanding, Tua Pek Kong refers to a specific historical person, not merely the Earth God in a general sense.

For this reason Tua Pek Kong is both like and not quite identical to the Earth God. Scholars (such as the researcher Jack Chia) generally note that Tua Pek Kong is not a standardised deity in China's official pantheon, but is more like an approachable, dependable "local deity" closely bound up with the migrant and pioneering experience. Temples and communities are not uniform in how they identify him.

## A "Nanyang" Deity

The centre of gravity of Tua Pek Kong worship lies in Nanyang. Although calling the Earth God "Pek Kong" (伯公) comes from southern China, the practice of deifying pioneers as Tua Pek Kong, and of forming networks of temples and associations around this deity, developed and grew mainly within the Chinese migrant societies of Malaysia, Singapore, Indonesia and elsewhere, and does not hold the same central place in mainland China.

This has to do with the historical circumstances of the Nanyang Chinese. From the nineteenth to the early twentieth century, large numbers of Chinese came south with tin mining, plantations and trade, making a living by pioneering an unfamiliar land. A deity who could both guard the locality and bring wealth, and who also carried the sense of "those who arrived first blessing those who came after", naturally won the strong identification of migrant communities. Tua Pek Kong is therefore often seen as a form of belief developed by the Nanyang Chinese communities themselves. For the broader picture of the Chinese folk religion and Taoism intertwined with it, see [Chinese religion and Taoism in Malaysia](/en/arts-culture/chinese-religion-in-malaysia).

## The Fountainhead Temple: Penang's Hai Choo Soo Tua Pek Kong Temple

Among the many Tua Pek Kong temples, Penang's **Hai Choo Soo Tua Pek Kong Temple** (海珠屿大伯公庙, in Tanjong Tokong; Hai Choo Soo / Thai Pak Koong Temple) holds a special place, said to be the oldest and often regarded as the source from which Tua Pek Kong worship spread outward.

The Tua Pek Kong enshrined in the temple is traditionally linked to a Hakka man, **Zhang Li** (张理). He is said to have crossed the sea southward in the mid-eighteenth century with two companions, intending to go to Sumatra, but their boat was blown by the wind to Penang Island — about forty years before Francis Light founded the settlement. They settled there and taught the local people; after his death Zhang Li was revered by the community as a deity, honoured as Tua Pek Kong, and buried behind the temple together with his two sworn brothers. The temple is said to have been established with a stone tablet in the fourth year of the Jiaqing reign of the Qing dynasty (1799), making it one of the oldest Chinese temples in Penang and in the country.

Because Tua Pek Kong is tied to wealth, control of the temple was historically valued by different dialect groups; the Hai Choo Soo Tua Pek Kong Temple has long been jointly managed by **five Hakka clan associations**. For the close relationship between such temples and clan associations and dialect groups, see also [Chinese clan associations in Malaysia](/en/arts-culture/chinese-clan-associations).

## The Lantern Festival "Fire-Requesting": A Century-Old Fortune Forecast

The Hai Choo Soo Tua Pek Kong Temple is quiet most of the time; the most prominent event of its year is the **"fire-requesting"** celebration (**请火**, Chneah Hoay, "gazing at the fire" in Hokkien), held by **Poh Hock Seah** (宝福社) of Armenian Street (义兴街) in George Town and carried on for over a century.

The celebration runs for three days, from the 14th to the 16th of the first lunar month, with the formal fire-requesting taking place at **midnight of the Lantern Festival**. Members first welcome the Tua Pek Kong image, the incense-pole baskets and the hand-held censers into the temple; after nightfall every electric light, candle and oil lamp in the temple is extinguished, and committee members and members draw fire from a brazier in the incense-pole baskets and fan it three times in succession — divining the coming year's fortunes from the strength of the flames, the three rounds corresponding to the three seasons of the year. Traditionally the fire-requesting is open only to male members; once the rite is complete and the lights relit, the public may then enter to worship. Because of its divination of whether the coming year will "prosper or not", some locally call it, half in jest, Penang's own "economic forecast".

## Birthdays, Temples and Everyday Worship

Besides celebrations tied to a particular temple like the fire-requesting, Tua Pek Kong, as Fude Zhengshen (the Earth God), also has generally observed birthdays. The table below lists a few common dates and practices for reference only — the actual arrangements of each temple and community are not uniform.

| Date (lunar calendar) | Common practice |
| --- | --- |
| 2nd day, 2nd month | One of Tua Pek Kong's birthdays (the Earth God's birthday); devotees offer incense and offerings, praying for a smooth year |
| 15th day, 8th month | Another common Earth God birthday, on the same day as the Mid-Autumn Festival; some places combine the celebrations |
| 14th–16th day, 1st month | Penang's Hai Choo Soo "fire-requesting" celebration (Poh Hock Seah), with the formal fire-requesting at midnight of the Lantern Festival |
| New openings and festivals | Businesses set up a shrine inside or at the entrance and offer incense, praying for a thriving trade and safe comings and goings |

Tua Pek Kong temples are found across the towns and countryside of Malaysia, of varying size: from the Poh San Teng Temple (宝山亭) at the foot of Bukit China in Malacca, to the dozens of Tua Pek Kong temples across Sarawak (as in Kuching and Miri, and one in Sibu with a seven-storey pagoda), each with its own local history. Beyond the temples, small shrines to Tua Pek Kong are also commonly seen by roadsides, in Chinese cemeteries and in shops, woven into everyday life. On Chinese cemeteries and the worship of the Earth God ("Pek Kong") within them, see also [Chinese cemeteries in Malaysia](/en/arts-culture/chinese-cemeteries).

Tua Pek Kong is not the only case of deifying a local forebear. The **Sin Sze Si Ya Temple** (仙四师爷庙) in Kuala Lumpur was built by the founding Kapitan **Yap Ah Loy** (叶亚来), and the "Si Ya" it enshrines are likewise modelled on real historical figures — another example of the Nanyang Chinese tradition of "deifying forebears", of one spirit with the Hai Choo Soo Tua Pek Kong Temple.

## Common Misconceptions

**Simply equating Tua Pek Kong with the Earth God.** The two are closely related — "Pek Kong" (伯公) is precisely the Hokkien, Teochew and Hakka name for the Earth God — but in local practice Tua Pek Kong also has the thread of "deifying forebears", and some temples enshrine a specific historical person. The two are linked but not entirely the same.

**Assuming Tua Pek Kong worship was transplanted unchanged from mainland China.** Calling the Earth God "Pek Kong" (伯公) comes from southern China, but the deification of pioneers around Tua Pek Kong, and the networks of temples and associations, took shape mainly within Nanyang Chinese society and carry a distinctly local character.

**Assuming every Tua Pek Kong temple enshrines the same figure.** Different temples are not uniform in how they identify Tua Pek Kong: some mean Fude Zhengshen, others a specific founding forebear, and both the figure enshrined and the ritual practices will differ.

**Seeing Tua Pek Kong only as a "god of wealth".** He is indeed often regarded as a god of fortune presiding over wealth, but he also carries the role of guarding a locality and blessing it with peace, and is not a single-purpose wealth deity.
