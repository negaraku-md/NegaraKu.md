A blade serrated like a tongue of flame, forged from iron and nickel folded hundreds of times by the hand of an empu — the keris is not merely a weapon, but the pulse of Malay civilisation. It was tucked into the belt of the warrior, borne aloft in royal ceremony, and passed down through generations as a family heirloom. To this day, the keris remains a symbol of sovereignty, courage and the identity of a people.

This article takes you deep into the Malay keris from the angles of history, the art of forging, the anatomy of the blade, regional types, symbolism, and its legal and heritage status in Malaysia — including the matter of heritage shared with Indonesia.

## What is a keris, and why is it more than a weapon?

The keris is a double-edged thrusting weapon whose blade is asymmetrical — a feature that distinguishes it from any other knife or sword in the world. According to UNESCO, the keris functions simultaneously as a weapon, a spiritual object, an amulet (talisman), an inherited heirloom, a ceremonial accessory, and a marker of social status. It is worn by both men and women.

This is why the keris cannot be understood merely as a cutting tool. For Malay society, the keris carries a "presence" — it is an object steeped in myth and spirituality. An heirloom keris is believed to possess a personality, good fortune, and a bond with its owner. It is this belief that makes the art of keris forging a discipline demanding not only manual skill, but also pure intent.

## Where did the keris originate?

According to UNESCO, the oldest known keris dates to around the **10th century**, and most likely spread from the **island of Java** throughout Southeast Asia. Malaysian academic sources, meanwhile, place evidence of the keris's existence at **at least the 9th century CE**, supported by archaeological finds and old Javanese-Malay manuscripts. Both references show that the keris is a very ancient forging technology in this region.

From Java, the keris spread and took root across the Malay World — the Malay Peninsula, Sumatra, Borneo, Sulawesi, all the way to Mindanao in the southern Philippines. Each region then developed its own style, hilt form and pamor taste, so that today an expert can identify the origin of a keris simply by examining its "tangguh" — an estimation of its era and place of origin.

## How is a keris forged? The knowledge of an empu

The keris smith is called an **empu** (or blacksmith). By tradition, the empu is not merely a craftsman, but a **practical scholar** who understands the properties of metal, forging temperatures and the structural balance of the blade. According to UNESCO, an empu is expected to possess knowledge spanning literature, history and the occult, because the keris is believed to have both a physical and a spiritual presence.

The forging process begins by arranging **layers of iron and nickel** — and in some older keris, **meteoritic nickel** was also used. The metal is heated in embers, forged with a hammer, folded, and this process is repeated dozens to **hundreds of times** with great precision. A high-quality keris can take years to complete.

It is this combination of folded iron and nickel that produces the **pamor** — the beautiful lined pattern on the surface of the blade, which emerges when the blade is etched with acid. Pamor is not merely decorative; the combination of different metals also strengthens the structure of the blade.

One fascinating aspect of this tradition is the etiquette of forging. According to Malaysian academic references, a keris is not forged in a state of anger or haste; rather it demands patience, discipline and pure intent throughout the work. This philosophy illustrates that the keris is regarded as a "living" object, not a mass-produced item.

## What are the parts of a keris?

The keris is divided into three main parts: the **hulu** (hilt), the **bilah** (blade), and the **sarung** (sheath, also called the *warangka* — where the blade is kept). Each part in turn has finer sub-parts. The Malay Art Gallery records at least 27 anatomical features on the blade alone.

- **Hulu** — the hilt, usually of wood or ivory, sometimes silver or copper. Before the coming of Islam, many keris hilt carvings depicted images of animals such as birds or figures of deities; after Islam, the motifs shifted to more abstract and plant-based forms.
- **Bilah** — the blade. Its features are determined by the *dapor* (the overall shape — straight or curved), the *perabot* (the finer parts), and the *pamor* (the metal pattern).
- **Pesi / puting** — the tapered shaft at the base of the blade that is inserted into the hilt.
- **Ganja** — the part at the base of the blade resembling a "saddle" through which the pesi passes.
- **Sarung / warangka** — where the blade is sheathed so that it is protected from damage; the widened upper part is called the sampir.
- **Mendak / selut** — a decorative metal ring between the hilt and the blade, made of copper, silver, or a copper-gold alloy (suasa).

### Luk: why is the curve always odd?

The most iconic feature of the keris is the **luk** — the number of curves on the blade. In the Malay and Javanese keris traditions, **the number of luk is always odd**: 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13 and so on. Keris with a very high number of luk exist but are rare, and are often associated with decorative or "tourist" keris. Each number of luk is often associated with a particular philosophy and aspiration in traditional belief, although interpretations vary by region and school.

It must be stressed: a keris is not necessarily curved. A **straight**-bladed keris is equally valid and respected — indeed a large proportion of ceremonial keris are straight-bladed.

## What are the types of Malay keris?

Each region of the Malay World produced its own distinctive keris style. Among the commonly referenced types:

| Type | Main features |
| --- | --- |
| **Keris Bugis** | Rooted in the Bugis tradition of Sulawesi, brought to the Peninsula through the migration of Bugis communities. |
| **Keris Sundang** | A long, broad-bladed keris in a "thrusting-sword" style, popular in the Sulu-Borneo region and the Peninsula. |
| **Keris Panjang** | A blade longer than usual; often used as a ceremonial keris in royal functions and the highest customs. |
| **Keris Tajong** | A straight-bladed keris with no luk, featuring a distinctively carved hilt — associated with the Kelantan-Pattani tradition. |

These stylistic differences are not merely decorative — they reflect the history of migration, trade networks, and royal ties that connect this entire region.

## The keris in Malay custom and sovereignty

In the Malay monarchical system, the keris is **regalia** — an instrument of royal grandeur symbolising power, custom and the continuity of the throne. Each Malay Sultanate state has its own official ceremonial keris.

The most celebrated keris in Malay history is the **Keris Taming Sari**. According to accounts and folklore, this keris originally belonged to a Majapahit warrior named Taming Sari, and was obtained by Hang Tuah in a duel and bestowed upon him. Legend holds that this keris could fight of its own accord. After the fall of the Melaka Sultanate, this keris is said to have passed to Perak and become part of the royal regalia of the Perak Sultanate to this day. (The details of this legend — including the claim that it was made from 21 kinds of metal — stem from oral tradition and not official record, and should be treated as folklore.)

The keris has also become a broader cultural symbol: it appears on coats of arms, institutional emblems, and monuments — an example of how a heritage symbol can carry different meanings depending on context.

## Does the keris belong to Malaysia or Indonesia?

This is a sensitive question that arises often. The answer: the keris is a **shared Nusantara heritage**.

UNESCO inscribed the **"Indonesian Kris"** — first as a *Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity* in **2005**, then incorporated into the **Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity** in **2008**. At the same time, that same source acknowledges the keris is indigenous not only to Indonesia, but also to Malaysia, Thailand, Brunei and Singapore.

From time to time, public debate arises over "overlapping claims" to cultural elements shared by this region. Such debates are most reasonably understood through the lens of shared heritage, given that the keris tradition took shape long before modern national borders existed. A country's UNESCO registration does not negate keris practice in neighbouring countries.

## How is the keris protected in Malaysia?

In Malaysia, cultural heritage is protected under the **National Heritage Act 2005 (Act 645)**. According to the text of that Act, **Section 23** establishes the **National Heritage Register** (an official register of the nation's heritage items), while **Section 67** provides for the declaration of National Heritage together with the criteria for the Minister's consideration in subsection 67(2) — among them historical importance, design or aesthetic characteristics, social or cultural association, as well as the rarity or uniqueness of the heritage.

On **14 February 2009**, a total of **173 heritage items** were declared as National Heritage, including 10 artefacts belonging to the Department of Museums Malaysia registered under Section 23 of the Act. The declaration ceremony was officiated by the then Minister of Unity, Culture, Arts and Heritage. As of the most recent reference, a total of 20 artefact collections have been declared National Heritage Objects by the Department of Museums Malaysia, encompassing archaeological, ethnological and specimen collections — with the Malay weapons collection (including the keris) among those preserved and digitised for documentation.

Beyond legal protection, various museums and institutions hold keris collections for public viewing and research, including museums under the Department of Museums Malaysia that preserve Malay weapon collections such as the keris.

## Common misconceptions about the keris

- **"The keris is just a weapon."** Wrong — in Malay culture the keris is regalia, an heirloom, an amulet and a status marker, not merely a cutting tool.
- **"All keris are curved."** Wrong — a straight-bladed keris is valid and common, and indeed many ceremonial keris are straight-bladed.
- **"The number of luk can be even."** Wrong — in the tradition, luk is always odd.
- **"The keris belongs to one country alone."** Too simplistic — the keris is a Nusantara heritage shared by several regional countries.
- **"Pamor is merely decoration."** Inaccurate — pamor results from a layered forging technique that also strengthens the structure of the blade.
- **"The Taming Sari legend is historical fact."** Be careful — details such as the number of metals and supernatural abilities stem from oral tradition, not official record.

## A decision framework: appraising a keris

For collectors or students who wish to appraise a keris, these four basic lenses help:

1. **Tangguh** — estimation of era and origin: from which region and which era do this blade, hilt and pamor style originate?
2. **Dhapur** — the shape of the blade: straight or curved, and how many luk (check whether it is odd).
3. **Pamor** — the metal pattern: does it result from genuine layered iron-nickel forging, or merely a surface etch?
4. **Condition and authenticity** — the completeness of the hilt, sheath and mendak; signs of repair; and lineage (if an heirloom keris).

For a purchase, always obtain expert verification and be wary of mass-produced "tourist" keris — including blades with an unusually large number of luk made purely to attract the buyer's attention.
