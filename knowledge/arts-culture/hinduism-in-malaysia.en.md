In almost any Malaysian town you can find a Mariamman temple — at the edge of an
old rubber estate, or on a venerable street in the town centre. Those gopurams
and Deepavali lamps are no accident; they tell the story of a community more than
a century and a half old.

Hinduism is Malaysia's **fourth-largest religion**. But its story does not end
with numbers: it is interwoven with the migration history of the Tamil community,
with the plantation economy, and with the country's religion-and-law framework.
This article describes those facts neutrally — it neither explains nor evaluates
religious belief.

## Numbers and distribution

According to the 2020 Population and Housing Census, about **6.1%** of
Malaysians — roughly **1.97 million people** — follow Hinduism. The country's
religious breakdown is as follows: Islam 63.5%, Buddhism 18.7%, Christianity
9.1%, Hinduism 6.1%. In the 2010 census there were 1,777,694 Hindus (~6.27%);
although the proportion has dipped slightly, the absolute number has risen.

The states where Hindus make up the largest share mirror the old economic
geography:

| State | Hindu proportion (2020) |
| --- | --- |
| Negeri Sembilan | ~13.1% |
| Selangor | ~10.3% |
| Perak | ~9.7% |
| Penang | ~8.4% |
| Kuala Lumpur | ~8.2% |
| Johor | ~7.1% |

These areas correspond to the former rubber-estate and tin-mining belt — where
Indian workers settled in large numbers.

## History: how Hinduism came to Malaysia

Hindu-Buddhist influence existed on the Malay Peninsula for more than a thousand
years through early contact and trade. But today's Malaysian Hindu community is
largely a product of the **British colonial period**.

In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, large numbers of workers were brought
from South India — chiefly from Tamil Nadu — to work on rubber estates and in
the railway, port and public-works sectors. Most came under the **kangani
system**: a trusted worker would recruit relatives and friends from his own home
village. This concentrated migration from particular South Indian districts. In
1907 the colonial government regulated this inflow through the **Tamil
Immigration Fund Ordinance**.

These workers mostly lived in isolated estate communities. There, temples were
not merely places of worship — they also served as centres that preserved
language, culture and social identity. Many old estate temples still carry that
history today.

## Worship traditions

Malaysian Hinduism is strongly **South Indian** in character. The majority
follow **Saivism**; the worship of Shiva and Murugan is widespread. Amman or
**Shakti worship** (Mariamman temples) is also very prominent — in estate
communities Mariamman was revered as a protective deity. **Vaishnavism** (mostly
among those of North Indian descent) is a minority.

Temples are mostly built in the South Indian **Dravidian architectural style**,
with sculpture-rich gopurams. Well-known examples include the Sri Maha Mariamman
Temple in Kuala Lumpur, the Murugan temple at **Batu Caves** in Gombak,
Selangor, and the Arulmigu Balathandayuthapani (Waterfall Hill) Temple in Penang.

## Festivals

The principal festivals of Malaysian Hindus come from the South Indian tradition:

- **Deepavali** — the "festival of lights." It is a public holiday in every
  state and federal territory **except Sarawak**. For more, see
  [Deepavali in Malaysia](/en/arts-culture/deepavali).
- **Thaipusam** — a festival centred on Lord Murugan; the procession at Batu
  Caves is its largest gathering. It is **not** a holiday nationally — it is a
  public holiday only in the states that gazette it (e.g. Kuala Lumpur,
  Putrajaya, Selangor, Penang, Perak, Negeri Sembilan, Johor; Kedah grants it in
  some years as an ad-hoc declaration rather than by standing gazette). See
  [Thaipusam](/en/arts-culture/thaipusam).
- **Thai Pongal** — a harvest festival, at the start of the Tamil month of Thai.
- **Navaratri** — a nine-night festival of Devi worship.

Those of Malayali heritage also celebrate festivals such as Onam and Vishu.

## Organisations and temple administration

The **Malaysia Hindu Sangam (MHS)** was founded in 1965 (23 January) and serves
as the national umbrella body for Hindu temples and organisations; its work
includes safeguarding religious rights, registering temples, and priest training
(the gurukkal programme).

Temple administration varies from state to state. In some states (e.g. Selangor,
Negeri Sembilan, Perak) government funding is extended only to temples
**registered with the MHS**. Penang also has a statutory body, the **Penang
Hindu Endowments Board**. Many small, unregistered temples — especially those on
old estate land — face land-tenure difficulties, an issue that remains a subject
of ongoing public-policy debate.

## Legal standing

In Malaysia, **religious freedom** is guaranteed under **Article 11** of the
Federal Constitution: everyone has the right to profess and practise their
religion. At the same time, **Article 3** names Islam "the religion of the
Federation" — but this is no bar to the right to practise other religions in
peace. For a fuller explanation of how these two articles relate, see
[Article 3 and religion](/en/law/article-3-religion).

## Common misconceptions

- **"All Malaysian Hindus are Tamils."** Although the majority are of Tamil
  descent, people of Malayali, Telugu and North Indian descent — and members of
  other communities — also follow Hinduism.
- **"Thaipusam is a national holiday."** No — it is a holiday gazetted at state
  level; not in every state.
- **"Religious count = ethnic group."** Religion and ethnicity are not the same;
  the census measures the two separately.
