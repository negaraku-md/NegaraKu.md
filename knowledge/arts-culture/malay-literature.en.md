In an old manuscript is engraved the tale of the kings of Melaka, the journey of Hang Tuah, and the fall of an empire — not as a dry historical record, but as a story alive with characters, dialogue and literary art. That manuscript, the *Sejarah Melayu* or *Sulalatus Salatin*, is among the earliest works that attest to how long and how rich the tradition of Malay letters is.

Malay literature is not a single era, but a current flowing across centuries — from the hikayat and syair copied by hand in [Jawi script](/en/arts-culture/jawi-script), through the transition to modern forms, to the novels and poems of today. This article traces that journey descriptively: the classical era, the transitional figure, the reformist movement, the nation's highest award, and the institution that preserves it.

## Classical literature: the world of hikayat and syair

Classical Malay literature grew out of court and religious traditions, and was largely copied in [Jawi script](/en/arts-culture/jawi-script) before the arrival of Rumi script. According to Encyclopaedia Britannica, the *Sejarah Melayu* — composed around the 15th or 16th century — is one of the finest literary and historical works in the Malay language, a grand depiction of the Melaka Sultanate founded around the year 1400.

Alongside this historical work stands the *Hikayat Hang Tuah*, an epic work regarded as among the most important classical Malay texts. Unlike the *Sejarah Melayu*, which takes the form of traditional historiography, the *Hikayat Hang Tuah* elevates the tale of the Admiral of Melaka as an epic hero, complete with elements that scholars study as an adaptation of influences from various cultures. Its author and actual date cannot be ascertained precisely.

Two main forms formed the backbone of this era. The **hikayat** is a prose story — tales of kings, heroes, prophets and fables — while the **syair** is a long narrative poem bound by rhyme. Short poetry such as the [pantun](/en/arts-culture/pantun) also flourished as part of the oral and written tradition. Besides historical and epic works, this era also produced religious hikayat, framed stories, and old Malay legal codes that enshrine the procedures of court governance.

The literary historian Liaw Yock Fang, in *A History of Classical Malay Literature* published by ISEAS, arranges this era by genre — history, hikayat, poetry and law — and marks the layered Indian, Arab and Islamic influences in the classical Malay corpus. Many of these works initially lived as an oral tradition before being copied by hand and disseminated in Jawi manuscript form, making the copying process itself a part of the history of their texts.

## Transition: Munshi Abdullah and the birth of modern literature

The turning point from court letters to a more personal and realistic form is often associated with **Abdullah bin Abdul Kadir** (1796–1854), better known as **Munshi Abdullah**. Encyclopaedia Britannica records that he is widely recognised as the father of modern Malay literature.

That title rests on the way his writing broke with the old tradition. In the *Hikayat Abdullah* (published 1849) and the *Kisah Pelayaran Abdullah*, Munshi Abdullah wrote autobiographically, observed his contemporary society critically, and used techniques uncommon in earlier Malay works. He was also among the earliest local Malay writers whose works were published.

Yet this title is not without debate. Some scholars question the "father of modern literature" label, arguing that the division of literature into "traditional" and "modern" eras is partly influenced by a colonial framework, and Abdullah's pro-British views also become material for criticism. His position nonetheless remains important as a bridge between two eras.

## Angkatan Sasterawan '50 (ASAS 50)

After the Second World War, a group of young writers set in motion a new wave. The **Angkatan Sasterawan '50**, or **ASAS 50**, was founded in Singapore on 6 August 1950 and was among the earliest literary organisations in the region. (Some reports state that the process of its formation began as early as 1947.)

The movement's motto — **"Art for Society"** — placed literature as a tool of the struggle of society and language, not merely of beauty. This stance sparked the famous polemic against the "Art for Art's Sake" current, so that some members such as Hamzah Hussin left to form other groups. The core figures of ASAS 50 included the ideologue Asraf, the poet Usman Awang, Masuri S.N., and **Keris Mas**. The movement also championed Malay as a language of unity and was involved in the language congresses that shaped the policy of modern [Malay](/en/arts-culture/bahasa-melayu).

## The National Laureate Award and major figures

The nation's highest recognition for writers is the **National Literary Award**, which carries the title **National Laureate** (Sasterawan Negara). This award was first held in **1981**, and its selection is driven by the National Literary Award Panel with the support of Dewan Bahasa dan Pustaka.

Its first recipient was **Keris Mas** (Kamaludin Muhammad, 1922–1992), a core figure of ASAS 50. He was followed by a roll of great names: Shahnon Ahmad (1982), Usman Awang (1983), A. Samad Said (1985), Arena Wati (1987) and Muhammad Haji Salleh (1991), among others. Each recipient represents a major contribution to the modern Malay novel, short story, poem or drama.

## The role of Dewan Bahasa dan Pustaka

The institution that is the backbone of the building of the Malay language and literature is **Dewan Bahasa dan Pustaka (DBP)**. DBP was founded in 1956 — closely linked with the Malay language and letters congress of the time — and was upgraded to a statutory body in 1959.

DBP's mandate encompasses building and enriching the Malay language in all fields, publishing references such as the *Kamus Dewan*, coordinating terminology and spelling (including the spelling of [Jawi script](/en/arts-culture/jawi-script)), and advancing the book industry. DBP is also the secretariat for the selection of the National Laureate, making it the principal driver of the nation's literary policy.

## Malay literature today

Malay literature continues to live in various forms. Novels, short stories, poems and dramas are published continuously, taught in the school curriculum, and honoured through various literary prizes alongside the National Laureate Award. The list of National Laureates too continues to grow, with names such as Zurinah Hassan (2015), Siti Zainon Ismail (2019) and Rahman Shaari (2022) continuing that legacy.

At the same time, the classical forms are not extinguished: the pantun is still sung at official and customary ceremonies, old manuscripts are re-studied at universities and heritage institutions, and works such as the *Sejarah Melayu* remain a cultural reference and material of study. The works of the National Laureates, meanwhile, continue to be reprinted, translated and adapted to stage and screen, extending the reach of Malay literature to new audiences. From the hand-copied Jawi manuscript to digital publishing, Malay literature remains one of the clearest mirrors of the soul and history of the Malay people.
