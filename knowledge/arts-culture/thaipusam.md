Before dawn, a limestone hill on the northern edge of Kuala Lumpur fills with sound — drums, chanting, the shuffle of hundreds of thousands of bare feet on stone. At the base of a 272-step staircase stands a golden figure taller than a fourteen-storey building. The climb has begun, and it will not stop for the better part of a day.

This is Thaipusam at Batu Caves, the largest single gathering of the festival in Malaysia and one of the most visible expressions of Hindu observance in the country.

## What Thaipusam marks

Thaipusam is a Hindu festival observed by Malaysia's Tamil community. It honours **Lord Murugan** — also known as Subramaniam — and falls in the Tamil month of *Thai*, which lands around late January or February on the common calendar.

The observance is devotional. Many devotees prepare for it with a period of fasting and abstinence, and undertake the pilgrimage as the fulfilment of a vow. NegaraKu.md records how the festival is organised and where it happens; the meaning of the rites belongs to those who keep them.

## Batu Caves: the destination

The focal point in the Klang Valley is the temple inside **Batu Caves**, a limestone outcrop in the Gombak district of Selangor. According to Tourism Selangor, the golden statue of Lord Murugan at the foot of the staircase stands **42.7 metres (140 feet) tall** — described as the tallest statue of a Hindu deity in Malaysia.

Behind it rise the **272 steps** to the Temple Cave. On an ordinary day this is a tourist climb; during Thaipusam it becomes a slow, dense procession of devotees carrying offerings up to the shrine.

Tourism Selangor estimates that the caves draw **up to 1.5 million people** during Thaipusam. Attendance figures reported elsewhere vary widely, which is why only this official state-tourism figure is cited here.

## The silver chariot from Kuala Lumpur

The Batu Caves observance is preceded by a chariot procession that begins in the city centre.

A **silver chariot** carrying statues of Lord Murugan and his consorts sets out from the **Sri Maha Mariamman Temple** on Jalan Tun H.S. Lee — near Kuala Lumpur's Chinatown — and travels to the Sri Subramaniar temple at Batu Caves. Bernama and the New Straits Times record the route as roughly **15 kilometres**, moving slowly through the streets and taking the better part of a day to complete as crowds line the way.

The procession runs in the days ahead of Thaipusam itself. In 2026, for example, with Thaipusam falling on 1 February, the chariot set out from the city temple in the preceding days and reached Batu Caves ahead of the main day.

## Penang's own tradition

Penang holds one of the country's other major Thaipusam observances, organised independently of the Selangor event.

The Penang state government describes a chariot procession running a route of about **6.6 kilometres**, beginning in George Town's old quarter and ending at the **Arulmigu Balathandayuthapani Temple** — the Waterfall Hilltop Temple — in Jalan Kebun Bunga. The **Nattukkottai Chettiar Temple** on the same road is a second focal point. A golden chariot departs from the Sri Mahamariamman Temple on Lebuh Queen, Penang's oldest Hindu temple, while a silver chariot follows its own starting point.

The Penang state portal traces the local tradition to the South Indian diaspora that settled on the island, and notes that in 2017 the long-standing single-chariot practice was expanded to two chariots.

## The kavadi and other offerings

The most photographed element of Thaipusam is the **kavadi**.

In its fullest form a kavadi is a decorated frame — often adorned with flowers, peacock feathers and images of Lord Murugan — that a devotee carries on the shoulders up to the temple. The term also covers simpler offerings. Many devotees carry **paal kudam**, pots of milk balanced on the head or shoulders as an offering to the deity.

Some devotees fulfil their vows through **body piercing**, with skewers or hooks through the cheeks, tongue or skin. Devotees typically walk **barefoot**, and the procession moves to the beat of drums and devotional song.

These are described here as practices; their spiritual significance is a matter for the tradition and its adherents, not for this record.

## Where Thaipusam is a public holiday

Thaipusam is **not a nationwide federal public holiday**. Whether it is a day off depends on the state or federal territory, each of which gazettes its own list.

It is observed as a public holiday in jurisdictions with large Hindu communities — including the Federal Territories of **Kuala Lumpur** and **Putrajaya**, and the states of **Selangor, Penang, Perak, Negeri Sembilan, Johor** and **Kedah** — while several other states do not gazette it. For 2026, the Peninsular Malaysia Labour Department confirmed Thaipusam and Federal Territory Day both fell on **1 February 2026**, a Sunday, with replacement-holiday rules applying under the Employment Act 1955.

Because the list is set jurisdiction by jurisdiction and can shift year to year, the reliable step is to check the official holiday notice for the state concerned.

## Points people often get wrong

**"Thaipusam is only at Batu Caves."** Batu Caves is the largest gathering, but Penang runs a major independent observance, and Murugan temples in other states hold their own. The festival is not a single-site event.

**"It's a national holiday."** It is gazetted state by state. In states that do not observe it, it is an ordinary working day.

**"The chariot procession happens on Thaipusam day."** The Kuala Lumpur silver chariot sets out in the days *before* Thaipusam, arriving at Batu Caves ahead of the main day.

**"The 42.7-metre statue is inside the caves."** The golden Murugan statue stands *outside*, at the foot of the 272 steps; the shrine is in the cave at the top.
