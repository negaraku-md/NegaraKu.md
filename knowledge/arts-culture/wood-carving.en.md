Look closely at the wood panel in the window of an old Malay house, and try to find where the pattern begins. You won't find it. That is not your eyes' fault — it was designed that way.

That pattern is called awan larat, and its point of origin is deliberately hidden. Malay carving is like that: not merely decoration applied to a surface, but a visual language that follows its own rules of space, balance and meaning.

## The Pattern With No Beginning: Awan Larat

Awan larat is inspired by the movement of clouds blown by the wind — a series of motifs arranged neatly, adjoining and connected to one another. It usually begins from a single point deliberately made untraceable, which is why it is called *sorok punca* ("hidden origin"), then develops into curves resembling the numeral 8 or the letter S.

There are two main types. Melayu Asli awan larat (or awan larat jawi) has larger, looser leaf and flower shapes, while Javanese awan larat is smaller, with a tighter arrangement of leaves and flowers.

Its design is not free-form — it is subject to several principles: the carved space must be balanced against the empty space around it, and where there is a central motif, the secondary elements around it are repeated consistently to maintain the overall harmony of the panel.

## Five Motif Categories

Malay wood carving is not confined to awan larat alone. Its motifs fall into several broad categories:

| Category | Example elements |
| --- | --- |
| Floral | Tendrils, leaves, mangosteen calyx, lotus flower, taro leaf, breadfruit flower |
| Cosmic (awan larat) | Clouds, stars, sun, moon, hills |
| Fauna | Chicken, rhinoceros and duck forms |
| Jawi calligraphy | Quoted verses or Jawi script stylised into a pattern |
| Geometric | Repeating shapes based on basic lines and forms |

Floral is the most widely used category. The reason is practical: this element is the most readily found in everyday surroundings, the easiest to carve and visualise, does not conflict with Islamic principles, and adapts most easily to any carving space — from wall panels to furniture frames.

## From Palace to Kampung House

The Department of National Heritage (Jabatan Warisan Negara) traces the history of Malay wood carving as far back as the era of the Melaka Malay Sultanate, with the tradition most prominent in Kelantan, Terengganu, Melaka and Negeri Sembilan. The wood chosen is not just any wood — cengal, merbau and kempas are among the types commonly used because their durability can last hundreds of years, long enough to be handed down through generations.

Its applications are wide-ranging: palaces, houses, mosques, archways, musical instruments, furniture, and traditional transport such as boats, sampans, and ox carts and horse carriages.

Istana Kenangan in Bukit Chandan, Kuala Kangsar, Perak, gives a picture of how wood and ornamentation meet in palace architecture. Built in 1926 as a temporary residence for Sultan Iskandar Shah while Istana Iskandariah was under construction, the entire structure is made of hardwood without nails, with a diamond-patterned roof and ornamentation using tepas woven according to traditional Malay weaving patterns. Since 1986, the building has functioned as a Royal Museum under the administration of the Perak state government.

## Besut: The District Declared for Wood Carving

If there is one place in Malaysia that can be officially linked to wood carving, it is Besut, Terengganu. The district was declared a Wood Carving District on 12 August 1999 — official recognition of its heritage carvers.

That recognition was later given physical form through Desa Ukiran Kayu (DUK): construction began in early 2005, was completed in June 2007 and officially opened on 21 February 2008. It was built on the site of Istana Tengku Long, a palace built in 1875 in Kampung Raja Lama.

Perbadanan Kemajuan Kraftangan Malaysia (the Malaysian Handicraft Development Corporation) also supports the industry nationally through a network of craft complexes — including the Kuala Lumpur Craft Complex, designed in the Terengganu Malay architectural style, which functions as an education, marketing and exhibition hub for crafts, alongside publishing technical and motif reference materials such as *Seni Kraf Kayu: Motif & Teknik* (Wood Craft Art: Motifs & Techniques) through the National Craft Institute (Institut Kraf Negara).

## Common Mistakes

- **Assuming Malay wood carving means only awan larat.** Awan larat is just one of several motif categories — there are also floral, fauna, Jawi calligraphy and geometric categories, each with its own function and context of use.
- **Assuming the awan larat pattern is free of rules.** Its design is subject to strict principles governing the division of space between the carved section and the empty space.
- **Assuming Malay wood carving only exists on old buildings.** The Department of National Heritage also records its continued use on furniture and everyday objects, not merely as historical architectural heritage.
- **Treating "Wood Carving District" as merely a tourism brand.** It is in fact official 1999 recognition of Besut's carvers, whose expertise has a generational background, not a label created purely for marketing.

## What's Next

Wood carving rarely stands alone in Malay craft heritage — it often appears alongside other crafts that share the same regional hub, such as [songket](/en/arts-culture/songket) in Terengganu and Kelantan, or Jawi calligraphy, which is itself one of its own motif categories in [Jawi script](/en/arts-culture/jawi-script). For those interested in seeing metal craft that grew alongside Malay architecture, [Malaysian pewter](/en/arts-culture/pewter) offers an interesting comparison in terms of materials and technique.

To see this carving in its real context, the state profiles of [Terengganu](/en/states/terengganu) and [Kelantan](/en/states/kelantan) make a good starting point — the two states most often cited as hubs of this tradition.
