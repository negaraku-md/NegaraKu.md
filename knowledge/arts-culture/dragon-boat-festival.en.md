As the fifth day of the fifth lunar month draws near, the scent of bamboo leaves and glutinous rice drifts from the kitchens of Chinese families. Homemakers pack soaked glutinous rice and fragrant stir-fried fillings layer by layer into leaves folded into a funnel shape, tie them tight with reed string, and lower string after string into the pot for a long boil. Meanwhile, at the Teluk Bahang Dam on the northwestern tip of Penang Island, slender dragon boats slice across the water; a drummer stands at the bow beating out the rhythm, and dozens of paddlers stroke in unison to the beat — these are the two faces of the Dragon Boat Festival in Malaysia: on one side the fragrance of zongzi in the kitchen, on the other the race on the water.

The Dragon Boat Festival (the fifth day of the fifth lunar month) is one of the traditional Chinese festivals. In Malaysia it is not a public holiday, yet it lives on within the Chinese community in two forms — a culinary folk tradition and a competitive sport. This article only describes its customs and how they are presented locally, and does not pass judgment on any belief.

## A Festival Set by the Lunar Calendar

The Dragon Boat Festival falls on the fifth day of the fifth lunar month, so in Chinese it is also called the "Duanyang Festival" or the "Fifth-Month Festival"; the corresponding Gregorian date shifts each year with the lunar calendar, usually falling sometime in June. Because eating zongzi and racing dragon boats are the most striking customs, the festival's English name is taken directly from the dragon boat race — the Dragon Boat Festival.

In Malaysia, the Dragon Boat Festival is not a statutory holiday, and work and school generally continue as usual. Family celebrations therefore tend to centre on a single meal of zongzi on the day itself, while large public events such as dragon boat races are often held on weekends or nearby dates so that the public can take part.

## Zongzi: The Heart of the Festival

The most representative food of the Dragon Boat Festival is the zongzi — commonly called bak chang (meat dumpling) in the local Hokkien dialect. It wraps fillings in glutinous rice, folded and tied into a triangular or elongated shape with bamboo or other leaves, then boiled or steamed for a long time. In Malaysia, the flavour of zongzi often varies by ancestral origin rather than simply by region.

| Type | Common features |
| --- | --- |
| Savoury meat zongzi | Glutinous rice with pork belly, salted egg yolk, mushrooms, chestnuts and the like; savoury and rich, the most common type |
| Alkaline-water zongzi | Glutinous rice with added lye water, giving a translucent, chewy, slightly golden texture; usually plain or lightly filled, eaten dipped in sugar or honey |
| Nyonya zongzi | A Nanyang-style preparation using spices, coconut sugar and pandan leaves, with some of the glutinous rice dyed pale blue with butterfly-pea flower |

Of these, the **nyonya zongzi** best embodies local character: it comes from the culinary tradition of the Baba Nyonya (Peranakan Chinese), blending the Chinese zongzi with Malay spices, coconut sugar, pandan leaves and other elements, its colour and aroma carrying a strong Nanyang flavour. In the run-up to the festival, markets, kopitiams and pastry shops roll out all kinds of zongzi, and many families still keep the habit of wrapping them by hand.

## Qu Yuan: The Most Widespread Origin Story

The most widely circulated account of the festival's origin is the folk legend commemorating the poet **Qu Yuan**. As the story goes, Qu Yuan of the state of Chu during the Warring States period was loyal but slandered, and drowned himself in a river; the people rowed boats out onto the river to search for him, and threw rice food into the water so that fish and shrimp would not devour his body. Later generations came to say the rowing was the origin of dragon boat racing, and the rice food thrown into the river the origin of zongzi.

It should be noted that this is an account passed down as folk legend. Scholars generally hold that some of the festival's customs — such as warding off pestilence and evil at midsummer — may predate Qu Yuan's time; folk tradition also has other versions, such as one commemorating Wu Zixu. Whichever account one follows, eating zongzi and racing dragon boats have become the two most central activities of today's Dragon Boat Festival.

## Dragon Boats: From Folk Custom to Competitive Sport

In Malaysia, dragon boat racing long ago went beyond mere festival folklore to become a formal water sport. The largest in scale is the **Penang international dragon boat race**: held for years at Penang's Teluk Bahang Dam and supported by the Penang state government, it draws teams from many countries and regions to compete side by side. The event has been held for more than forty editions — the 45th in 2024 — confirming its standing as a regional dragon boat spectacle.

Besides Penang, **Putrajaya**, **Malacca** and other places have also hosted large dragon boat events. Notably, as a competitive sport, modern dragon boating is long since not limited to the Chinese: government agencies, companies, universities and colleges, and sports enthusiasts of every ethnic group form teams to compete, making dragon boating a cross-ethnic water sport. For this reason, locally the timing of dragon boat races is not necessarily tied closely to the fifth day of the fifth lunar month, but follows the organiser's schedule, sometimes held separately from the festival itself.

## Mugwort, Calamus and Standing Eggs

Besides eating zongzi and racing dragon boats, some families still keep a few small seasonal customs. Some hang a bundle of **mugwort** and **calamus** at the front door, for their meaning of warding off evil and repelling insects — these aromatic herbs are, in traditional thinking, connected with midsummer disease prevention and praying for well-being.

Another intriguing practice is **standing eggs upright**: it is said that at the moment of noon on the Dragon Boat Festival, when the yang energy is at its peak, it is easier to stand an egg on its end. Many families and schools try to stand eggs upright at midday that day, both a seasonal game and a source of fun for children. These customs are not practised by every household, and ways of doing them differ from family to family.

## Common Misconceptions

**Thinking the Dragon Boat Festival is a public holiday in Malaysia.** The Dragon Boat Festival is not a statutory holiday in Malaysia; work and school generally continue as usual, and celebrations are mostly concentrated on the day itself or a nearby weekend.

**Thinking there is only one kind of zongzi.** Malaysia's zongzi come in many kinds — savoury meat zongzi, alkaline-water zongzi and nyonya zongzi each have their own character, and the flavour often varies by ancestral origin (such as Cantonese, Hokkien, Hakka or Foochow).

**Thinking the nyonya zongzi is an ancient zongzi brought from China.** The nyonya zongzi is a preparation developed locally by the Baba Nyonya community, blending the Chinese zongzi with Malay spices, coconut sugar and pandan leaves — a Nanyang speciality, not a tradition originating in ancient China.

**Thinking dragon boat racing is only a Chinese religious or ritual activity.** In today's Malaysia, dragon boating is more a cross-ethnic competitive sport, with competing teams spanning every ethnic group and institution, which is not in conflict with its folk origin commemorating Qu Yuan.
