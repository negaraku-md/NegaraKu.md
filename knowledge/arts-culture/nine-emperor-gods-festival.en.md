On the eve of the first day of the ninth lunar month, a line of devotees dressed in white with yellow sashes at the waist files out of the temple, holding sticks of incense, and heads for the nearest river or seashore. Gongs and drums go in front, incense smoke behind — they are going to "invite the water" and "welcome the gods", to escort the Nine Emperor Gods back to the temple. For the next nine days the incense never stops inside the temple, and the aroma of vegetarian food drifts outside; on the night of the ninth day, the same procession will again form up at the water's edge to send the deities off.

The Nine Emperor Gods Festival is an important Taoist festival among Malaysia's Chinese community. It is held every year on the first nine days of the ninth lunar month, to the rhythm of inviting the water, vegetarian fasting and sending off the gods. This article describes only its deities and ritual customs, and does not pass judgment on any religious belief.

## Who Are the Nine Emperor Gods

The "Nine Emperor Gods" (Jiu Huang Da Di, known colloquially in Hokkien as Kaw Ong Ya) venerated by devotees are regarded as the **Nine Emperor Star Lords of the Northern Dipper**. According to Taoist tradition, they are said to be the nine sons of **Dou Mu Yuan Jun** — the Mother of the Dipper stars — and high-ranking star lords who govern people's lifespans, destinies and fortunes.

For this reason, many temples that enshrine the Nine Emperor Gods are named "**Dou Mu Gong**", with Dou Mu Yuan Jun venerated as the principal deity. The Nine Emperor Gods Festival belongs to a faith tradition where Chinese folk religion and Taoism are interwoven; for this broader context, see [Chinese religion and Taoism in Malaysia](/en/arts-culture/chinese-religion-in-malaysia).

## The Nine-Day Period: From Inviting the Water to Sending Off the Gods

The festival period traditionally runs for nine consecutive days, from the **1st to the 9th day of the ninth lunar month**. Because devotees believe the deities arrive by water and depart by water, the whole period is bracketed by two rituals at the water's edge.

| Point in the festival | Common ritual |
| --- | --- |
| Eve of the first day (last day of the eighth month) | "Inviting the water" / "welcoming the gods" — a procession to a river or the sea to invite the Nine Emperor Gods back to the temple to be enshrined |
| Days 1 to 8 | Scripture chanting, offering incense and praying in the temple; devotees keep the vegetarian fast; temple fairs and street processions get under way |
| Day 9 (the main feast day) | The peak of the festival; some temples hold firewalking, cheek-skewering and other rituals, with street processions |
| Night of Day 9 | "Sending off" — another procession to the water to see the deities off, closing the festival |

Temples differ somewhat in the details of the dates and rituals, but the opening-and-closing structure of "welcomed from the water, sent back to the water" is broadly shared.

## Vegetarian Fasting and Yellow Attire

The most widespread custom during the festival is **vegetarian fasting** ("shi zhai"). Devotees may choose to keep a meat-free diet for one day, three days or the full nine days, abstaining from all meat and seafood, regarded as a way of purifying body and mind and expressing devotion. Around the festival, large numbers of **vegetarian food stalls and meat-free food fairs** often appear near the temples, offering vegetarian rice, mock-meat dishes and all kinds of Chinese vegetarian snacks.

Traditional Nine Emperor Gods vegetarian cooking pays attention to avoiding the "**five pungents**" — garlic, onion, chives, green garlic and shallots and other strong-smelling vegetables; stricter devotees also use cookware that has never touched meat or seafood. However, whether dairy, the five pungents and separate cookware are avoided varies between temples and families.

As for dress, many participants **wear white clothing with a yellow sash**. Some temples require that only devotees who have kept the fast for a set number of days may join the sending-off procession or enter particular halls.

## Temples and Processions

The Nine Emperor Gods Festival flourishes in many parts of Malaysia, and several temples are known for their large scale:

- **Nan Tian Gong in Ampang, Selangor** is a long-established and busy Nine Emperor Gods temple in the central region; its annual water-inviting ritual and temple fair draw large crowds of devotees.
- **Penang** is one of the areas where the festival atmosphere is strongest: the **Tow Boo Keong (Dou Mu Gong) on Hong Kong Street** in George Town (around Cheong Fatt Tze Road) and the Dou Mu Gong in Butterworth both hold large celebrations and vegetarian food offerings during the festival.
- **Ipoh and Taiping in Perak** are also important centres of worship.

**Street processions** are common during the festival: devotees carry the deities' palanquins, raise flags and beat drums as they parade through streets and residential areas, with households along the route setting out incense tables to receive them. Such processions are both a faith activity and often a draw for residents of different ethnic backgrounds who stop to watch.

## Firewalking and Cheek-Skewering: Austerities That Vary by Temple

At the festival in some temples, one may see austerities such as **firewalking** (walking barefoot across burning charcoal) and **cheek-skewering** (piercing the cheeks with steel skewers or rods), usually performed by **spirit mediums** (tang-ki, believed to be possessed by the deities) and regarded by devotees as a manifestation of divine power.

It must be stressed that these austerities are **not held at every temple**, and their form and scale also differ from temple to temple. Some temples focus on scripture chanting, processions and fasting, and do not hold firewalking or cheek-skewering. There is no single nationwide fixed form for the rituals of the Nine Emperor Gods Festival in Malaysia — and that is precisely the key to understanding this festival.

## Common Misconceptions

**Taking the Nine Emperor Gods Festival for Chinese New Year or the Hungry Ghost Festival.** The three differ in timing, object and meaning: the Nine Emperor Gods Festival is devoted specifically to the Nine Emperor Gods in the ninth lunar month; [the Hungry Ghost Festival (Yulan)](/en/arts-culture/hungry-ghost-festival) falls in the seventh month, centred on the salvation of wandering souls; [Chinese New Year](/en/arts-culture/chinese-new-year) is a seasonal festival centred on family reunion.

**Assuming every Nine Emperor Gods temple has firewalking and cheek-skewering.** These austerities are seen only at some temples, in varying forms and scales; many temples do not hold them.

**Treating 'shi zhai' as simply eating vegetarian.** Traditional Nine Emperor Gods vegetarian practice pays attention to avoiding the five pungents, and stricter observers keep separate cookware; but practices regarding dairy, the five pungents and cookware are not uniform across temples and families.

**Lumping the Nine Emperor Gods Festival in with Buddhism.** It is a faith festival where Chinese folk religion and Taoism are interwoven, a different context from [Buddhism](/en/arts-culture/buddhism-in-malaysia), even though the two often overlap in census statistics and everyday practice.
