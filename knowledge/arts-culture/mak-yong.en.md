The first sound doesn't come from a performer. It comes from a pear-shaped piece of wood with three strings — a rebab — bowed slowly at the edge of an open wooden stage. The audience sits on three sides. The roof is palm thatch. And before a single line of dialogue is spoken, a princess has already bowed toward the instrument in a dance whose name alone explains everything: **Mengadap Rebab**.

That is the opening of Mak Yong, a form of traditional Malay dance theatre centred in Kelantan and now listed on UNESCO's intangible cultural heritage register.

## Not dance, not drama — both at once

The most common mistake about Mak Yong is trying to fit it into a single box. UNESCO describes it as an ancient theatre tradition combining acting, vocal and instrumental music, gesture and elaborate costume. JKKN's cultural mapping records list six elements at once: dance, music, drama, singing, ritual and comedy.

All of this happens in the same performance, sometimes in the same scene. A character may sing, then dance, then switch to spontaneous comic dialogue with a clown — without a fixed script, because most of the monologue and dialogue is improvised over the frame of a story that is already known.

According to UNESCO, a single story can be staged over several consecutive nights, with each night's performance running about three hours. It is built for an audience with time to spare, not for a two-minute slot.

## The order of a performance

A Mak Yong performance does not begin straight into the story. JKKN's records set out a fixed opening sequence:

| Stage | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| Bertabuh | Instruments are struck to call and prepare the audience |
| San Gendang | The performers enter the stage |
| Lagu Mengadap Rebab | Dance and song facing the rebab player |
| Subsequent songs | Transition toward the narrative opening |
| Cerita (the story) | The main scenes begin |

This sequence explains the rebab's place in Mak Yong. The instrument is not mere accompaniment; it is the reference point the performers face before the story is allowed to begin. In JKKN's records, the rebab player is the head of the troupe, known as **Tok Minduk**.

## A small orchestra, a sound hard to mistake

UNESCO lists three core instruments:

- **Rebab** — a three-stringed bowed instrument
- **Gendang** — a pair of double-headed drums
- **Tetawak** — a hanging knobbed gong

JKKN's records add that the basic ensemble is the rebab, a mother-and-child pair of gendang, and a gong, while fuller performances also use canang, kesi, geduk, gedombak and serunai. The serunai supplies the high, quavering tone that shapes the dramatic mood in certain scenes.

Compare this with [wayang kulit](/en/arts-culture/wayang-kulit), which is also centred in Kelantan and likewise uses gong, gendang and serunai — but no rebab, and with a single dalang standing in for the entire troupe of performers.

## Characters that recur from story to story

Mak Yong uses a set of stock characters. JKKN and Jabatan Warisan Negara record, among others:

- **Pak Yong** — a king or ruler
- **Pak Yong Muda** — the prince, the story's hero
- **Puteri Mak Yong** — the principal female character
- **Dayang / Inang** — attendants
- **Peran** — a clown, an assistant
- **Tok Wak**, **Betara Guru**, **Gergasi** — the elder, deity and ogre characters

One convention stands out: UNESCO notes that most characters are played by women. JKKN's records state the same thing more specifically — traditionally, female performers play all characters except for the clown role and certain attendant roles. That means even the king and prince are played by female performers.

The stories are drawn from myths, legends and classical hikayat filled with royal characters, deities and clowns. Jabatan Warisan Negara puts the main repertoire at 10 to 15 stories; JKKN's cultural mapping records put it at twelve complete stories, including *Dewa Muda*, *Raja Tangkai Hati* and *Gading Bertimang*.

> The figures genuinely differ between the two official sources. We list both and pick neither, because there is no official basis for rejecting one in favour of the other.

## From palace to village

JKKN's records trace Mak Yong's origins back more than 200 years, beginning at the Yala palace within the Patani kingdom before it spread to Kelantan and beyond. Jabatan Warisan Negara likewise describes it as a palace tradition at first, before it developed into rural community entertainment around the 1920s.

That shift, according to JKKN's records, occurred as patronage from the nobility declined — a change linked to the effects of the 1926 flood. As palace patronage receded, Mak Yong moved to village grounds.

UNESCO also records that Mak Yong was performed both as entertainment and for ritual purposes connected with healing practices, and that this art form predates the Islamisation of the region.

## How UNESCO's recognition came about

Many people cite "2005" and many also cite "2008". Both are correct, and they refer to two different events.

| Year | What happened |
| --- | --- |
| 2005 | Mak Yong was proclaimed a **Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity** in UNESCO's third proclamation |
| 4 November 2008 | All **90 Masterpieces** from the three proclamations (2001, 2003, 2005) were inscribed on the **Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity** |

The Masterpiece programme ended once the 2003 Convention entered into force — under Article 31.3 of the Convention, no further Masterpiece proclamations were made, and subsequent recognition has come through the Convention's own listing procedure. Mak Yong's nomination file in UNESCO's system carries the number 00167, under Malaysia.

## Status of performance

This section is stated as fact only.

Public performances of Mak Yong in Kelantan are restricted under the entertainment and places-of-entertainment control enactment passed by the Kelantan State Legislative Assembly (Dewan Undangan Negeri) in 1998, alongside several other traditional art forms including wayang kulit, menora and main puteri.

On 25 September 2019, Kelantan Deputy Menteri Besar Datuk Mohd Amar Nik Abdullah announced that the restriction on Mak Yong was being lifted, subject to conditions set by the state government: performances must comply with syariah requirements, contain no elements of worship, and performers must cover their aurat. The state government and the state religious department stated that monitoring would continue to ensure compliance with those conditions.

NegaraKu.md makes no judgement on that policy. What is recorded here is what happened, when, and who announced it.

## Common mistakes

**"Mak Yong is a dance."** It is dance theatre. Dance is only one of six elements; narrative, singing and improvised acting matter just as much.

**"Mak Yong was UNESCO-listed in 2005."** It was *proclaimed a Masterpiece* in 2005. Inscription on the Representative List took place on 4 November 2008, alongside 89 other Masterpiece elements worldwide.

**"Mak Yong and wayang kulit are the same thing."** Both are centred in Kelantan and share several instruments, but wayang kulit is shadow theatre with leather puppets and a single dalang, while Mak Yong is performed by a troupe of actors on an open stage.

**"All the male characters are played by men."** The opposite is true. Traditionally, female performers play almost every character, including Pak Yong and Pak Yong Muda.

## What threatens its continuity

UNESCO sets out the risk plainly: training in Mak Yong takes years, and few young people are willing to commit to an apprenticeship that demanding. The result, according to UNESCO, is a shrinking dramatic and musical repertoire and a shortage of experienced performers.

This is a different problem from the problem of recognition. A UNESCO certificate does not train a rebab player. What trains a rebab player is a living Tok Minduk, and a student willing to sit with them for years.
