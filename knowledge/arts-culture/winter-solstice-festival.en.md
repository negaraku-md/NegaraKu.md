On an evening in late December, the scent of glutinous rice flour mingles with the sweetness of ginger soup in the kitchen. The family gathers round the table, rolling little lumps of glutinous rice dough into balls — some white, some dyed pink or pale green. The balls in the children's hands come in all sizes, and as the adults roll they say: once you have eaten the tangyuan, you have grown a year older. This day is the Winter Solstice.

The Winter Solstice (Dongzhi) is a traditional Chinese seasonal festival, falling around 21–23 December each year — the day with the shortest daylight and longest night of the year. It is reckoned not by a lunar month but by the 24 solar terms (the position of the sun on the ecliptic), so its Gregorian date changes little from year to year. This article only describes the customs by which Malaysian Chinese observe the Winter Solstice and how it appears locally, noting that these practices vary from family to family and by dialect group.

## The Longest Night of the Year

The Winter Solstice marks the day with the shortest daylight and longest night in the Northern Hemisphere; after it, the days lengthen little by little. As one of the 24 solar terms, it was an important marker of the seasons in farming society, and was traditionally seen as the moment when "the yang energy is first born" — the turning point from winter toward spring.

Precisely because it is fixed by the solar term, the Winter Solstice carries a sense of steadiness within the Chinese sense of the calendar: unlike the many festivals that drift with the lunar calendar, it always arrives around 22 December.

## "Winter Outweighs the New Year": An Old Saying

A folk saying has been handed down: **"winter outweighs the New Year"**, also put as "the Winter Solstice is as big as the New Year". It means that the Winter Solstice is no less weighty than the New Year itself. This saying reflects how the Winter Solstice was traditionally a seasonal festival of the first importance — in ancient times it was even, for a period, kept as a major public festival.

In Malaysia, this old saying is still on the lips of some Chinese families. In places such as Penang there are Chinese families who feel the Winter Solstice deserves more attention than Chinese New Year — because it rests more purely on the single matter of "the family being together". Such views, however, vary from family to family and are not universal.

## Tangyuan: The Star of the Day

The most central custom of the Winter Solstice is eating **tangyuan** (also written as tangwan or dongzhiyuan). These are small balls rolled from glutinous rice flour and cooked in a sweet syrup; their round, full shape symbolises reunion, completeness and harmony.

Tangyuan is also tied to the idea of "gaining a year": in the past, folk tradition treated the Winter Solstice as the point at which one grew a year older, and elders would often say "eat the tangyuan and you are a year older". So many families do not merely eat them but make a point of **rolling them together** — the whole family seated round the table, shaping the balls by hand, is itself part of the reunion. These days many families use ready-made glutinous rice flour, sparing themselves the old labour of grinding the rice on a stone mill, but the habit of making them together has been kept.

## Sweet or Savoury: Every Family Differs

How tangyuan is made varies from family to family and by dialect group. The most widespread is **sweet tangyuan**: the little balls may be plain, or filled with black sesame, peanut or red bean, cooked and served in a ginger syrup or a lightly sweet syrup. In some families of Hokkien and Teochew heritage, however, **savoury tangyuan (salty soup balls)** is also made — the balls are dropped into a salty soup with vegetables, meat and other ingredients, served as a savoury dish.

In colour, tangyuan are commonly white, red or green; size is not fixed either, and some families will deliberately roll one especially large ball, set aside for a particular member of the household. These details are a matter of each family's own preference, with no uniform rule.

## Ancestor Worship and a Quiet Celebration at Home

In some Malaysian Chinese families, tangyuan are not only eaten by the household but first used for **ancestor worship and prayer**: the cooked tangyuan are offered before the ancestral tablets or the family altar, and then the family eats together. Such practices are most often seen in families that follow Taoism, Buddhism or traditional folk beliefs.

Worship, however, is not something everyone does — many families treat the Winter Solstice simply as a day for "the family to eat tangyuan", without any worship at all. In Malaysia, the Winter Solstice is not a public holiday, and there are no large processions or public celebrations; the Chinese mostly spend it quietly at home: after work and school, the family sits down together, rolls tangyuan and shares a meal, and that is all.

## Compared with the Major Festivals

Set against major festivals such as Chinese New Year, Deepavali and Hari Raya Aidilfitri — gazetted as public holidays and shared across ethnic lines — the Winter Solstice is on a far smaller scale and far more inward-looking: it belongs almost entirely within the family. Precisely because there is no holiday and no public activity, it feels more like a quiet day of "going home for a bowl of tangyuan".

It is this very quietness that makes the old saying "winter outweighs the New Year" so intriguing: a solar term with no day off and no fanfare nevertheless carries, in the hearts of some families, a weight that rivals the New Year.

## Common Misconceptions

**Thinking the Winter Solstice is just a "tangyuan festival" with nothing more to it.** Eating tangyuan is indeed central, but the Winter Solstice is first of all a solar term marking the shortest day of the year, carrying meanings of reunion, gaining a year and the turning of the seasons.

**Thinking tangyuan come only in a sweet form.** Sweet tangyuan are the most widespread, but some families of the Hokkien and Teochew dialect groups also make savoury tangyuan (salty soup balls), cooking the balls in a salty soup.

**Thinking the Winter Solstice must involve ancestor worship.** Ancestor worship and prayer vary from family to family — some families make an offering before eating, while many others simply eat tangyuan and reunite, without any worship.

**Thinking the Winter Solstice is a public holiday.** In Malaysia, the Winter Solstice is not a public holiday; the Chinese mostly celebrate it quietly at home rather than holding large public events.
