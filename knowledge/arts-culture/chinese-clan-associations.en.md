Walk into the area around Fort Cornwallis in George Town, Penang, and amid the rows of shophouses a clan temple with soaring, ornately carved eaves and painted beams suddenly appears — that is the Leong San Tong Khoo Kongsi. Head further toward the sea and you find rows of wooden stilt houses extending out over the water, each village named after a single surname: the Chew Jetty, the Lim Jetty, the Tan Jetty. These buildings are neither temples nor merely dwellings, but the physical remains of one of the oldest forms of organisation in Chinese immigrant society — the clan and hometown associations.

Chinese clan and hometown associations are the oldest grassroots organisations of Malaysia's Chinese community. They arose with the waves of migration in the nineteenth century, regrouping people who came from different parts of southern China, spoke different dialects and belonged to different surnames along the lines of "same hometown", "same clan" and "same trade". This article only describes their types, historical roles and contemporary functions, and makes no political or religious judgment.

## Three Ways of Forging Ties

Associations are generally classified by the bond that ties people together. Malaysia has tens of thousands of registered Chinese associations, most of which fall into the following three types (and many organisations combine several of these characteristics at once):

| Type | Bond | Common examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Territorial (native-place associations) | A shared ancestral hometown or dialect | Hokkien associations, Cantonese associations, Hakka associations / Kah Yeng associations, Teochew associations, Hainan associations, Kwangsi associations |
| Kinship (clan associations) | A shared surname or lineage | Tan, Lee and Lim clan associations; combined-surname (several-surname) bodies; large clan temples such as the Leong San Tong Khoo Kongsi |
| Trade (guild associations) | A shared trade or occupation | Various chambers of commerce and trade guilds; the Chinese chambers of commerce in each locality |

Territorial associations often overlap with dialect groups — early Chinese society was divided into "bang" (帮), with the Hokkien, Cantonese, Hakka, Teochew and Hainanese bang each having their own associations. For more on the dialect groups themselves, see [the Chinese dialect groups of Malaysia](/en/arts-culture/chinese-dialects).

## The Early Role: A Foothold for the Sinkeh

In the migration era from the nineteenth to the early twentieth century, the social security provided by the colonial government was limited. Migrants newly arrived from China were called "sinkeh" (新客) — strangers to the place, unable to speak the local language, with no family to turn to. Associations therefore became their first stop for settling in and finding their feet.

The early functions of associations were wide-ranging: receiving and lodging new arrivals, finding them work, extending small loans, mediating disputes between fellow townsmen, caring for the sick and weak, and handling funeral affairs. More importantly, associations pooled people's collective strength to build a great deal of public infrastructure — Chinese schools, temples, and the Chinese cemeteries (义山) where fellow townsmen were buried. Many of today's long-established Chinese schools can trace their origins back to the schools founded by associations or clan temples in those years. For more on this tradition of founding schools, see [Chinese education in Malaysia](/en/education/chinese-education-in-malaysia).

## Kinship Clan Associations and the "Kongsi": The Khoo Kongsi as an Example

Kinship-based clan associations are bound by a shared surname or lineage, and one of their core activities is the joint worship of ancestors — many clan associations maintain a clan temple housing the ancestral tablets. The **Leong San Tong Khoo Kongsi** in Penang is the most famous example.

The Khoo trace their ancestral home to the Xiamen area of Fujian and belong to the Hoklo community. According to records, the Khoo forebears built a clan temple in the mid-nineteenth century, naming it "Leong San Tong"; the original building burned down in 1901 and was rebuilt on a smaller scale in 1906, renowned for its fine wood carving, stone carving and painted decoration. In the nineteenth century, such a "kongsi" was like a miniature clan village, with its own education, finance, welfare and social organisation inside.

The "kongsi" here is not a registered enterprise in the modern sense, but a form of partnership and clan organisation commonly used by early Chinese immigrants. In the era of tin mining, a "kongsi" referred both to a mining enterprise jointly run by fellow townsmen or clansmen and to a body managing worship, mutual aid and internal self-government. The Khoo Kongsi is precisely a clan temple that bears the name "kongsi", not a commercial firm.

## The Two "Associations of Associations"

Above the local associations there are also national leadership bodies, often called the "associations of associations":

- **The Federation of Chinese Associations Malaysia (Huazong)**: founded in 1991, with the Chinese name 马来西亚中华大会堂总会. It is made up of the Chinese assembly halls or federations of Chinese associations of each state, and concerns itself with the cultural, educational, economic, social and livelihood issues of the Chinese community.
- **The Associated Chinese Chambers of Commerce and Industry of Malaysia (ACCCIM)**: founded on 2 July 1921, a federation of Chinese business bodies focusing on commercial and economic affairs, currently made up of seventeen constituent chambers across the states and federal territories.

Neither is a government body; both are voluntary civil federations made up of numerous local organisations, speaking for the Chinese community along two main lines — cultural-educational-social affairs and commercial-economic affairs respectively.

## Associations Today: Scholarships, Culture and Heritage Tourism

With the establishment of the social welfare system, the "emergency mutual aid" role that associations once played has changed greatly. The work of contemporary associations has largely shifted toward awarding scholarships and study loans, preserving dialects and traditional culture, holding festivals and social gatherings, and promoting community welfare.

At the same time, many long-established association buildings have become focal points of cultural tourism. After George Town's historic core was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2008, clan temples such as the Leong San Tong Khoo Kongsi and the seaside **Clan Jetties** all became popular attractions. The Clan Jetties are waterborne wooden stilt-house villages built by nineteenth-century Chinese immigrants who settled by surname, most of whose forebears came from the Tong'an area of Xiamen, Fujian; several still survive today — the Chew, Lim, Tan, Lee, Yeoh and Ong jetties and a mixed-surname jetty — of which the Chew Jetty draws the most visitors. Heritage tourism brings vitality on the one hand, but on the other has also prompted discussion about the lives of the original residents and the continuity of the community.

## Common Misconceptions

**Thinking that "huiguan" and "clan associations" are the same thing.** "Huiguan" usually refers to territorial organisations bound by ancestral hometown or dialect, while "clan associations" are bound by a shared surname or lineage; the bonds differ, and the names are not used strictly in practice.

**Thinking that a "kongsi" is a modern enterprise.** In the context of associations, a "kongsi" is a form of partnership and clan organisation of the early Chinese, combining economic, ancestral-worship and self-governing functions — not the same thing as a modern registered company.

**Thinking that associations are merely social clubs for the elderly.** Many associations to this day still award scholarships and study loans, preserve culture, promote welfare, and take part in heritage conservation.

**Thinking that Huazong or the ACCCIM are government bodies.** Both are voluntary civil federations formed by local organisations, not government departments.
