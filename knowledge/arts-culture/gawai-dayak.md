The clock has not yet struck midnight on the last day of May, and in a longhouse somewhere along a Sarawak river the gong is waiting. The rice is in. The gallery has been swept and hung with fresh mats. A jar of tuak sits ready. When the gong finally sounds, the longhouse chief will raise a cup, and everyone down the long communal veranda will drink to the same three words: long life, health, prosperity.

That is the heart of **Gawai Dayak** — the harvest festival of the Dayak peoples of Sarawak, celebrated every year on 1 and 2 June.

## What "Gawai Dayak" actually means

The name is two words doing two jobs.

*Gawai* means a festival or ritual. *Dayak* is a collective term for the indigenous peoples of Sarawak and, more broadly, of Borneo. It is not a single ethnic group but an umbrella covering many communities.

According to the Sarawak Tourism Board, the Dayak who keep Gawai include the **Iban, Bidayuh, Kayan, Kenyah, Kelabit, Lun Bawang — and many more**. The upriver groups among them — Kayan, Kenyah, Kelabit, Lun Bawang and others — are often referred to together as the **Orang Ulu**, meaning "upriver people".

So Gawai Dayak is not one community's festival that others borrowed. It is a shared harvest thanksgiving observed across a wide family of peoples, each with its own longhouse, language and variations of custom.

## A festival built around the rice harvest

Gawai marks the end of the *padi* (rice) harvesting season. After the work of planting, tending and reaping, it is the point at which the community pauses to give thanks for what the fields yielded and to ask for the seasons ahead to be kind.

That agricultural core is why the dates matter. The festival lands at the close of the harvest cycle, and in traditional belief the thanksgiving is directed to **Petara** — the gods or spirits. NegaraKu.md records these as the communities' own beliefs, described respectfully, not as assertions of fact.

## The sequence of a Gawai night

Although the public holiday falls on 1 June, the observance begins the evening before. The Sarawak Tourism Board records a recognisable sequence inside the longhouse.

| Stage | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| Muai Antu Rua (eve of 31 May) | Casting away the spirit of greed and bad luck before the celebration begins |
| Miring | The thanksgiving offering, opened with the beating of the *gendang rayah* (ritual music) |
| Ngalu Petara | A procession of young maidens and warriors moves up and down the *ruai* (longhouse gallery) seven times |
| Midnight toast | The gong is sounded and the chief leads a toast of *Ai Pengayu* — tuak for long life |
| Ngajat and open house | Days of dancing, food, tuak and visiting follow |

The **miring** is the ceremonial centre. It is presided over by the **Tuai Rumah**, the longhouse chief, who gives thanks for the good harvest. When the gong sounds at midnight, the community shares the *Ai Pengayu* and wishes one another long life, health and prosperity.

## The ngajat, the hornbill and the dance floor

No account of Gawai is complete without the **ngajat**, the traditional Dayak dance.

The male ngajat imitates the movements of a **hornbill** — the bird that gives Sarawak its nickname, the Land of the Hornbills — with steps meant to convey courage, heroism and strength. The women's ngajat is the opposite in temperament: soft, graceful, with slow and very precise turns of the body.

Around the dancing sits the rest of the celebration. **Tuak**, a rice wine brewed for the occasion, is offered to guests. Longhouse tables carry traditional dishes, among them **ayam pansuh** — chicken cooked inside a length of bamboo with seasonings and tapioca leaves, a dish the Iban and Bidayuh prepare especially for Gawai. And the longhouse door is thrown open: hospitality to visitors, family and neighbours is part of the festival, not an add-on to it.

## Rolling up the mat

Gawai does not simply stop at dusk on 2 June. In many communities the wider Gawai season continues through the month, and it is closed with a distinct ceremony.

That closing rite is the **ngiling tikai**, sometimes called *ngiling bidai* — literally "rolling up the mat". By rolling away the mats used through the festivities, the community signals, in the traditional understanding, that the Gawai season has ended: it is an honourable way of informing Petara and the spirits that the celebration is over so that the longhouse may return to ordinary life. It is typically held towards the end of June.

## How Gawai became a holiday

The customs are old. The public holiday is not — and its history is worth stating plainly.

The idea of a single annual festival for all the Dayak of Sarawak was aired publicly as early as **1957**, when it was proposed over Radio Sarawak. Before the festival was formally recognised, the colonial administration had designated 1 June simply as "Sarawak Day".

Gawai Dayak was **gazetted as a public holiday on 25 September 1964**, and it was celebrated officially for the first time on **1 June 1965**. From that point it has served as a shared occasion of thanksgiving, homecoming and identity for the Dayak communities of Sarawak.

| Year | Milestone |
| --- | --- |
| 1957 | The idea of a Dayak festival is proposed publicly over Radio Sarawak |
| 25 September 1964 | Gawai Dayak is gazetted as a public holiday |
| 1 June 1965 | First official Gawai Dayak celebration |

## Common misunderstandings

**"Dayak is one ethnic group."** It is a collective term. The Iban, Bidayuh and the various Orang Ulu communities are distinct peoples with their own languages and customs who share the festival.

**"Gawai is the same as Kaamatan."** Both are harvest festivals, but they are separate. Gawai Dayak belongs to the Dayak of Sarawak and falls on 1 and 2 June; [Kaamatan](/en/arts-culture/kaamatan) belongs to the Kadazandusun and other communities of Sabah and falls on 30 and 31 May.

**"The party is on 1 June."** The main ritual sequence starts on the *evening of 31 May*, and the season is not formally closed until the *ngiling tikai* later in June.

**"It's a tourist show."** Public celebrations and open houses do welcome visitors, but Gawai is first a family and longhouse observance — a homecoming, a thanksgiving and, for many, a return to the *kampung* or longhouse of their birth.
