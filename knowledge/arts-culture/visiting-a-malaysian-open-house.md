faq:
  - q: "Do I need to RSVP to an open house, or can I just turn up?"
    a: "An open house runs on an open invitation within stated hours, so a formal RSVP is usually not expected the way it would be for a sit-down dinner. How loosely that works depends on the host and how the invite was phrased — a WhatsApp 'come by anytime' is more relaxed than a printed card. When in doubt, send a short message to confirm the window and whether you may bring your family or a friend, rather than assuming either way."
  - q: "I don't eat certain foods or don't drink alcohol. Is it rude to decline?"
    a: "No. Polite declining on dietary or religious grounds is normal and well understood in Malaysia. Take a small portion of something you can eat, or just a drink, thank the host warmly, and explain simply — vegetarian, don't drink, fasting, halal only. A host would far rather you say so than go without or feel cornered. You are never obliged to eat or drink anything against your diet, faith or preference."
  - q: "Should I give duit raya or ang pow as a guest?"
    a: "Usually not, if you would normally be on the receiving side. Ang pow at Chinese New Year is conventionally given by married people to children and unmarried juniors; duit raya at Hari Raya is commonly given by working adults to children and the elderly. An unmarried guest typically receives rather than gives, and non-Muslim guests are not expected to give duit raya at all. Amounts are not fixed and vary by family — there is no mandatory sum, so do not feel you must match anyone."
  - q: "What should I wear?"
    a: "Neat and modest is the safe standard everywhere — cover your shoulders and knees, and avoid very short, slit or low-cut clothing, especially in a Malay-Muslim home or anywhere a prayer room (surau) is present. Festive colours suit Hari Raya and Deepavali; at Chinese New Year red and gold are auspicious and all-black or all-white is best avoided for its mourning associations. You will almost always remove your shoes at the door, so wear something easy to slip off."

You have been invited to a *rumah terbuka* — an open house. Maybe it's a
colleague's Hari Raya, a neighbour's Chinese New Year, a friend's Deepavali, or
a longhouse at Gawai. The invitation feels warm and a little daunting at once:
how open is "open," what do you bring, what do you wear, and what happens if the
food, the greeting or the gift turns out to carry a meaning you didn't know
about?

This guide answers that. It separates the courtesies that hold in almost every
Malaysian home from the places where communities genuinely differ — because the
quickest way to put a foot wrong is to treat one community's custom as a rule
for all of them. Throughout, one rule never fails: **follow the host's lead, and
ask when you're unsure.** This is etiquette, not religious ruling, and practice
varies by family, region, generation and degree of religiosity.

## What an open house actually is

The open house is a distinctively Malaysian expression of *muhibbah* — communal
goodwill. During major festivals, a household throws its doors open to a circle
far wider than family: neighbours, colleagues, friends and sometimes complete
strangers are welcomed to eat, greet and celebrate across ethnic and religious
lines (Arab News). The same pattern runs through Hari Raya Aidilfitri, Chinese
New Year, Deepavali, Christmas, and in East Malaysia Gawai Dayak and Kaamatan,
and it scales up to company and government open houses. For the origin and the
national-unity framing of the custom itself, see the concept note
[Rumah Terbuka](/en/glossary/open-house); this page is about being a good guest
at one.

The practical consequence of "open" is that there is usually **no tightly
managed guest list and no sit-down seating plan**. People come and go within
stated hours. If a Malaysian friend or colleague invites you, it is polite to
accept (Cultural Atlas).

## The invitation: how "open" works

- **Come within the window, not at the very start or the very end.** An open
  house runs over a block of hours; arriving somewhere inside that window is
  perfectly normal, and you need not stay for the whole thing (Free Malaysia
  Today).
- **A formal RSVP is usually not expected**, the way it would be for a dinner.
  But how loose the invitation is depends on the host — a WhatsApp "come by
  anytime" is more relaxed than a printed card. If you want to bring your family
  or a friend, or you're not sure of the hours, send a short message to confirm
  rather than assuming either way.
- **Greet the host when you arrive** rather than walking straight in, wish them
  the festival greeting, and thank them for having you (Free Malaysia Today).
- **Don't give yourself a tour, and don't be the last guest lingering.** Say
  goodbye to those present when you leave, especially the elders (Free Malaysia
  Today).

## The courtesies shared across (almost) every home

These hold regardless of whose open house it is:

- **Shoes off at the door.** Removing your shoes before entering a home is close
  to universal across communities — a pile of shoes at the entrance is your cue
  (Cultural Atlas). Wear something easy to slip off.
- **Accept at least a little.** Taking some food or a drink, even if you've
  already eaten, is read as courtesy and warmth. Wait for the host to signal you
  may start, then accept graciously (Cultural Atlas).
- **Use your right hand.** Much food is eaten by hand; wash first, and use the
  **right hand** to bring food to your mouth and to pass or receive things. The
  left hand is traditionally reserved for cleansing and isn't used alone to give
  or receive (Cultural Atlas).
- **Dress neatly and modestly** — cover shoulders and knees, more carefully in a
  Malay-Muslim home or where a prayer room (*surau*) is present (Malay Mail).
- **Greet the eldest first** and show elders extra deference (Cultural Atlas).
- **Declining politely is fine.** If something doesn't fit your diet or faith,
  take a small portion of something else or just a drink, thank the host, and
  explain simply. You are never obliged to eat or drink anything against your
  diet, religion or preference.

## Where homes genuinely differ

Keep these distinct. None of them is "the Malaysian custom" — each belongs to a
community, and even then varies by family.

### A Malay-Muslim home (Hari Raya Aidilfitri)

- The food is **halal** and the household does not serve pork or alcohol — the
  permissibility standard set by Malaysia's Islamic authorities (JAKIM, Halal
  Malaysia Portal). **Never bring** non-halal food, alcohol, pork-derived items,
  or gifts depicting pigs or dogs (Cultural Atlas; Malay Mail).
- Festive dishes you might meet include *ketupat*, *rendang* and *lemang* — but
  treat these as examples, not a fixed menu; spreads vary by family and region.
- A prayer room may be in use. If the household pauses to pray, wait graciously;
  don't enter a prayer space uninvited. Follow the host's lead.

### A Chinese home (Chinese New Year)

- **Mandarin oranges** are a classic token to bring and are often exchanged;
  their gold colour signals prosperity (New Straits Times).
- **Number and colour conventions** apply. Avoid gifts in sets of **four** (the
  word sounds like "death"), avoid giving **clocks** (associated with funerals)
  and sharp objects like knives or scissors (associated with "severing ties");
  red and gold are auspicious, while all-black and all-white carry mourning
  associations (New Straits Times). Many of the more obscure taboos are now
  loosely observed, so don't agonise — the headline ones above are enough.

### An Indian / Hindu home (Deepavali)

- **Some** Hindu households are vegetarian or serve a vegetarian spread; many
  others serve a mixed table for guests of every community — **do not assume
  either way** (Malay Mail). *Murukku* and sweets are typical.
- Remove your shoes before entering. You may see oil lamps and a decorative
  floor design (*kolam* / rangoli) at the entrance — admire them, and take care
  not to step on or disturb them.

### An East Malaysian longhouse (Gawai Dayak / Kaamatan)

- *Tuak* (rice wine; also *lihing* or *tapai* in Sabah) is central to
  hospitality, and sharing a drink is read as a mark of respect and acceptance —
  so a blunt refusal can feel like refusing the welcome itself (Zafigo; The
  Star).
- To pace yourself politely, accept a small cup, take a token sip, and keep the
  glass part-full rather than emptying and refilling it (Zafigo). If you don't
  drink, a simple, friendly "I don't drink alcohol" or "I'm driving" is generally
  understood — the festival, as Kaamatan hosts put it, is less about drinking
  than about sharing (Zafigo).

## Gifts, duit raya and ang pow

- A host gift (*buah tangan*, "fruit of the hands") — fruit, sweets, a small
  craft — is welcome but **often not required**; an over-grand gift can embarrass
  a host who then feels obliged to reciprocate (Cultural Atlas). Match the gift
  to the home: halal items only for a Muslim host; nothing in fours and no
  clocks or blades for a Chinese host; no leather or alcohol for a Hindu host.
- **Get the direction of giving right.** *Ang pow* at Chinese New Year is
  conventionally given by **married** people to children and unmarried juniors;
  *duit raya* at Hari Raya is commonly given by **working adults** to children
  and the elderly. If you'd normally be on the receiving side, you receive — you
  don't hand out money. **Non-Muslim guests are not expected to give duit raya**
  (SAYS).
- **Amounts are not fixed** and vary by family; there is no mandatory sum, so
  don't feel you must match anyone.

## Greetings and the cross-gender salam

A handshake is the common cross-ethnic greeting, but **many Muslim men and women
do not shake hands with the opposite gender**. The courteous response is to
place your **right hand over your heart with a slight nod** — and if someone
offers you that gesture, return it; it is a warm greeting, not a rejection of
you (Cultural Atlas; Facts and Details). **Let the other person lead** — wait to
see whether a hand is offered before extending yours.

A festival greeting in the right language is always warmly received: *Selamat
Hari Raya* at Raya, *Gong Xi Fa Cai* or *Selamat Tahun Baru Cina* at Chinese New
Year, *Happy Deepavali* at Deepavali, *Selamat Gawai* at Gawai.

## When you're not sure

Ask, or watch what the household does and follow it. Malaysians generally
welcome a guest who asks sincerely far more than one who guesses and gets it
wrong. A few steadying reminders:

- Ask before photographing people, food spreads, altars or a *kolam*.
- This is **etiquette, not a religious ruling** — nothing here is a *fatwa* or a
  halal certification, and you don't need to practise anyone's faith to be a
  respectful guest.
- Everything above is a typical pattern, not a law. Families differ, regions
  differ, and generations differ.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating one community's custom as universal.** The number-four taboo is a
  Chinese-New-Year point, not a rule for a Raya open house; halal is a
  Malay-Muslim point, not a blanket assumption about a Hindu home.
- **Bringing the wrong gift to a Muslim home.** Alcohol, anything pork-derived,
  or an image of a pig or dog is a serious misstep — bring halal items only.
- **Handing out duit raya when you'd normally receive it**, or assuming a
  non-Muslim guest must give it. Neither is expected.
- **Forcing food or drink on yourself to be polite.** Declining gracefully is
  normal; eating against your diet or faith is not required.
- **Cleaning your plate and waiting for a refill at a longhouse** when you meant
  to slow down — keep the glass part-full instead.
