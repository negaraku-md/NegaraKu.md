On the old streets of Penang, the shops that hold up an entire row of storefronts are mostly Hokkien-run businesses; in cities that rose on tin such as Kuala Lumpur and Ipoh, the mines and guilds were packed with Cantonese and Hakka people; and whatever town you walk into, the old coffee shop selling Hainanese coffee and toast with butter and kaya often has a Hainanese standing behind the counter.

All three are Chinese, yet behind them stand three different "bang". Although Malaysia's Chinese belong to a single ethnic group, they have traditionally been divided internally by ancestral origin into several groups — locally nicknamed "bang", also called dialect groups or ancestral-origin groups.

This article is only about these groups as a **community**: where they settled, which trades they were historically linked to, and the clan associations, temples and cemeteries they built. As for the tongues each bang speaks — Hokkien, Cantonese and Hakka are mutually unintelligible — that is a matter of language, dealt with separately in [Chinese dialects](/en/arts-culture/chinese-dialects). This article states data and sources, and does not evaluate any policy.

## "Bang": The Chinese Sub-groups of the Colonial Towns

To understand the "bang", we have to go back to the age of migration. From the nineteenth to the twentieth century, large numbers of Chinese left the south-eastern coast of China for the Nanyang (South Seas). In a foreign land with no home government to fall back on, people of the same ancestral origin who spoke the same tongue naturally gathered together — referring one another to work, pooling capital for business, looking out for each other. This grouping formed by ancestral origin is the "bang".

In the towns of the colonial era, the bang was not just fellowship but the basic unit for organising Chinese society. It often decided which street a newcomer settled on, which trade he entered, which temple he worshipped at, and on which hillside he would one day be buried. First, the demographic baseline: the Chinese make up about a fifth of Malaysia's citizen population (Department of Statistics Malaysia data), the second-largest ethnic group; and within that group are the main bang below.

## The Five Great Bang and Their "Trades of Old"

Each bang was historically linked to certain trades — the result of the order in which people migrated, settlement patterns and the interweaving of hometown referrals, not of any rule. Let us take them one by one.

### The Hokkien Bang — Ports and Commerce

The Hokkien (ancestral origins mostly in southern Fujian: Quanzhou and Zhangzhou) are the most numerous bang in Malaysia, concentrated especially in Penang in the north and in early-opened port cities such as Malacca. They were known for commerce from early on: import-export, wholesale, grocery retail — the business streets of many towns were held up by Hokkien firms. In quite a few states, Hokkien even became the lingua franca of trade.

### The Cantonese Bang — Tin Cities and Crafts

The Cantonese (ancestral origins around the Pearl River Delta in Guangdong) poured in in large numbers, inseparable from the nineteenth-century **tin-mining industry**. Cities that rose on tin, such as Kuala Lumpur, Ipoh and Seremban, drew great numbers of Cantonese migrants; to this day, Cantonese remains the common tongue of the Chinese in these central-region cities. Besides mining, the Cantonese were also known for crafts, construction and food.

### The Hakka Bang — Mining Pioneers, Chinese Medicine and Tailoring

The Hakka (ancestral origins across the hill country of eastern Guangdong, western Fujian and southern Jiangxi) were **pioneers in developing the gold and tin mines** of Malaya and Borneo; the mining districts of Selangor, Perak (Taiping, Ipoh), Negeri Sembilan, and Sabah and Sarawak all had large numbers of Hakka settlers. As mining declined, many moved to the rubber estates of Kedah and Johor. The Hakka were also well known for running **Chinese-medicine shops** and **tailoring**.

### The Teochew Bang — Cultivation and Trade

The Teochew (ancestral origins in the Chaoshan region of Guangdong) settled early around Province Wellesley (Seberang Perai) and Kuala Muda in Kedah, and were historically engaged in cultivating cash crops such as **gambier and pepper**; later they also settled in Johor, with some in fishing and trade.

### The Hainanese Bang — From European Cooks to Coffee Shops

The Hainanese (ancestral origins on Hainan Island) migrated to Malaya relatively late. By the time they arrived, most trades were already taken by other bang, so many first worked as **domestic helpers and cooks** in European households, or as seamen on steamships. It was in European kitchens that they learned Western-style cooking; once they had means, they set up on their own and opened **coffee shops (kopitiam) and restaurants**, blending Chinese and Western methods to develop the well-known **Hainanese coffee, Hainanese bread and Hainanese chicken chop** — "Hainanese Western food". Penang's Hainanese community took shape roughly after the mid-nineteenth century and built a temple dedicated to Mazu (Thean Hou) as its centre of faith and gathering.

### And Several Smaller Groups

Besides these five great bang, there is the **Guangxi bang (Gui)**, with ancestral origins in Guangxi, mostly settled around Bentong, Mentakab and Raub in Pahang; the **Foochow (Fuzhou) bang**, with ancestral origins in eastern Fujian, highly concentrated in Sibu, Sarawak; and smaller groups such as **Sanjiang** (the Jiangsu-Zhejiang-Shanghai area) and **Henghua (Putian)** — the Henghua were historically much connected with the transport trade (from rickshaws and trishaws to buses, taxis and motor parts).

## Clan Associations, Temples and Cemeteries: The Three Pillars of the Bang

The bang were not merely loose fellowship; they put down roots in a foreign land through three kinds of institution.

**Clan associations** came together by place (same hometown) or by kinship (same surname) and were the bang's most central organisation: fostering fellowship, settling disputes, helping newcomers, and bearing for years the duty of **running schools and education** — many Chinese-language schools were founded by clan associations. On this category of hometown and kinship organisation, see also [Chinese clan and hometown associations](/en/arts-culture/chinese-clan-associations).

**Temples** enshrined deities brought over from the homeland, and were both places of worship and community centres. The Hainanese mostly built **Thean Hou temples** dedicated to Mazu — this goddess of the sea being the very patron of seamen and fishermen; other bang each had their own native deities. In the early days many temples themselves also served as clan associations, with independent association bodies splitting off only later.

**Cemeteries** (Chinese communal burial grounds) gave people from the same place a resting place in death, and cemeteries divided by bang once existed in many towns. On the culture of Chinese burial and communal cemeteries, see [Chinese cemeteries and burial culture](/en/arts-culture/chinese-cemeteries).

Scholars have noted that clan associations and the faiths they enshrined often also played a role in **identifying and marking the boundaries of the bang** — which association one belonged to, and which native deity one worshipped, were themselves markers of bang affiliation.

## The Fading of the Lines Between the Bang

Today, the lines between the bang have clearly blurred. Several forces are at work: **cross-bang intermarriage** means more and more families carry several ancestral origins at once; **urbanisation** mixes people of different bang into the same city and the same neighbourhood; and **Mandarin**, through Chinese-language education, has become a cross-dialect common tongue — children of different bang communicate in Mandarin in the same classroom, and the dividing lines of ancestral origin naturally fade in daily life.

As a result, the younger generation's sense of ancestral origin is mostly weaker than their forebears'. But the bang have not disappeared: clan associations, hometown associations, ancestral halls and cemeteries around the country are still active, many people still know clearly "where their ancestors came from", and the bang's influence can still be seen at occasions such as Qingming tomb-sweeping and hometown-association gatherings. The bang are gradually shifting from being "the backbone that organised society" to "a memory of culture and history".

## The Difference from the Peranakan

One pair of easily confused concepts must be kept clearly apart: the **bang** and the **Peranakan (Baba-Nyonya)** are not the same thing.

The bang are divided by **ancestral origin** — they are about "which part of China the ancestors came from". The Peranakan refers to the group formed by early (mostly Hokkien) migrants who intermarried with locals and, over several generations of **localisation**, came to be known for a way of life built on the Malay language and a fusion of Chinese and Malay culture, found mostly around Malacca and Penang. In other words, a person can perfectly well be of Hokkien ancestry (the bang dimension) and also stand within the Peranakan cultural lineage (the localisation dimension) — the two are classifications on different dimensions and should not be conflated.

## Common Misconceptions

**Taking "bang" to be just accent or dialect.** The bang are first of all a **community** — a network of settlement, trades and institutions; language is only one facet. This article is about the community; the language details are in Chinese dialects.

**Thinking each bang still corresponds to a fixed trade.** "The Hokkien do business, the Hakka mine, the Hainanese run coffee shops" were broad **historical** tendencies, products of migration patterns, and no longer hold as such today.

**Equating the bang with the Peranakan.** The former is divided by ancestral origin, the latter is about localisation and intermarriage — two different dimensions.

**Thinking the bang have disappeared entirely.** The lines have indeed faded, but clan associations, cemeteries and hometown associations are still here, and ancestral origin lives on as a cultural memory.
