A speaker from Kota Bharu, one from Alor Setar and one from Kuching might all say they speak Malay — but when they chat casually in their respective villages, the sound can be so different that it takes a little adjustment to understand one another. It is this difference that is called a dialect, or in everyday language, loghat.

This article explains Malay dialects descriptively: what a dialect is, how it relates to the standard language, the main groups in the Peninsula and in Borneo, and how these varieties differ from one another.

## The standard language and dialects

The first distinction to understand is between **standard Malay** and **regional dialects**. The standard language is the standard variety of [Malay](/en/arts-culture/bahasa-melayu) used in education, official broadcasting, government documents and publishing. It is the variety that is coordinated and developed, among others by Dewan Bahasa dan Pustaka (DBP), as a common language across regions.

According to linguistic study, standard Malay in Malaysia is largely based on the **Johor-Riau dialect**, that is, the Malay variety of the southern Peninsula. In Mukhlis Abu Bakar's study of Johor-Riau pronunciation and standard pronunciation, Johor-Riau pronunciation is characterised as the pronunciation that became the standard reference in Malaysia and Singapore after the role of radio in the mid-20th century. The question of whether official pronunciation should be based on spelling (standard pronunciation) or on Johor-Riau pronunciation was once a language-policy issue that changed from time to time.

Regional dialects, meanwhile, are the everyday spoken varieties of local communities. A person typically uses their village loghat when chatting with family, but switches to the standard language in official matters — a form of register-switching that is common in a multi-dialect society. Dialects differ chiefly in **phonology** (the sound and pronunciation system) and **vocabulary**, with some differences in grammar.

Below is a brief overview of several main Malay dialects and their areas:

| Dialect / loghat | Main features and area |
| --- | --- |
| Johor-Riau (southern) | Basis of standard Malay; covers Johor, Melaka and Selangor as well as central and southern Perak |
| Kelantan-Pattani | Highly distinctive phonology and vocabulary; Kelantan and the Pattani region in southern Thailand |
| Terengganu | An east-coast variety related to the Kelantan-Pattani cluster but distinct |
| Northern / Kedah | Covers Kedah, Perlis, Penang and northern Perak (around Taiping) |
| Perak | Several subdialects; some belong to the southern cluster |
| Pahang | Interior and river dialects; studied among others by James T. Collins |
| Negeri Sembilan | Associated with Minangkabau ancestry from Sumatra |
| Sarawak Malay | A Bornean variety distinctive in pronunciation and vocabulary |
| Sabah Malay | The lingua franca of Sabah; close to Brunei Malay |
| Baba Malay | The variety of the Peranakan community; Malay vocabulary with Hokkien influence |

## Northern and eastern Peninsular dialects

In the northern part of the Peninsula lies the **northern dialect**, or Kedah dialect. According to Asmah Haji Omar's classification, the Kedah dialect covers Kedah (including Langkawi), Perlis, Penang and northern Perak around Taiping, and is in turn divided into several subdialects such as Coastal Kedah and Northern Kedah. The northern loghat is well known for its distinctive pronunciation features, including vowel-sound changes at the end of words.

On the north-east coast lie two of the most distinctive varieties. The **Kelantan vernacular** — closely related to the Pattani dialect in southern Thailand — has a sound system and vocabulary so different that speakers from other areas often need time to get used to it. The **Terengganu dialect** shares some features with the Kelantan-Pattani cluster but remains distinct. Recent academic study even suggests that these north-eastern varieties are distinctive enough that some scholars consider whether they should be regarded as languages of their own, not merely dialects.

Dialectologists do not fully agree on how to group the Peninsular dialects. Ismail Hussein (1973) divides them into four main clusters — Johor-Melaka-Selangor with central Perak; the Pattani-Kelantan-Terengganu cluster; the Negeri Sembilan cluster; and the Kedah dialect. Asmah Haji Omar, meanwhile, through phonological comparison, proposes a division into several groups with the Kedah dialect as the northern Malay dialect. There are also more detailed lists based on the districts of the speakers.

## The southern and Negeri Sembilan dialects

The **southern** cluster — Johor, Melaka and Selangor, together with central and southern Perak — is the area closest to the standard language, because it is this Johor-Riau dialect that forms the basis of the standard variety. For many speakers, the southern loghat feels the most "ordinary" because it is the one most often heard in broadcasting.

The **Negeri Sembilan dialect** is interesting because of its historical background. The Malay population of Negeri Sembilan is largely descended from Minangkabau migrants from West Sumatra, who also brought the adat perpatih system. For this reason, the Negeri Sembilan dialect is often associated with the Minangkabau language. Even so, lexical study shows that it is actually closer to standard Malay than to the Minangkabau language itself — an example of how the historical lineage of speakers and linguistic proximity do not necessarily go together. The timing and nature of the Minangkabau migration itself are still debated by historians.

## Bornean dialects: Sarawak and Sabah

Across the South China Sea, in East Malaysia, there are distinctive Bornean Malay varieties. **Sarawak Malay** has a pronunciation and vocabulary markedly different from the standard language, and functions as a language of communication among the state's multi-ethnic communities.

**Sabah Malay**, meanwhile, according to T. G. Hoogervorst's study, developed as a lingua franca in Sabah and is close to Brunei Malay, with its phonology and vocabulary also shaped by the indigenous and immigrant languages of the state. Some scholars see Sabah, Sarawak and Brunei Malay as part of a single dialect continuum, rather than sharply separate varieties. These Bornean Malay varieties exist alongside the dozens of [Bornean indigenous languages](/en/arts-culture/borneo-indigenous-languages) that do not belong to the Malay dialects.

## Baba Malay and other variations

Besides the regional dialects, there are also varieties formed through cultural and linguistic contact. The best known is **Baba Malay**, the variety of the Peranakan or Straits Chinese community. According to Nala Huiying Lee's grammatical study of Baba Malay, this variety combines Malay-based vocabulary with influence from the Hokkien dialect, and was formed as a result of contact between Hokkien-speaking traders and the local communities of the Malay world. It is spoken mainly in Melaka, Penang and Singapore, and now belongs among the endangered varieties. The status of Baba Malay — whether it is regarded as a creole or a kind of Malay dialect — is still a matter of scholarly discussion.

In addition, almost every state and district has its own subdialects and idiolects, so that researchers such as James T. Collins have compiled an extensive treasury of Malay dialects, covering the Pahang dialect, island dialects such as Tioman, and many more.

## Dialects in life today

Malay dialects are not a vanishing relic, but live on in everyday speech. The loghat is a strong marker of regional identity: speakers can often guess where someone comes from just by hearing their pronunciation and word choice. The loghat is also material for entertainment, drama, music and social media, and some of it is now being recorded and studied by linguists and institutions such as DBP.

At the same time, standard Malay remains the common variety for education, administration and official communication — enabling speakers of widely different loghat to stay easily connected. Dialects and the standard language are therefore not rivals, but two complementary layers in the linguistic life of the Malay community.
