Before dawn has fully broken, a line of cars has already pulled up outside the bride's home. The groom holds a bouquet, a few "brothers" behind him, but he is stopped by several "sisters" peering out from the doorway: first answer their questions, then sing a song, eat a few paper-thin slices of lime, and finally push a few red packets through the gap in the door — only once this "door-crashing" (chuang men) challenge is passed does the door open and the groom get to see the bride, dressed all in red. A morning like this, full of teasing and merriment, is how many Chinese weddings in Malaysia begin.

Traditional Chinese wedding customs are a set of life rituals that Malaysia's Chinese community has carried on for many generations. They are not a single ceremony but a process made up of a series of stages — from the pre-wedding betrothal, setting of the bridal bed and hair-combing, to the fetching of the bride, the tea ceremony and the wedding banquet on the day itself, and then the return visit after the wedding. This article only describes these customs themselves and their common local practice, and does not pass judgment on any religious belief; the details of each stage vary by dialect group (such as Hokkien, Cantonese, Teochew, Hakka and Hainanese) and family, and many Malaysian Chinese families also blend modern and Western elements on top of tradition.

## Guo Da Li: Betrothal, Bride Price and Dowry

Wedding preparations often begin with the two families "matching the eight characters" (ba zi, birth data) and discussing an auspicious date, and the formal betrothal ritual is called "guo da li". Before the wedding (commonly on an auspicious day about two to four weeks beforehand), the groom's family delivers the **bride price** and an assortment of gifts to the bride's home, as a token sealing the marriage contract.

The contents of the gifts differ among dialect groups; common ones include dragon-and-phoenix wedding cakes, pastries, wine, tea, and ceremonial items in matching pairs, often accompanied by gold jewellery for the bride. On the same day the bride's family usually "returns gifts" — handing back part of the bride price together with some of the gifts, though jewellery is generally not returned — to show the two families are equal and mutually respectful. The bride's side also prepares a **dowry**, which may include everyday items, bedding, gold jewellery and even things for the new home. The amount of the bride price, the proportion returned and the contents of the dowry have no fixed standard, depending on discussion between the two families and their circumstances.

## An Chuang and Shang Tou: Two Pre-Wedding Rites

Before the wedding there are two further rites, mostly carried out at home.

**An chuang (setting the bridal bed)** is the dressing of the couple's wedding bed: on a chosen auspicious day and hour, an elder who is "blessed with good fortune" (happily married with both sons and daughters) lays out brand-new red bedding and places on the bed items such as longans, red dates, lotus seeds and peanuts, symbolising "may you soon have precious children" and "completeness"; some families also invite a young boy to roll about on the new bed for the good omen of "adding sons". After the bed is set, others are generally not supposed to sit or lie on it carelessly.

**Shang tou (hair-combing)** is a coming-of-age combing rite carried out separately in the homes of both the bride and the groom on the eve of the wedding, symbolising that the couple are entering adulthood and about to start a family. After bathing and changing, the bride or groom has their hair combed by a parent or a "person of good fortune", who recites four auspicious lines while combing, commonly "one stroke to the end (of a long life together), two strokes for growing old together, three strokes for children and grandchildren filling the hall…". Some families are particular about doing it where the moon can be seen. Practice varies by family — some keep the full set, some simplify.

## Welcoming the Bride: Fetching the Bride and "Sisters Tease the Groom"

On the morning of the wedding, after a little preparation at home, the groom leads his "brothers" as a **bride-welcoming** procession to the bride's home to **fetch the bride**.

At the door, he usually first has to get past a series of challenges set up by the bride's "sisters" (bridesmaids), locally known as **"sisters tease the groom" (chuang men, door-crashing)**: the sisters set tasks to test the groom's sincerity and rapport — all sorts of things, from answering questions about the relationship, performing a talent and eating sour-and-spicy food to completing little physical games — while the groom and his brothers keep handing out red packets to "buy their way through". This part is lively and playful, not a solemn rite; once past it, the groom can finally enter and see the bride, and take her from her maiden home to the groom's house or a hotel.

## The Tea Ceremony and Wedding Banquet: Serving Tea, Return Gifts and the Feast

**Feng cha (the tea ceremony / serving tea)** is one of the weightiest stages of a traditional wedding, expressing the couple's respect and gratitude to their elders. In order of seniority, from eldest to youngest, the couple serve tea one by one to their parents, grandparents and other elders, addressing them by their new titles (such as now calling them "Dad" and "Mum"); after drinking the tea, the elders usually give **red packets or gold jewellery** in return, signifying acceptance and blessing. Tea is usually served first at one family's home, then, after the bride changes her outfit, at the other's.

In the evening comes the **wedding banquet (xi jiu)** — the feast for relatives and friends. Local Chinese wedding banquets are mostly Chinese-style table dinners, often with eight to ten courses whose names and presentation all carry auspicious meanings, such as roast suckling pig, lobster, fish ("surplus year after year"), black moss, longevity noodles or sweet tang yuan (for a complete and sweet union). During the meal, the couple go from table to table to **toast (jing jiu)** their guests in thanks, while the guests give "red packets" as congratulatory gifts. Many banquets also incorporate Western elements such as cutting a cake, a champagne tower and the crossed-arm "cup of union" toast.

## Hui Men, Auspicious Dates, Colours and Taboos

After the wedding, the bride returns with the groom to visit her parents, which is called the **return visit (hui men, "three-morning return")**, traditionally on the third day after the wedding, with the bride's family hosting a meal — symbolising that although the bride has married out, her ties to her maiden family continue.

Running through the whole wedding are the colour **red** and the **"double happiness" (囍) character** — appearing on invitations, gifts, the bridal bed, wedding attire and the venue décor, the most emblematic festive symbols of a Chinese wedding. An **auspicious date** is also usually chosen for the wedding. Other common conventions include preferring **even numbers** for cash gifts and gifts, avoiding numbers that sound unlucky, and pregnant women or those in mourning sometimes staying away from certain stages. How strictly these taboos are kept varies by family belief and dialect group, and many families keep only some of them.

## Dialect-Group Differences and Modern Fusion

Malaysia's Chinese come from several dialect groups, each of which has kept some distinctive wedding details; the following is a common general outline (actual practice still varies by family):

| Dialect group | Common wedding features (examples) |
| --- | --- |
| Hokkien (Minnan) | Sugarcane (whole with roots and leaves, in pairs) is common at the betrothal and in the new home, symbolising "a good beginning and end", rising prosperity and protection |
| Teochew | Values the "si dian jin" gold set (necklace, pendant, earrings, bracelet), and commonly prepares Teochew-style pastries and sweets |
| Cantonese | Particular about pastries and the etiquette of "returning gifts"; the ritual wording and offerings for shang tou, an chuang and the like form a style of their own |
| Hakka | Keeps plainer, simpler customs, valuing the tea ceremony and the participation of clan elders |
| Hainanese | Relatively simple customs, with details depending on each family's handed-down traditions |

Over recent decades, Chinese weddings in Malaysia have generally taken on a "tradition alongside modernity" face: many couples keep core stages such as the betrothal, hair-combing and tea ceremony, while also adopting a Western wedding gown, a church or outdoor solemnisation, and wedding planning and photography. Which to keep and which to simplify is often decided jointly by the elders of both families and the couple.

## Common Misconceptions

**Assuming every Chinese wedding in Malaysia is the same.** From the contents of the betrothal gifts to the ritual wording and the taboos observed, the Hokkien, Cantonese, Teochew, Hakka, Hainanese and other dialect groups each differ, and even different families within the same dialect group are not identical.

**Thinking the bride price is "selling the daughter" or that the bride's family keeps all of it.** The bride price is a ceremonial token sealing the marriage, its amount discussed between the two families; many families return part of it together with the return gifts, or fold the bride price into the dowry for the couple to set up home.

**Confusing "an chuang" with "shang tou".** An chuang is the setting of the bridal bed, while shang tou is the hair-combing coming-of-age rite; they are two different pre-wedding rites.

**Thinking "sisters tease the groom" is a compulsory formal rite.** The door games are a lively, playful part; whether they are done, and how far they go, is entirely up to both sides, and many weddings simplify or skip them.
