Once the seventh lunar month arrives, the nightfall scene at many street corners in Penang, Klang and Johor quietly changes: small tables appear by the roadside, laid with fruit, rice, tea and stacks of gold-and-silver joss paper, incense and candles flickering in the dusk. On open ground in the neighbourhood, a stern-faced paper deity several metres tall has been raised, a stage set up facing it; after dark the lights blaze and singing rings out — yet the whole front row of chairs below the stage stays empty throughout.

This is the Hungry Ghost Festival, the folk observance held by Malaysia's Chinese community in the seventh lunar month. Folk belief holds that in this month the 'Gate of Ghosts opens' and spirits wandering the world (commonly called 'good brothers') return to the realm of the living, so people make offerings and perform 'universal deliverance' to appease and provide for them. This article only describes its customs and how they are presented locally, and passes no judgment on any religious belief.

## The Seventh Lunar Month: The Belief That the 'Gate of Ghosts Opens'

The sense of time in the Hungry Ghost Festival is set against the seventh lunar month. According to folk and Taoist accounts, on the first day of the seventh month the 'Gate of Ghosts opens', the gateway of the underworld swings wide, and the many ownerless spirits are able to return to the human world until the gate 'closes' again at the end of the month. The whole seventh month is therefore regarded by many as a time for caution and restraint, while the fifteenth of the seventh month (the main day of Zhongyuan) is often seen as the peak of the offerings.

The festival goes by several names: folk tradition commonly calls it the 'Ghost Festival'; Taoism calls it 'Zhongyuan', which together with Shangyuan (the Lantern Festival) and Xiayuan makes up the 'Three Yuan'; and Buddhism calls it 'Ullambana' or the 'Yulan Assembly', from the story of Mulian rescuing his mother and delivering departed kin. In Malaysia these names are often used interchangeably, and in Hokkien the whole festival's offerings are commonly referred to as 'Phor Thor' (普度).

## Two Levels: Family Offerings and the Yulan Assembly

Malaysia's Hungry Ghost Festival largely plays out on two parallel levels.

The first is **offerings by families and businesses**. After dark, people set out offering tables by the roadside in front of their homes or shops, laying out cooked food, fruit, tea and wine, and burning gold-and-silver joss paper and 'street clothes' (paper money printed with images of clothing, for the spirits to use). This practice of burning offerings along the street is commonly known as 'burning street clothes'. The offerings are set out for the 'good brothers' who wander about with no one to make offerings to them, in the hope of peace and good fortune.

The second is the **community-organised Yulan assembly**. Locality associations, temples, business federations and neighbourhood committees organise large public ceremonies, setting up altars, chanting scriptures, preparing lavish offerings and delivering the local spirits, with getai and other performances, communal dinners and charity fundraising running throughout. Many Yulan assemblies are run year after year by standing committees and are an important occasion for fellowship and mutual aid within the local Chinese community.

## Da Shi Ye: The Presiding King of Ghosts

The visual centrepiece of a Yulan assembly is often a towering paper effigy of **Da Shi Ye**. Commonly called the 'King of Ghosts' or 'Phor Thor Gong', Da Shi Ye is believed to keep the many spirits returning to the world in the seventh month under control, maintaining order so that they do not cause trouble or linger too long; some ceremonies also set up his two attendant generals to help keep watch.

Da Shi Ye is mostly made from bamboo strips and paper, fierce of face and astonishing in scale — associations in different places often vie with one another over how 'tall' their effigies are. For instance, the Da Shi Ye of the Bukit Mertajam area in Penang grew, according to reports, from about 27 feet in 2019 to about 28 feet in 2022; and in 2026 the Da Shi Ye paper effigy at the Dou Mu Gong in Alor Setar, Kedah, was billed as one of the tallest in Southeast Asia at about 15.24 metres. At the close of the festival, Da Shi Ye is burnt together with heaps of paper offerings, signifying that he and the spirits are being respectfully sent back.

## The Getai: Entertaining Both Spirits and the Living

Setting up a stage opposite the Yulan assembly for performances is one of the most recognisable sights of the Malaysian Hungry Ghost Festival. Traditionally the 'grand shows' were mostly Teochew opera and puppet shows, originally performed for the 'good brothers' to watch; in recent decades the cheaper and livelier **getai** (Hokkien 'Ko Tai', live singing and variety performances) has gradually become the mainstream. According to Penang reports, hiring a Teochew opera troupe can cost several thousand ringgit a night, while a getai often costs only about half of that, which is why it has become more widespread.

The getai has one widely known rule: **the front row of seats is to be left empty**, reserved for the unseen guests who have come to 'watch the show', and the audience present generally will not sit in the front row. This practice of 'leaving the front row open', like the roadside offering tables and the burnt joss paper, embodies the core idea of the Hungry Ghost Festival — 'playing host to the good brothers'.

## Bidding and Charity: The Face of Community Mutual Aid

A Yulan assembly often ends with a communal dinner, and the liveliest part of the dinner is the **bidding** (bidding for blessed items). The organisers put the auspicious offerings from the altar tables — fruit, rice, drinks, and charcoal sticks known as 'black gold', all counted as 'blessed items' — up for open auction one by one, with the highest bidder 'winning' each, taking the lucky meaning of 'bidding' the good fortune home.

The proceeds from the bidding and the dinner-time donations are usually used to cover the hefty cost of the ceremony, with the remainder donated to good causes — most commonly to support Chinese-language schools. According to reports, Penang's united Hungry Ghost Festival organisations have in recent years continued to raise funds for Chinese schools, with a single school year's target and donations reaching into the millions of ringgit. This makes the Hungry Ghost Festival in Malaysia not only a folk ceremony but also a charitable platform that binds the community together and gives back to Chinese education.

## Common Taboos and a Quick Guide to the Period

A good many taboos circulate around the seventh lunar month. They differ from person to person and family to family; some keep to them strictly, while others treat them as reminders to be careful rather than hard rules:

| Point / matter | Common saying |
| --- | --- |
| 1st of the seventh month | The 'Gate of Ghosts opens', the period begins, some households start roadside offerings |
| 15th of the seventh month (Zhongyuan) | The peak of universal deliverance, with offerings and Yulan assemblies clustered around these days |
| End of the month | The 'Gate of Ghosts closes', the period ends, the Da Shi Ye effigy is burnt and sent off |
| Night-time | Folk advice to return home early and not to linger alone in remote places late at night |
| Near water | A saying that one should avoid playing in water or night swimming in the seventh month |
| Words and deeds | Sayings such as not to step on roadside offerings, not to pick up money off the ground, and not to say the word 'ghost' outright |

It should be stressed that the taboos above belong to the realm of folk custom, and that what is observed differs from place to place and family to family.

## It Is Not Qingming

One common confusion is to treat the Hungry Ghost Festival and **Qingming** as the same thing. Both are connected with remembering the dead, yet they are different festivals held at different times: Qingming falls around the third lunar month (roughly early April on the Gregorian calendar), and its core is sweeping the graves of one's ancestors and paying respects to **one's own forebears**; the Hungry Ghost Festival falls in the seventh lunar month, and its core is 'universal deliverance' — making offerings to the **ownerless, wandering spirits** roaming the world — with public Yulan assemblies organised by associations. Grasping this is the key to understanding the differences between the two in timing, object and atmosphere.

## Common Misconceptions

**Equating the Hungry Ghost Festival with a particular religion.** It blends elements of folk belief, Taoism (Zhongyuan) and Buddhism (Ullambana), but many Malaysian Chinese take part in it as a cultural and community tradition, whatever their personal beliefs. This article only describes the customs themselves and passes no judgment on any belief.

**Assuming the Hungry Ghost Festival is a public holiday.** It is not a nationwide or state public holiday in Malaysia; the offerings, Yulan assemblies and getai mostly take place in the evening and at night after working hours.

**Assuming the Hungry Ghost Festival is the same as Qingming.** The two fall at different times and make offerings to different recipients — Qingming is tomb-sweeping for one's own ancestors, while the Hungry Ghost Festival is universal deliverance for ownerless spirits.

**Assuming the practice is the same nationwide.** From dialect and offerings to the scale of Da Shi Ye and the grandeur of the getai, the Hungry Ghost Festival in Penang, the Klang Valley, Johor and elsewhere each has its own local character, and the practices are not uniform.
