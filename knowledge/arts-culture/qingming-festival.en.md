One early April morning, families begin to appear along the paths of the cemetery. Some carry buckets and sickles; others carry baskets filled with cooked food, fruit and joss paper. Finding their family's grave, the younger ones set to clearing the weeds and sweeping away the leaves while the elders point out how to repaint, stroke by stroke, the faded characters on the headstone; only once the grave is clean are the offerings laid out, incense lit and respects paid, and finally the joss paper burned on a clear patch of ground nearby. Amid the smoke and flame, another year's Qingming passes.

The Qingming Festival (most Malaysian Chinese use the Hokkien name Cheng Beng, meaning the tomb-sweeping festival) is the traditional Chinese occasion for honouring the departed and tending ancestral graves. It corresponds to the solar term "Qingming" among the twenty-four solar terms of the lunar calendar, usually falling on or around 4 or 5 April in the Gregorian calendar. The Chinese make up about a fifth of Malaysia's citizen population (DOSM data puts it at 22.2% in 2025), and although Qingming is not a public holiday, it is an important season of ancestor worship in the year of the local Chinese community. This article only describes its customs and how they are presented locally, and does not pass judgment on any religious belief.

## What the Qingming Festival Is

Qingming is both a solar term and a festival. As a solar term, it marks the time across northern China when winter gives way to spring and the weather turns "clear" (qing) and "bright" (ming); as a festival, it is the day on which Chinese people express filial piety and remember their forebears. The core activity of the season is tomb-sweeping — going to the grave of an ancestor, or to the columbarium where their ashes are kept, to tend and pay respects.

Because it is set by a solar term (rather than by a particular day of a lunar month), the Gregorian date of Qingming is fairly fixed, mostly on or around 4 or 5 April. Together with Chinese New Year at the start of the year and the Hungry Ghost Festival in the seventh lunar month, it forms one of the main seasonal observances in the Chinese year.

## Tomb-Sweeping: From Tending to Worship

The first part of tomb-sweeping (known locally as "bai shan") is the "sweeping" — tending the grave. On arriving, the family first clears the weeds on and around the grave, sweeps away fallen leaves, straightens whatever has gone crooked or loose, and repaints the headstone inscription that has faded under sun and rain, so the text can be read clearly again. This work is often shared among the younger, more able-bodied members of the family.

Only once the grave is clean does the worship begin: cooked food, fruit, tea and wine and flowers are laid out, incense and candles are lit, and respects are paid in order of seniority. Before paying respects to their own ancestors, many families first pay their respects to the **Hou Tu (that is, Tu Di Gong, the Earth Deity)** beside the grave — the Hou Tu is regarded as the deity guarding that plot of ground. The whole process is a matter of reverence and the wish to "look after one's forebears well".

## Offerings and Burning: Joss Paper and Paper Effigies

Qingming offerings fall roughly into two kinds. One is food: meat dishes such as chicken and roast pork, assorted fruit, and cakes such as pink and golden steamed sponge cakes (fa gao); after the worship, this food is usually taken home to be eaten. The other is what is burned: **joss paper (paper money) and paper effigies**.

Burning paper money and paper clothes, and even paper houses and paper cars and other effigies, symbolises the "sending" of money and everyday goods to forebears for use in the other world; this is a very characteristic part of Chinese ancestor worship. Because it involves open flames and smoke, many cemeteries and memorial parks set aside dedicated burning areas or lay down rules, and in recent years some families have switched to simpler, more environmentally friendly approaches.

## Visits Spread Across About Ten Days

Although Qingming has a "proper day", tomb-sweeping is not limited to that day. Traditionally, visits may take place within a few days before or after Qingming; in Malaysia, many families stretch this to a window of roughly ten days around Qingming, flexibly choosing a weekend or holiday on which the whole family can go together.

There are practical reasons for this arrangement: cemeteries are often crowded with people and vehicles on the proper day, and staggering the dates helps avoid the crush; family members working or studying away also need time to return home. A family will therefore often agree on a date in advance and go up to the grave together once everyone has arrived.

| Point in time | Common practice |
| --- | --- |
| About ten days before Qingming | Families going earlier begin sweeping the graves, avoiding the crowds of the proper day |
| The proper day of Qingming (around 4/5 April) | The peak of grave-visiting, with cemeteries and memorial parks at their busiest |
| About ten days after Qingming | Families going later, or waiting for members to return home, visit in turn |

## Where Graves Are Swept: Cemeteries, Memorial Parks and Columbaria

Across Malaysia there are Chinese cemeteries (public burial grounds) as well as modern, privately run memorial parks. The most historically significant is **Bukit Cina in Melaka** — an old hillside burial ground preserving a vast number of traditional Chinese graves, generally described as one of the largest Chinese cemeteries outside China, and said to be connected with the retinue that came south with Princess Hang Li Po in the fifteenth century and settled here; today it is also a public green hill where people walk and exercise in the morning.

Besides traditional cemeteries, the memorial parks that have arisen in recent decades (such as Nirvana Memorial Park) offer neatly planned grounds and **columbaria (where ashes are kept)**. As cremation becomes more common, more and more families visit a columbarium to pay their respects at Qingming rather than a traditional burial grave; this has also moved some of the observance from the open hillside indoors.

## The Qingming Festival ≠ the Hungry Ghost Festival

Qingming is often confused with the seventh-lunar-month Hungry Ghost Festival, but the two actually differ in both their focus and their setting. Put simply, Qingming tends **one's own ancestors**, whereas the Hungry Ghost Festival mainly makes offerings to **wandering, ownerless spirits**.

| Aspect | Qingming Festival | Hungry Ghost Festival (seventh lunar month) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Timing | Around April in the Gregorian calendar (a solar term) | The seventh lunar month |
| Focus of worship | One's own ancestors and forebears | Mainly wandering, ownerless spirits |
| Main locations | Cemeteries, Chinese burial grounds, columbaria | Homes, roadsides, temples, public offering grounds |
| Typical practices | Tomb-sweeping, tending graves, offerings, burning paper | Offerings to spirits, almsgiving, worship, often with public activities such as getai stage shows |

## Common Misconceptions

**Thinking the Qingming Festival is a public holiday.** The Qingming Festival is not a national or state public holiday in Malaysia, and most businesses operate as usual on the day; families mostly rely on weekends or the flexible period around the festival to sweep the graves.

**Confusing the Qingming Festival with the Hungry Ghost Festival.** Qingming falls around April and tends one's own ancestors; the Hungry Ghost Festival falls in the seventh lunar month and is mainly about releasing and making offerings to wandering spirits — the two differ in focus and in practice.

**Thinking the grave must be swept on the proper day.** Tomb-sweeping may take place within the roughly ten days around Qingming, and it is precisely this that many families use to avoid the crowds and wait for members to return home.

**Thinking tomb-sweeping is just "paying respects".** The cleaning and tending before the worship — clearing weeds, sweeping, repainting the headstone — are just as much a part of tomb-sweeping; the word "sweep" itself makes this plain.
