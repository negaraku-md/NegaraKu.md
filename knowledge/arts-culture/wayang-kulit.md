  - q: "Is Malaysian wayang kulit the same as Indonesian wayang?"
    a: "They are related but distinct traditions. Wayang Kulit Kelantan has its own repertoire, puppet-carving style, ensemble and performance conventions. UNESCO inscribed the Indonesian wayang puppet theatre on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity; that inscription is for the Indonesian tradition and does not cover the Kelantanese form."
  - q: "Why is it rarely performed now?"
    a: "Two pressures overlapped. From the 1990s the Kelantan state government restricted performances on religious grounds, and over the same period television, film and other entertainment displaced the village demand that had sustained the form. The combined effect was fewer performances and fewer young performers training as dalang."

The lamp is lit first. Behind a white cotton screen stretched tight on a wooden frame, one man sits cross-legged, a stack of flat leather puppets within reach on either side of him, a small orchestra of drums, gongs and a piercing double-reed serunai waiting behind him. He will not leave that spot for the rest of the night. By the time the last shadow crosses the screen, hours from now, he will have voiced kings, demons, clowns and princesses, narrated the transitions between scenes, and cued every musician — alone.

This is the Tok Dalang, the puppet master at the centre of Wayang Kulit Kelantan, and the performance that unfolds around him is one of the oldest continuously practiced theatrical forms in Malaysia.

## One man, every voice

The puppets themselves — kulit means "skin" or "hide" — are cut and carved from buffalo or cow leather, painted, and mounted on sticks that let the dalang articulate their arms from behind the screen. But the puppets are only half the craft. A dalang trains for years to hold dozens of distinct character voices, switch between them mid-sentence, deliver comic asides in regional dialect, and keep a live orchestra playing to the correct rhythm for a scene — all without a script, since much of the performance is improvised around a known story outline rather than read from a fixed text.

## Borrowing a story and making it local

The core repertoire comes from the Hikayat Seri Rama, a Malay retelling of the Ramayana — the story of Rama, Sita and the demon king Ravana, filtered through centuries of Malay court and village storytelling until its characters, clowns and moral emphases feel distinctly local rather than imported. This lineage is itself a small monument to how old Indian religious epics travelled through Southeast Asia and were absorbed into local performing arts long before Islam reached the peninsula — the same current of Hindu-Buddhist cultural influence visible in Langkasuka's court centuries earlier survives, transformed, in a Kelantanese puppet show.

## A ritual, not just a show

Older Wayang Kulit performances typically opened with a short ritual segment intended to appease unseen forces and protect the performers and audience — a reminder that the show was understood, for much of its history, as something closer to a communal rite than pure entertainment. Performances traditionally ran through an entire night, staged for village celebrations, harvest thanksgiving, or simply because a patron wanted one — a form of entertainment that assumed an audience with the patience, and the darkness, of an entire evening to spare.

> A single dalang, one lamp, and a night that doesn't end until the story does — that was the whole technology required.

## A tradition under pressure

Wayang Kulit's fortunes changed sharply from the 1990s onward. Kelantan's state government, led by the Islamic party PAS, moved to restrict performances it considered incompatible with Islamic teaching — a policy shift that, alongside competition from television and film, cut deeply into how often the art form was staged and how many young performers came forward to train as dalang. What was once regular village entertainment became, within a generation, an occasional cultural showcase — kept alive mostly through festivals, tourism performances and cultural preservation efforts rather than the everyday demand that originally sustained it.

## Why it still matters

Wayang Kulit is a rare case where you can trace, in a single living art form, the layered history of the Malay world: a pre-Islamic Indian epic, adapted into Malay language and humour, performed through a craft (leather puppet carving) with its own separate lineage, accompanied by an ensemble whose instruments hint at older regional trade in music and craft. Its current precariousness is not abstract heritage-loss rhetoric — the number of practising Tok Dalang capable of a full traditional performance is small and ageing, which makes every remaining performance closer to a documentation opportunity than routine entertainment. Losing it would not just mean losing a puppet show; it would mean losing one of the last visible threads tying modern Malay culture back to a much older regional story tradition.
