> Pisang emas dibawa belayar,
> Masak sebiji di atas peti;
> Hutang emas boleh dibayar,
> Hutang budi dibawa mati.

Almost every Malay knows this stanza. Its first two lines tell of a golden banana ripening by itself on a chest during a voyage — an image that at first glance has nothing to do with anything. But the next two lines open up its meaning: a debt of gold can be settled, yet a person's kindness and service is carried to the grave. That is the pantun — a form of poetry that builds an image first, and only then delivers its message.

This article explains the pantun descriptively: what a pantun is, how its structure works, what its types are, what its functions are in Malay culture and custom, and how it came to be recognised as world heritage.

## What a pantun is

The pantun is a form of traditional Malay oral poetry. According to the *Kamus Dewan* published by Dewan Bahasa dan Pustaka (DBP), the pantun is "a type of old poetry that usually consists of four lines in each stanza", with "the first two lines being the foreshadowing of the meaning and the rest containing its meaning".

Two important things are contained in this definition. First, the pantun is *oral* poetry — it was born, lived and spread through speech, song and memory, long before it was recorded in writing. Academic sources characterise it as among the oldest forms of oral literature in Malay civilisation. Second, the pantun is not merely a string of rhyming lines; it has a specific internal division between the "image" part and the "meaning" part.

The pantun appears in the earliest classical Malay texts. A study published in *BiblioAsia* (National Library Board, Singapore) records that the pantun is embedded in the *Sejarah Melayu* and the hikayat contemporary with it, and is even inserted into works such as the *Syair Ken Tambuhan* — a sign that the pantun was already mature as a form when Malay literature began to be set down in books.

## Structure: pembayang and maksud, abab rhyme

The most common form of the pantun is a four-line stanza with an **abab** rhyme scheme — the end sound of the first line matches the third, and the second line matches the fourth. UNESCO itself, in its description of the pantun, mentions the a-b-a-b rhyme scheme and the four-line form as the most widespread.

The four-line stanza is divided into two parts:

- **Pembayang** (also known as **sampiran**) — the first two lines. It typically builds an image drawn from the surrounding natural world: fruit, birds, the sea, hills, the weather. This part serves as an opening before the meaning is delivered.
- **Maksud** — the next two lines. This is the part that conveys the actual message, feeling or purpose of the pantun.

According to the analysis of the ISEAS – Yusof Ishak Institute, each line of a pantun typically contains about eight to twelve syllables and four to six words. The relationship between the pembayang and the maksud, meanwhile, is a subject of scholarly discussion: some hold that the image of the pembayang foreshadows the meaning, and some insist that the strongest bond between the two parts is a bond of *sound* — rhyme, alliteration, assonance and repetition — and not necessarily literal meaning. In the "pisang emas" example above, for instance, the main link between the pembayang and the maksud is the rhyme *belayar–dibayar* and *peti–mati*.

## Types of pantun

Although the four-line pantun (empat kerat) is the best known, the pantun form actually ranges widely — from the simplest to the long and chained. The table below summarises some main types by number of lines and arrangement:

| Type of pantun | Features |
| --- | --- |
| Two-line pantun (dua kerat) | A two-line stanza; one line of pembayang, one line of maksud — the simplest form |
| Four-line pantun (empat kerat) | A four-line stanza, abab rhyme; two lines of pembayang, two lines of maksud — the most common form |
| Long multi-stanza pantun | A stanza of six, eight lines or more, with expanded pembayang and maksud parts |
| Linked pantun (berkait) | Several chained stanzas; the lines of one stanza are repeated and carried into the next so that a continuous series is interwoven |

The linked pantun shows clearly how flexible this form is: not a single self-standing stanza, but a chain of interlinked stanzas, allowing the poet to build a longer idea or story without leaving the basic mould of the pantun.

## Functions in culture and custom

The pantun is not merely a linguistic ornament; it is a social tool that lives in the everyday life of Malay society. UNESCO, in its inscription, characterises the pantun as a subtle and polite way of saying something *indirectly* — allowing a person to convey a difficult or sensitive matter without being blunt, and even used to defuse disputes diplomatically.

Among its main functions:

- **Courtship and ceremonies** — the old pantun was used in the customs of merisik (discreet enquiry), proposal, betrothal and wedding ceremonies, and the practice of exchanging pantun between two families' sides continues to this day.
- **Advice and teaching** — many pantun carry values such as respect, moderation, patience and courtesy, and convey admonition and satire while still maintaining politeness.
- **Recorder of life** — the pantun records everyday life, history, legend and custom, and thereby becomes a sociocultural reference source of the Malay community, as discussed in the *BiblioAsia* study.
- **Public communication** — the pantun is also used in official speeches, cultural events and political discourse to this day.

In ceremonies, the pantun is typically delivered by a *pemantun* (pantun reciter), sometimes accompanied by song and music. The bond of the pantun with [Malay literature](/en/arts-culture/malay-literature) and with [the Malay language](/en/arts-culture/bahasa-melayu) itself makes it not merely a literary genre, but part of the way the Malays speak and observe custom.

## UNESCO recognition 2020

At the 15th session of the Intergovernmental Committee for the Safeguarding of the Intangible Cultural Heritage, held in Paris in December 2020, UNESCO inscribed the **Pantun** on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. The inscription (decision 15.COM 8.b.30, nomination No. 01613) was the result of a **joint nomination by Malaysia and Indonesia**, recognising the pantun as a shared Malay heritage across borders.

In that decision, UNESCO characterised the pantun as "the most widespread oral open form in maritime Southeast Asia", which has been used in the region for at least 500 years. It is seen not only as a literary form, but as a living practice inherited through music, song and writing, taught formally in schools and art workshops, and informally through sessions and contests of exchanging pantun.

This recognition was later extended: according to UNESCO's records, in 2025 an expanded inscription was adopted to also encompass **Brunei Darussalam**, with the new inscription merging and replacing the original "Pantun" inscription of 2020.

## The pantun today

The pantun is far from a mere historical relic. It continues to be taught in the school curriculum as part of the content of Malay language arts and literature, practised through pantun-exchange contests, and recited at official ceremonies, weddings and cultural events. Expressions such as "hutang budi dibawa mati" (a debt of kindness is carried to the grave) have already become ingrained in everyday speech, often without the speaker realising that it is quoting a pantun.

The flexibility of the pantun form — simple for two lines, long and chained for the linked pantun — makes it easy for it to live in new media, from the speeches of leaders to social media. From the four-line stanza that is easy to memorise to the UNESCO recognition that places it alongside world heritage, the pantun remains one of the most distinctive Malay contributions to the oral treasury of humanity.
