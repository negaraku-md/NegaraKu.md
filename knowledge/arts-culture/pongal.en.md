In the early dawn as the month of Thai begins, the courtyards and doorsteps of
Tamil families across Malaysia are abuzz. In the middle of a freshly drawn
kolam, milk boils in a clay pot set on a stove. At the moment it boils up and
spills past the rim of the pot, everyone in the household gathers and calls out
'Pongalo Pongal'. That boiling over is no accident; it is the symbol of a
year's abundance and gratitude brimming over.

Thai Pongal is the Tamil **harvest and thanksgiving festival**. It is a seasonal
celebration giving thanks to nature, to the Sun that gave the harvest, and to
the cattle that helped with the ploughing. This article describes the practices
of the festival and how it is celebrated in Malaysia in a neutral way — it does
not explain or evaluate the religious meaning of any ritual.

## What Thai Pongal Is

The word "Pongal" means "to boil over", that is, to rise up and spill. The
festival begins on the first day of the month of **Thai** in the Tamil calendar
— usually **14 or 15 January** in the Gregorian calendar. It marks the Sun's
northward journey (Uttarayana); a joyful season after the harvest is done.

Thai Pongal is primarily a **cultural festival** — it is also called the Tamils'
festival day (Tamizhar Thirunal). More than any devotional belief, its central
idea is giving thanks for the abundance of the harvest and the bounty of
nature. That is what sets it apart from other festivals such as Deepavali.

## A Four-Day Festival

Thai Pongal is not a one-day festival; by tradition it is a four-day
celebration. Each day has its own name and meaning:

| Day | Tamil name | Core idea |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Day one | Bhogi (போகி) | Clearing out the old, cleaning the house, and making way for the new |
| Day two | Thai Pongal / Surya Pongal (சூரிய பொங்கல்) | Boiling the new rice and offering it in thanks to the Sun |
| Day three | Mattu Pongal (மாட்டுப் பொங்கல்) | Bathing, decorating and honouring the cattle that help with ploughing |
| Day four | Kaanum Pongal (காணும் பொங்கல்) | Visiting relatives and going out to enjoy the day |

**Bhogi** is the day of renewal — houses are cleaned and old things are
discarded. **Thai Pongal**, or Surya Pongal, is the main day; it is on this day
that pongal is cooked and offered to the Sun. On **Mattu Pongal**, the horns of
the cattle are painted and they are decorated with garlands. On the final day,
**Kaanum Pongal**, families gather and renew their ties.

## Cooking the Pongal: The Moment the Pot Boils Over

The heart of the festival is the **cooking of the pongal** itself. Newly
harvested rice is boiled with milk (often with jaggery added) in a **clay pot**
— in many homes out in the open, in the sunlight. It is at the moment the milk
boils up and spills over the pot that the cry of 'Pongalo Pongal' rises; that
boiling over is regarded as a symbol of swelling abundance and prosperity.

Some of the symbols bound up with the festival:

- **Sugarcane** — sweetness and prosperity. On Pongal day, selling and sharing
  pieces of sugarcane is widespread.
- **Turmeric** — a bunch of turmeric is tied around the clay pot; a symbol of
  auspiciousness.
- **Kolam** — patterns drawn with rice flour at the doorstep; a sign of welcome
  and of abundance.
- **Mango and banana leaves** — for the doorway festoons and as the leaf on
  which pongal is offered.

In Malaysia, it is precisely to buy these clay pots, sugarcane, milk, jaggery
and mango leaves that Tamil families come to markets such as Little India during
the Pongal season.

## Thai Pongal in Malaysia

In Malaysia's Tamil community, Thai Pongal is an important cultural event. In
Tamil-dense areas such as **Brickfields (Little India)** in Kuala Lumpur,
**Klang** and **Penang**, the market crowds swell as the Pongal season draws
near. In the days before the festival, Brickfields is full of stalls selling
clay pots, sugarcane, mango leaves, flowers and fruit; the crowds surge late
into the night.

Besides cooking pongal at home, public celebrations are also held — community
organisations and temples cook pongal together in many pots at once and hold the
festival with cultural performances. At some temples, decorated cattle also
feature in the day's celebration.

**Holiday status:** Thai Pongal is **not a gazetted public holiday at the
federal level or in any state** in Malaysia. Among Malaysia's Hindu / Indian
festivals, only **Deepavali** is a federal public holiday (except in Sarawak),
while **Thaipusam** is a holiday only in some states. In 2020, a Malaysian
minister clarified that Pongal is not a religious festival, but a harvest-season
celebration and an event for communal unity.

## Common Misconceptions

- **"Thai Pongal is a public holiday in Malaysia."** No — it is not a gazetted
  holiday at the federal or state level. (Deepavali and Thaipusam are different;
  those are holidays.)
- **"Thai Pongal and Deepavali are the same."** No — they are different
  festivals; Deepavali is the festival of lights, Thai Pongal is the harvest
  festival.
- **"Pongal is just one day."** By tradition it is a four-day festival; in
  cities and diaspora communities some shorten it to one or two days.
- **"It is a religious festival."** It is primarily a harvest-based cultural /
  seasonal festival; some families visiting a temple is a personal custom.
