Walk into an old townhouse on Melaka's Heeren Street and the story is told in objects before anyone says a word. A tightly fitted lace blouse pinned at the front with a three-piece gold brooch. A pair of slippers whose toes are covered in thousands of tiny glass beads, each stitched by hand. A long marble-topped table set for a meal that is neither quite Chinese nor quite Malay. These belong to the Peranakans — the Baba-Nyonya — a community that made a culture out of living between two worlds.

## Who the Peranakans are

The word *Peranakan* comes from the Malay *anak*, meaning child, and describes people who are **locally born**. A man of the community is called a *Baba*; a woman is called a *Nyonya* (sometimes spelled Nonya). The Baba-Nyonya are the **Chinese Peranakans**: descendants of Chinese men who settled in the trading ports of the Malay Archipelago, married local women, and raised generations that absorbed the language, food and customs around them while keeping much of their Chinese ancestry.

Historically the community clustered in the ports that became the British **Straits Settlements** — Melaka, Penang (George Town) and Singapore — and for that reason they are also known as the **Straits Chinese** or Straits-born Chinese. Precisely when the community first formed is debated, and popular accounts that fix it to a single voyage or year should be treated with caution; what is clear is that it emerged from centuries of intermarriage and settlement in Melaka and grew into a recognisable culture under the Straits Settlements.

"Peranakan" is an umbrella term rather than a single group. Alongside the Chinese Baba-Nyonya, the region is home to other locally rooted communities such as the **Chitty** (Peranakan of Indian, chiefly Tamil, descent in Melaka) and the **Jawi Peranakan** (of mixed Indian-Muslim and Malay descent). This article focuses on the Chinese Peranakans, who are the group most people mean by "Baba-Nyonya".

## A language of their own

One of the clearest markers of the community is **Baba Malay**, a creole built on a Malay grammatical base and threaded through with vocabulary borrowed largely from Hokkien Chinese. It is a spoken home language rather than a literary one, and the fact that a community of Chinese descent came to speak a form of Malay as its mother tongue tells you how deeply the two cultures merged.

## Nyonya cuisine

If any part of Peranakan culture is famous beyond the community, it is the food. **Nyonya cuisine** takes Chinese ingredients and techniques and marries them to the Malay pantry — coconut milk, tamarind, lemongrass, galangal, candlenut, belacan (fermented shrimp paste) and fresh herbs — producing dishes that are recognisably neither wholly Chinese nor wholly Malay.

The kitchen was traditionally the domain of the Nyonya, and cooking skill was a point of pride passed from mother to daughter. Meals of celebration were served *tok panjang* style — the phrase means "long table" — a communal spread laid out for guests to share. The cuisine differs by city, too: Penang Nyonya food leans more sharply sour and spicy, reflecting Thai influence to the north, while Melaka's tends to be richer and more coconut-forward.

## Dress and the decorative arts

Peranakan women are most associated with two garments. The older is the **baju panjang**, a long loose tunic worn over a batik sarong. The more iconic is the **Nyonya kebaya**: a fitted, sheer blouse, often finely embroidered along its edges with floral motifs, fastened at the front by a set of linked brooches called a *kerongsang*, and paired with a batik sarong. On the feet come *kasut manek* — slippers whose uppers are covered in intricate beadwork, a craft in which Nyonyas demonstrated their patience and skill. Peranakan men traditionally wore Chinese-style dress such as the *baju lok chuan*.

The kebaya is shared across Southeast Asia, and in December 2024 UNESCO inscribed "Kebaya: knowledge, skills, traditions and practices" on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, in a joint nomination by Brunei Darussalam, Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore and Thailand — a recognition of a garment central to Nyonya identity as well as to the wider region.

Beyond dress, the community developed a distinctive material world of its own:

| Element | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| Nyonyaware | Brightly coloured famille-rose porcelain — pinks, greens, yellows — commissioned from Chinese kilns for Peranakan households |
| Kasut manek | Hand-beaded slippers, sometimes taking weeks to complete a single pair |
| Kerongsang | Linked brooch set used to fasten the kebaya at the front |
| Straits furniture | Blackwood and mother-of-pearl inlaid furniture blending Chinese, European and Malay forms |

## Houses, shophouses and heritage

Peranakan wealth and taste are written into the architecture of Melaka and George Town. The community favoured **Straits-eclectic townhouses and shophouses** — deep, narrow buildings whose facades mixed Chinese decorative elements with European columns, ceramic tiles and shuttered windows. Two survive as museums built around real family homes: the **Baba & Nyonya Heritage Museum** at 48 and 50 Jalan Tun Tan Cheng Lock (the old Heeren Street) in Melaka, and the **Pinang Peranakan Mansion** in George Town.

These streetscapes are part of why **Melaka and George Town, Historic Cities of the Straits of Malacca** were together inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List on **7 July 2008**, under cultural criteria (ii), (iii) and (iv). The listing recognises towns shaped by almost 500 years of trade between East and West, and a "multi-cultural living heritage" that is both tangible and intangible. The Baba-Nyonya are one strand of that heritage, alongside the Malay, Indian, and European-descended communities of the two cities. You can read more about the broader listing in [Malaysia's UNESCO World Heritage sites](/en/arts-culture/unesco-world-heritage), and about the [batik](/en/arts-culture/batik) sarong that anchors the Nyonya kebaya.

Malaysia also protects heritage more broadly through the **National Heritage Act 2005**, administered by the Jabatan Warisan Negara (Department of National Heritage), which maintains a register of tangible and intangible cultural heritage.

## A living culture, not a museum piece

It would be easy to file the Peranakans under "history", but the culture is still lived. Nyonya kitchens still cook, family kebayas are still worn at weddings, and Peranakan associations in Melaka, Penang and beyond keep the language, recipes and crafts in circulation. State tourism campaigns in Melaka have even encouraged visitors to don the kebaya at heritage sites. The community is small, and much of its distinctiveness — the beadwork, the porcelain, the spoken Baba Malay — depends on being actively passed on rather than merely displayed.
