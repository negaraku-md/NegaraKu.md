In Ujong Pasir, Melaka, there is a seaside settlement where people greet one another in a language that is neither Malay, nor English, nor even modern Portuguese — but rather the living remnant of the tongue of the conquerors who arrived five centuries ago. This is the world of the Kristang community.

## Who are the Kristang?

The Kristang are a Eurasian community of mixed Portuguese and local ancestry (mostly Malay, and later Indian and others). The name "Kristang" comes from the Portuguese word for "Christian" — fitting for the Roman Catholic faith that remains the pillar of their identity to this day.

The roots of this community trace back to the year **1511**, when Afonso de Albuquerque conquered Melaka and placed it under Portuguese rule. To strengthen the settlement, the Portuguese encouraged intermarriage, including through the *Orfas del Rei* programme that brought Portuguese orphan girls to be married to the settlers. From these marriages a new community was born — the Kristang — who remained rooted in Melaka even after Portuguese power fell to the Dutch in 1641.

Today they are often referred to as **Eurasians**. They share some similarities with the [Baba-Nyonya Peranakan](/en/arts-culture/peranakan) community of Melaka — both born from the cultural intermingling of that old port city — but Kristang identity is shaped by its Portuguese heritage and Catholic faith.

## What is the Portuguese Settlement in Melaka?

By the early 20th century, the Portuguese descendants of Melaka had become scattered and many were poor. To give them a place to gather, two priests and a mission representative acquired roughly **28 acres** of seaside marshland at the foot of **St John's Hill** from the British colonial government in the mid-1920s.

The marshland was reclaimed and wooden houses were built. According to records, by the early 1930s the first ten houses had been completed, and the number grew to around **60** by the end of that decade. Because most of the residents were fishermen, the seaside location was crucial. This Portuguese Settlement at Ujong Pasir has remained the cultural heart of the Kristang community ever since, complete with a square (Medan Portugis), a church, and seafood stalls.

## Why is the Papia Kristang language endangered?

The community's most distinctive heritage is its language, **Papia Kristang** (literally "to speak Kristang"). It is a **creole based on old Portuguese**, built upon 16th-century Portuguese vocabulary but absorbing Malay grammatical structure and vocabulary, alongside other influences in the region. The result is a unique tongue — Portuguese enough to be recognisable, yet Malay enough to sound local.

But this language is fading. Papia Kristang has been **listed as an endangered language in UNESCO's Atlas of the World's Languages in Danger since the 1980s**. Researchers describe it as critically endangered; in Singapore alone, an estimated **fewer than 100 fluent speakers** remain. Professor Stefanie Sharmila Pillai of Universiti Malaya warns that the language faces extinction due to a low rate of transmission — younger generations tend to switch to English, and Kristang is rarely spoken at home anymore.

## How is Kristang culture celebrated?

If the language grows quiet, its music and dance remain lively. The most famous performance is the **branyo**, a couples' dance derived from the Portuguese folk dance *corridinho*. It is usually danced to the classic song **"Jinkli Nona"** and accompanied by accordion and guitar, sometimes together with the Malay rebana — another sign of cultural blending. Heritage scholars note that the branyo follows several traditional rhythmic patterns such as *jingkli nona*, *kanji-pape*, *sarampeh* and *chorte forte*.

There are various variations. **Branyo Sinta** was once danced for three days throughout Intrudu but is no longer practised, while **Branyo Rudia** — the "roaming" version — circles the settlement from around 7 pm to 8.30 pm. There is also the more energetic **malhao** dance: dancers form a circle while clapping and spinning to the song "O Malhao Malhao", a dance that originated in the Estremadura region north of Lisbon.

The community's calendar revolves around the Catholic and the maritime:

| Festival | When | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Intrudu | Sunday before Ash Wednesday | Marks the beginning of Lent; a water festival where participants splash water on one another |
| Festa San Pedro | June | Honours Saint Peter, patron of fishermen |
| Festa San Juan (San Juang) | June | Honours Saint John; accompanied by branyo |
| Christmas | December | The largest Catholic celebration, with traditional food and decorations |

When dancing, the dancers wear costumes resembling Portuguese folk attire — the men in black bolero jackets and hats, while the women wear colourful embroidered skirts.

## How are this language and culture being preserved?

Because the threat of extinction is real, several preservation efforts have been set in motion. In Singapore, the community initiative **Kodrah Kristang** ("Awaken, Kristang") launched in **March 2016** has revived interest in the language. Its free classes drew **more than 400 students**, and **1,400 people** attended the inaugural Kristang Language Festival in **May 2017**. The initiative also developed the first structured curriculum for the language.

In Melaka, the continuity of the culture depends heavily on community arts groups. For example, **Tropa de Santa Maria** was founded in **2012** by Sara Frederica Santa Maria, a fourth-generation resident of the Portuguese Settlement. The performing-arts heritage body **PUSAKA** also collaborates with figures such as Gerard De Costa and the 1511 O Maliao Maliao dance troupe to preserve the branyo and traditional songs.

All these efforts share a common premise: a language and culture live because they are used and passed on, not because they are stored in a museum.
