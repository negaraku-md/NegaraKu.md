Step into any Chinese temple in Malaysia and you will likely find Buddhist, Taoist
and folk deities sharing a single space: amid the drifting incense there is
Guanyin, and also Mazu, and the strongly local Tua Pek Kong. This juxtaposition is
not chaos but the truest face of Malaysian Chinese belief — **syncretism**.

Precisely because it is syncretic, this kind of belief is hard to box in
statistically. To understand it, one must begin with a statistical blank.

## A statistical blank

Malaysia's census **does** include a category for Confucianism, Taoism and other
traditional Chinese religion — but it records a **negligible number**, folded into
"other religions" (about 0.9%). Because most practitioners instead report
themselves as **Buddhists**, they are largely absorbed into the "18.7% Buddhist"
figure. The result is that there is **no separate official count** reflecting the
true numbers of Chinese folk religion and Taoism.

This is also why, in discussing Malaysian Chinese belief, Buddhism and folk
religion are often hard to separate cleanly. For the three main traditions of
Buddhism itself, see [Buddhism in Malaysia](/en/arts-culture/buddhism-in-malaysia).

## A syncretic belief

For many Chinese families, Buddhism, **Taoism**, Confucian thought and **ancestor
veneration** are not mutually exclusive choices but a set of practices carried on
at the same time. One person may burn incense on the first and fifteenth of the
lunar month, sweep the graves at Qingming, and visit a temple to honour the Buddha
on Vesak Day. This blending is the core feature of Chinese folk religion.

## Deities

Malaysian Chinese temples enshrine many deities; common ones include:

- **Tua Pek Kong** — a deity with strongly local character; the oldest Tua Pek
  Kong temple is said to be at Tanjung Tokong in Penang, with the belief later
  spreading across Malaysia, Singapore and Indonesia.
- **Mazu (the Heavenly Empress)** — a deity of the sea and of protection,
  especially revered in coastal Chinese communities.
- **Guanyin (Avalokiteshvara)** — a symbol of compassion, spanning both Buddhism
  and folk religion.
- **The Jade Emperor** and **Guan Gong (Lord Guan)**, among others, are also
  widely venerated.

## Festivals

- **Chinese New Year** — beyond its cultural meaning, it also carries religious
  elements such as ancestor veneration and prayers for blessings; see
  [Chinese New Year](/en/arts-culture/chinese-new-year).
- **The Nine Emperor Gods Festival** — the celebration of the Nine Emperor Gods
  cult, best known for the temple fairs at Ampang in Kuala Lumpur and in Penang.
- **The Hungry Ghost Festival (Yulan)** — commonly called the "Ghost Festival,"
  when Chinese communities make offerings and perform rites of deliverance in the
  seventh lunar month.
- **Qingming** — the season for sweeping graves and honouring ancestors; the
  **Mid-Autumn Festival** carries both cultural and folk significance.

## Temples and organisations

Temples are called **tokong** in Malay. Well-known ones include **Cheng Hoon
Teng** in Malacca and the **Thean Hou Temple** in Kuala Lumpur, reflecting the
religious life of early Chinese immigrants. At the organisational level, bodies
such as the **Federation of Taoist Associations Malaysia (FTAM; established around
1994)** represent Taoist organisations, promoting liaison and standard-setting work; most temples, however,
remain independently registered and managed.

## Common misconceptions

- **"All Chinese are Buddhists."** Many Chinese practices in fact blend Buddhism,
  Taoism and folk religion; the census mostly counts them as Buddhist, but this
  does not equal Buddhism in the strict sense.
- **"Taoism and Buddhism are opposed."** In the daily practice of Malaysian
  Chinese, the two often coexist without conflict.
- **"Religion equals ethnic group."** Religion and ethnicity are two different
  things; the census measures each separately.
