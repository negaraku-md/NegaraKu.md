In an old village on Jalan Gajah Berang, Melaka, there is a cluster of families who pray to the god Shiva, dress like Malays, cook curries fragrant with coconut milk, and speak a Malay laced with Tamil words. They are not Indian, not Malay, not Chinese — they are the Chetti Melaka, and their numbers are growing ever smaller.

## Who are the Chetti Melaka?

The Chetti Melaka — also spelled **Chitty** — are a **Peranakan Indian** community. Their ancestors were **Tamil** traders from southern India who came to trade and settled in Melaka around the era of the **Melaka Malay Sultanate** (15th to 16th centuries). Because most of those traders came without their families, they married local women of **Malay and Chinese** descent, giving rise to a hybrid community that endured through the Portuguese, Dutch and British colonial periods.

The word "Chetti" or "Chitty" comes from Tamil and means *trader* — a reminder of their origins as a merchant class. Like the Chinese Peranakan community (Baba-Nyonya), the Chetti Melaka absorbed many local elements while still preserving the core of their ancestral identity.

## What sets them apart?

The most striking thing is the combination of the **Hindu religion with a Malay way of life**. The Chetti Melaka remained **Saivite** Hindus, that is, worshippers of the **god Shiva**, while their everyday dress, food and language are largely Malay.

| Element | Chetti Melaka trait |
| --- | --- |
| Religion | Saivite Hindu (worshippers of the god Shiva) |
| Language | A creole blending Bazaar Malay, Tamil and Chinese |
| Dress & cuisine | Malay-based, with a touch of Indian spice |
| Settlement | Kampung Chetti, Jalan Gajah Berang, Melaka |

Their language is fascinating: a **creole** that researchers describe as a blend of Bazaar Malay, Tamil and Chinese — for example *nenek* (Malay) for grandmother, but *thatha* (Tamil) for grandfather. Ironically, **Tamil** itself is now barely spoken in daily life; it survives mainly in religious ceremonies.

## What are their main festivals?

The Chetti Melaka celebrate Hindu festivals such as **Deepavali**, **Bhogi** and **Pongal**. Their biggest annual festival is **Sembahyang Dato Chachar**, also known as **Megammay Thiruvizha** — the veneration of the goddess **Mariamman**. This roughly week-long festival is centred on the **Sri Muthu Mariamman Temple** in Gajah Berang and draws thousands of devotees; according to *The Star*'s coverage in 2012, it took place around the month of **April**. (The actual date shifts each year with the Tamil calendar.)

## Why is their heritage endangered?

This is the most worrying part. According to Singapore's National Heritage Board (Roots), the Chetti Melaka community in Melaka shrank from around **400 families in the 1980s to fewer than 50 families** today. A *Free Malaysia Today* report, meanwhile, estimates that only around **20 to 30 families** still live in Kampung Chetti — a lower figure, but both sources describe a community growing ever smaller. In addition, an estimated **5,000** people of Chetti descent are thought to live in Singapore.

Researchers link this decline to **intermarriage** outside the community, **out-migration** in search of work, and younger generations increasingly **losing touch with their Chetti roots**. Because the community is small and little known, some have called it "the lost tribe" — a culture facing the risk of vanishing.

There are efforts to preserve it. According to *Free Malaysia Today*, Kampung Chetti was granted protection as a **heritage zone in June 2000**, on a par with the Portuguese Settlement and Kampung Morten in Melaka. (The cited source does not state the exact legal basis for that protection, which predates the National Heritage Act 2005.) A small museum at the village entrance is reported to display artefacts as well as old photographs of the community.
