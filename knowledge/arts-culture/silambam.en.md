At a temple festival or a Tamil New Year event in Malaysia, you may see a young man twirling a long bamboo staff above his head and around his body at lightning speed, in time with the beat of the drums. That spinning is not merely for show — it is the hallmark of silambam, a Tamil martial art more than one and a half thousand years old.

Silambam is **a martial art that originated in Tamil Nadu and uses the bamboo staff as its primary weapon**. It came to Malaysia with Tamil migration and continues today as a martial art, a sport and a cultural performance. This article describes, in a neutral way, the art's origins, its techniques, and its place within Malaysia's Tamil community.

## History and Origins

Silambam is an ancient weapon art that developed in Tamil Nadu. On the basis of references in Sangam-era literature, scholars consider that it has been practised **at least since the fourth century BCE**; traces of it appear in works including the Silappadikaram. Some extend its antiquity to as much as three thousand years — so its exact age is not firmly established.

As for the name, it is generally said to be connected to the Tamil word **silambu**, meaning "mountain"; it is said that the silambu bamboo grown in the Kurinji hill country gave its name to the art of the staff. There is also a folk usage that links the name to the sound made by the spinning staff and to the sound of the ankle bells (silambu).

Historical accounts say that in the eighteenth-century struggles against the British East India Company, figures such as Puli Thevar, Dheeran Chinnamalai and the Marudu brothers made use of their silambam skill. Colonial rule later banned the art; as firearm-based training was promoted, silambam began to decline.

## Weapons and Techniques

At the heart of silambam is the **long bamboo staff** — cut to a length suited to the practitioner's height. The techniques of twirling the staff above the head, around the body and shifting it between both hands (staff-twirling) are the art's signature sight. After the basic staff training, the techniques extend to a range of weapons:

| Weapon | Description |
| --- | --- |
| Long staff | The primary weapon, made of bamboo; length suited to the practitioner's height |
| Maru / madu | A double-pointed thrusting weapon made from deer horn |
| Vaal (sword) | Usually a curved sword |
| Savuku (whip) | A whirling lash |
| Katti / aruval (knife / sickle) | Short cutting weapons |
| Urumi | A flexible metal sword |

All of these weapon trainings are based on the movements and rhythm learned in staff practice.

## Footwork and Adavu

In silambam it is said that **footwork**, rather than weapon skill, is the foundation. The designed movements that shift the feet forward and back, sideways and in rotation are called **adavu** (or kaaladi). These develop balance, speed and the ability to move around an opponent; it is the combination of the staff's spin and the movement of the feet that gives silambam its flow.

The part fought bare-handed, without a weapon, is called **kuttu varisai**; it includes forms based on the movements of animals. Early-stage training focuses on strengthening flexibility, agility, hand-eye coordination and stamina.

## How Silambam Came to Malaysia

Today's Malaysian silambam tradition is largely bound up with Tamil migration during the **British colonial period**. In the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, labourers who came from South India — mainly from Tamil Nadu — to work on rubber plantations and in other jobs brought the art of silambam with them, just as they did their language and culture.

In the isolated plantation communities, silambam continued as physical exercise, as a method of self-defence and as a performance at temple festivals. It is precisely because of these community roots that silambam still has strong community support in Malaysia today. To learn more about this background, see [Tamils in Malaysia](/en/arts-culture/tamil-in-malaysia) and [the estate Tamil community](/en/arts-culture/estate-tamil-community).

## In Malaysia Today: Art, Sport, Self-defence

Today in Malaysia silambam has a **triple identity** — martial art, competitive sport and cultural performance.

- **As a cultural performance:** silambam displays are a regular part of events such as Tamil New Year, temple festivals and community celebrations. It is promoted as a way of connecting young people with their roots.
- **As a competitive sport:** national silambam competitions are held in Malaysia; it has featured in the Malaysia Games (SUKMA) in some years (for example as a medal event in 2010, 2011 and 2013; as a demonstration event in 2004, 2008 and 2022), though its status has varied from year to year. Malaysian athletes also take part at the international level — the international championship held on a Penang university campus in 2026, with eleven countries taking part, is one example.
- **As a martial art:** it is taught through school and youth training programmes, workshops and weekly training under individual teachers; it is also seen as training that builds self-confidence and discipline.

There are several **associations** that coordinate silambam in Malaysia (for example, Persatuan Silambam Malaysia, and separate bodies connected to silambam forms). The relationships between these bodies, and the status of their sporting-administrative recognition, have changed from time to time; so it is best to verify the current official position against the sources of the relevant period.

## Common Misconceptions

- **"Silambam and silat are the same."** No. Silambam is a Tamil art that originated in Tamil Nadu; silat is a different martial art of the Malay / Nusantara tradition. Although both exist in Malaysia, they have separate histories and techniques.
- **"Silambam is merely a stage art."** Silambam is not only a cultural performance but also a martial art with defined techniques and a competitive sport.
- **"Silambam means only the staff."** Although the staff is the primary weapon, the art also includes many weapons such as the maru, sword, knife and urumi, the footwork known as adavu, and the bare-hand kuttu varisai.
