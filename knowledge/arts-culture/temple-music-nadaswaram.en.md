At the front of a Thaipusam procession, at the moment a temple chariot sets out,
or at the entrance to a wedding hall — at any Malaysian Tamil Hindu occasion, a
particular sound is the first to fill the air: the long melody of the nadaswaram
and the boom of the thavil. That sound is no accident — it is the mark of the
**mangala vadyam** (auspicious-instrument) tradition that has travelled with
this community for well over a century.

This article describes, in a neutral way, these musical traditions that sound in
Malaysian temples and festivals — the **nadaswaram–thavil** pair and the street
**urumi melam**. It does not try to explain or evaluate the religious meaning of
worship; it only notes the instruments, the settings in which they are used, and
how the tradition is sustained.

## The Nadaswaram and the Thavil: Mangala Vadyam

The **nadaswaram** is a large **double-reed wind instrument** of the South Indian
tradition — with a long wooden body and a flared bell. It is one of the loudest
of wind instruments, which is why it suits open-air processions without
amplification and large temple halls.

Played alongside it is the **thavil** — a **barrel-shaped hand drum** headed with
skin on both sides. The nadaswaram plays the melody while the thavil provides the
rhythm. In the South Indian tradition the two are regarded as an inseparable
pair — the **mangala vadyam** (auspicious instruments). A group usually features
two nadaswaram players and one thavil player.

## In Temple Rituals and Processions

In Malaysian Tamil Hindu temples the nadaswaram–thavil pair sounds in a variety
of settings:

- **Daily worship and special pujas** — in some temples it is traditional for the
  music to sound from the early-morning puja through to the evening worship.
- **Chariot festivals** — as the chariot travels the streets, the ensembles play
  all along the route, completing the festive atmosphere.
- **[Thaipusam](/en/arts-culture/thaipusam) processions** — reports note that as
  the chariot of Kuala Lumpur's Sri Mahamariamman Temple travels to Batu Caves
  and back, many nadaswaram–thavil ensembles play along the way. In Penang's
  golden and silver chariot processions too, traditional Indian music including
  the thavil and nadaswaram echoes through the streets.

## At Weddings

At auspicious occasions such as weddings, the nadaswaram–thavil pair also takes a
central place. It is traditional for the ensemble to arrive and begin playing
even before the ceremony starts; particular melodies sound at the welcoming of
the couple and at key ritual moments. The very name "mangala vadyam" shows how
closely these instruments are bound up with auspicious occasions.

## Urumi Melam: Street Folk Percussion

Beyond the classical character of the nadaswaram, another tradition that is very
widespread in Malaysia is the **urumi melam** — loud, pulsating street folk
percussion music. It comprises three main instruments, often with the thalam
added as well:

| Instrument | Type / character |
| --- | --- |
| Urumi | A double-headed drum played with curved sticks; a distinctive sound |
| Thavil | A barrel-shaped hand drum; fast rhythm |
| Pambai | A double cylindrical drum |
| Thalam | Hand cymbals (often added) |

At [Thaipusam](/en/arts-culture/thaipusam) the urumi melam takes a central
place: **kavadi-bearers** walk and dance to its fast rhythm to fulfil their vows.
As the musicians sing devotional songs and sound the drums, the kavadi dance
comes alive. This is why the urumi melam is described by many as the pulse of the
Thaipusam atmosphere.

According to one instructor, in the **Malaysian style** the urumi, thavil and
pambai are all played together as a single urumi melam ensemble; in Tamil Nadu
these instruments are generally used for separate occasions, and it is the
nadaswaram–thavil pair that sounds most at kavadi processions, it is noted.

## How the Tradition Is Sustained in Malaysia

Many parties carry these musical traditions forward:

- **Temple musicians and nadaswaram–thavil ensembles** — professional groups
  invited for temple rituals, chariot festivals and weddings.
- **Urumi melam groups** — numerous groups operate across the country; some have
  been running continuously for many years, such as since 1999.
- **Youth groups** — young people who begin learning at an early age are hugely
  popular at Thaipusam. Many groups reinvest their earnings into uniforms,
  instruments and group meals, operating as a non-profit service.

Today this music sounds beyond temples and festivals in settings such as
receptions and public celebrations too.

## Common Misconceptions

- **"The nadaswaram and the urumi melam are the same."** No. The nadaswaram–thavil
  pair is a classical-natured mangala vadyam; the urumi melam is a loud street
  folk percussion tradition of urumi, thavil and pambai. Although both share the
  thavil, they differ in sound and setting.
- **"The nadaswaram is stage classical music."** Although the nadaswaram also
  features in Carnatic music, what this article refers to is its temple-and-festival
  use. Stage [Indian classical arts](/en/arts-culture/indian-classical-arts-in-malaysia)
  — Bharatanatyam and Carnatic music — are described in a separate article.
- **"This music is the same in India and Malaysia."** Although the basics are
  shared, the Malaysian style of the urumi melam — in features such as playing
  all three instruments together — differs from the Tamil Nadu practice.
