Walk into Semenyih, Kampar or Sekinchan today and what you mostly see is the ordinary face of a small town: rows of shophouses, a Chinese primary school, temples and clan associations, roadside kopitiams and sundry shops. But many of these places share a common identity — they are "New Villages". Behind this seemingly plain label lies a history of the Malayan Emergency, counter-insurgency and mass relocation.

The Chinese New Villages are a specific type of settlement community in Malaysia. To understand them, you have to go back to the years from 1948 to 1960 known as the "Malayan Emergency". This article explains, in a descriptive and restrained way, how the New Villages came about, early life under control, and how they evolved into today's towns after the war — without passing political judgment on any party of that time.

## The History Behind a Name

The words "New Village" literally mean no more than "a new village", yet in practice they point to a resettlement system formed under specific historical conditions. These were not traditional villages that the Chinese settled spontaneously, but residential areas that the government deliberately demarcated, built and, for a time, strictly controlled in the 1950s. For that very reason, the distribution and form of the New Villages — and the land problems they face today — are all closely tied to this origin.

Today there are still several hundred communities across Malaysia known as "traditional New Villages", with residents mainly Chinese but also some Indians and people of other ethnicities. Most are now ordinary towns, but the name "New Village" has been retained, becoming a distinctive local identity.

## The Emergency and the Briggs Plan

In June 1948, the British colonial government declared a state of emergency in Malaya to counter the armed struggle launched by the Malayan Communist Party (MCP). Part of the MCP's supplies and intelligence came from residents scattered across the countryside and forest fringes, many of them living by small-scale farming and rubber tapping — among them many Chinese farming households without formal land titles.

In 1950, the British general Sir Harold Briggs, appointed Director of Operations, proposed a strategy that came to be known as the **Briggs Plan**. Its core method was to concentrate these scattered rural dwellers into government-designated resettlement areas, so as to sever their links with the MCP in food, intelligence and medicine. Those resettled were overwhelmingly Chinese; according to different sources the total was roughly 470,000 to 570,000, about a tenth of Malaya's population at the time. The Malayan Chinese Association (MCA), founded in that era, played a coordinating role in resettling and assisting the Chinese community.

It should be noted that the figures for the number resettled (roughly 470,000 to 570,000) and the number of New Villages (common figures include 450, 480 and 613) are not consistent across sources, the differences arising from different points in time and counting methods. This article sets out ranges to show that there is still room for discussion in quantifying this history.

## Inside the Fence: Life in the Early New Villages

The early New Villages were a far cry from today's towns. To achieve the aim of "isolation", many New Villages were ringed with barbed wire, with watchtowers and armed guards, and checkpoints at the entrances verifying identity. Most New Villages were under a curfew, and residents had to return inside the village within a set time. To prevent food and supplies from flowing out to help those beyond the village, some areas for a time practised communal cooking or strict food rationing.

At the same time, to win over the residents, the authorities progressively provided some New Villages with basic infrastructure such as electricity, piped water, schools and clinics. Some historians emphasise the coercion and hardship of the relocation process; other studies note that some New Villages thereby gained public services that the countryside had previously lacked. These two aspects coexisted, forming the complex historical picture of the early New Villages. This article merely states these facts and does not pass value judgments on any party.

## From Resettlement Camp to Town: The Post-War Evolution

After the Emergency ended in 1960, the controls — fences, curfews, food rationing and so on — were gradually removed, and the New Villages ceased to be controlled resettlement areas. Freed from the constraint of the fences, many New Villages drew on their existing population, commerce and community networks to grow slowly into ordinary towns.

A good number of New Villages are concentrated in **Selangor, Perak and Johor**. Because the residents moved in back then often clustered by their home-region dialect group, some New Villages retain a distinct dialect character to this day (see [the Chinese dialects of Malaysia](/en/arts-culture/chinese-dialects)). Chinese primary schools, temples and [clan associations](/en/arts-culture/chinese-clan-associations) are often the community core of a village, which also makes the New Villages a window onto [Chinese education](/en/education/chinese-education-in-malaysia) and Chinese community life in Malaysia.

## The New Villages Today: Land, Population and Issues

After several decades, the New Villages today face a different set of issues:

- **Land tenure.** The land status of the New Villages is not uniform: some plots are held on a 99-year leasehold title, some only on a **Temporary Occupation Licence (TOL)** that must be renewed periodically; those built on forest or river reserves may not even be able to apply for a title. Because land matters fall under state government jurisdiction, there is no uniform national standard. In recent years some parties and legislators have advocated issuing freehold titles to New Village residents, but the legal constraints under the National Land Code must also be considered.
- **Ageing population and youth out-migration.** Government surveys have noted that many New Villages generally face problems of young people moving to the cities, an ageing population and ageing infrastructure.
- **Identity.** For many families the New Village is the home of several generations, carrying memories of dialect, food and community.

## Heritage and Tourism Revitalisation

In recent years, the New Villages have come to be seen anew as a historical and cultural asset worth preserving. Bodies such as ICOMOS Malaysia have held seminars exploring the possibility of listing New Villages as national cultural heritage, or even as UNESCO World Heritage; because land rights belong to the state governments, any such listing must follow the National Heritage Act and be handled by the relevant departments.

At the community level, some New Villages have developed cultural tourism through murals, food trails and village-history narratives. Sekinchan in Selangor is a frequently cited example, drawing visitors with its paddy fields, fishery and rural scenery. Such revitalisation efforts turn the "New Village" from a weighty historical memory into a local story that can be told and visited.

## Common Misconceptions

**Thinking the New Villages are still "controlled zones" or detention facilities today.** This is not so. The controls — fences, curfews, food rationing and the like — were lifted long ago after the Emergency ended, and today's New Villages are ordinary settlement communities and towns.

**Equating the New Villages with ordinary traditional Chinese villages.** The New Villages were resettlement areas deliberately demarcated and built under the Briggs Plan, different from traditional villages that grew up through spontaneous settlement; this is also the origin of their special land status.

**Thinking everyone resettled was Chinese.** Those moved in were overwhelmingly Chinese (about 80% or more), but also included other ethnicities; moreover, during the Emergency some Orang Asli communities were also relocated, which is a separate chapter of history.

**Thinking there is a single exact figure for the number of people and villages.** The figures for the number resettled and the number of New Villages differ across sources (for example 470,000 to 570,000 people, 450 to 600 villages), and should be taken from academic and official studies.
