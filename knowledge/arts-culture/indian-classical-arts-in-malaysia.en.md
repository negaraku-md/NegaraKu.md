On a Deepavali evening or a temple-festival day in any of Malaysia's big cities, you can see a Bharatanatyam artist take the stage, ankle bells ringing, moving to the rhythm of the mridangam. Those steps and gestures are not random — they tell of a South Indian art tradition more than a thousand years old, and of the way the Malaysian Tamil community has carried it for over a century and a half.

Indian classical arts — chiefly the **Bharatanatyam** classical dance and **Carnatic music** — are stage arts largely sustained by the Tamil community in Malaysia. This article describes their forms, their teaching tradition and their institutions in a neutral way — without assessing or ranking the religious or aesthetic value of any art.

## What classical arts are

"Classical arts" are art forms with long-established traditions of training, grammar, and song-and-rhythm structure. In the Indian tradition they divide mostly into two broad branches: dance and music. Because people of South Indian Tamil descent form the majority in Malaysia, the South Indian forms — Bharatanatyam and Carnatic music — take precedence here.

These are not merely entertainment; they belong to the **guru-shishya** (teacher-disciple) tradition, in which a student trains formally under a guru for many years. Because they are interwoven with the language, culture and religious life of the Malaysian Indian community, learning and performing them is also seen as a way of preserving identity.

## Bharatanatyam and the arangetram tradition

Bharatanatyam is a classical dance form that arose in South India — its hallmarks are sculpted postures, precise footwork, hand gestures (mudras) and facial expression (abhinaya, the portrayal of emotion). It is the most widely learned Indian dance form in Malaysia.

A key milestone of this training is the **arangetram** — the first full-length solo stage recital a student performs after many years of training. The Tamil word means "ascending the stage"; only afterwards is the student considered qualified to perform solo and to teach others. In Malaysia it is a major occasion celebrated together by family, guru and community. For example, in 2022 five artists from the first graduating cohort of a Kuala Lumpur dance school held their arangetram — the students had been performing at temple festivals, Deepavali events and opening ceremonies as part of their training.

Dance schools (many refer to themselves as "sabha" or academy) are found across the country. Some schools' founders held their own arangetram in places such as Thanjavur in India before returning to Malaysia to start their schools.

## Carnatic music: voice and instruments

Carnatic music is the classical music tradition of South India. It is built on **raga** (melodic structure) and **tala** (rhythmic structure); although vocal music is its core, several solo instruments also take a leading place. In Malaysia, Carnatic vocal and instrumental music is taught and performed through dance schools, music academies and temple events.

| Instrument | Type | Role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Veena | Plucked string instrument | Solo / accompaniment to voice |
| Mridangam | Percussion instrument | Main rhythmic accompaniment |
| Violin | Bowed string instrument | Accompaniment to voice / solo |
| Flute | Wind instrument | Solo / accompaniment to voice |

At a Bharatanatyam performance, a Carnatic ensemble — vocalist, mridangam, violin and so on — often plays live; dance and music thus grow in dependence on each other.

## Other classical dance forms

Although South Indian forms predominate, other classical dances from the North and East Indian traditions also exist in Malaysia:

- **Odissi** — the tradition of Odisha state; known for its curved postures. In Malaysia it is especially well sustained by institutions such as the Sutra Foundation.
- **Kathak** — a North Indian tradition; known for fast spins and footwork.
- **Kuchipudi** — a dance-drama form from the Andhra tradition.

These are often taught through Indian cultural organisations too. For example, the **Netaji Subhash Chandra Bose Indian Cultural Centre** in Kuala Lumpur, operating under the High Commission of India, offers training in Kathak, Manipuri dance, Carnatic vocal music and more.

## Key institutions and venues

Some key institutions that have shaped Malaysia's classical arts scene:

- **Temple of Fine Arts (TFA)** — a non-profit organisation founded in 1981; with branches in Kuala Lumpur, Johor Bahru, Penang and Malacca. It teaches Bharatanatyam, Odissi, Kuchipudi and Kathak dance, Carnatic/Hindustani vocal music, and instruments such as the mridangam, veena, violin, sitar and tabla.
- **Sutra Foundation / Sutra Dance Theatre** — Datuk Ramli Ibrahim founded the Sutra Dance Theatre in Kuala Lumpur in 1983 and created the Sutra Foundation in 2007. It teaches and performs Odissi and Bharatanatyam (the Adyar K. Lakshman lineage). Ramli Ibrahim was awarded India's Padma Shri in 2018.
- **University groups** — at places such as Universiti Malaya, student performances linked to the Tamil Language Society / cultural centre serve as a stage for dance, music and drama.

Besides these, festivals such as **Deepavali** and **Thaipusam**, temple festivals and opening ceremonies serve as natural venues for young artists to perform.

## Preserving tradition and new learners

For the Tamil community, who lived in both estate communities and urban areas, learning and teaching these arts is a way of passing language and culture from generation to generation. Dance schools, music academies and temple events are the main channels of this transmission.

At the same time, these arts are not confined to the Indian community. Non-Indian Malaysians also learn Bharatanatyam, Odissi and the like — the fact that Malaysia's most renowned Odissi artist, Datuk Ramli Ibrahim, is a Malay is the notable example of this. By attracting multi-ethnic students in this way, these arts are also part of Malaysia's multicultural arts scene.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Indian classical arts = only Bharatanatyam."** Although Bharatanatyam is the most widespread, many forms such as Carnatic music, Odissi, Kathak and Kuchipudi also exist in Malaysia.
- **"Bharatanatyam and nadaswaram are the same."** No — Bharatanatyam is a dance form; nadaswaram is the instrumental music tradition of temple/auspicious events. The two are different.
- **"Only Indians can learn these."** No — students of every ethnicity and religion learn them.
- **"An arangetram is just a performance."** It is a milestone marking the completion of years of training — only afterwards is a student considered qualified to perform solo.
