During Navaratri, along one side wall of the reception room of a Malaysian Tamil Hindu home, steps are raised in an odd number. On these cloth-covered steps, deity figurines and dolls that recount scenes from the myths are arranged in rows. In the evening, relatives and neighbours come to view the **Golu**; songs ring out; and departing guests are given thamboolam and sweets. This is not a one-day event — it is the hallmark of a festival that stretches across nine nights.

Navaratri (in Tamil, *Navaratri* — "nine nights") is a Hindu festival worshipping the **Goddess**, that is, Shakti. This article describes its customs and how they are observed in Malaysia in a neutral way — without explaining or evaluating the religious meaning of any form of worship.

## What Navaratri Is

Navaratri is a nine-night festival. In the Tamil tradition these nine nights are generally divided into three sets of three and dedicated to three goddesses:

| Nights | Goddess worshipped | Association |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First three | Durga (Shakti) | Strength, the vanquishing of evil |
| Next three | Lakshmi | Wealth, prosperity |
| Final three | Saraswati | Learning, art, knowledge |

The tenth day that follows the nine nights is **Vijayadashami** — regarded as the day Durga vanquished Mahishasura. In many parts of North India this day is called Dasara. Navaratri and Dasara are thus parts of a single festive cycle.

## Golu: The Doll Display

In the South Indian — particularly Tamil — tradition, the most striking feature of Navaratri is the **Golu** (Golu; in Tamil, bommai kolu). At home, steps are set up in an odd number, and deity figurines and dolls are arranged on them. This tiered arrangement resembles a temple gopuram; ascending the steps is explained as representing the journey from the human towards the divine. The top step usually holds the **Navashakti** and a **kalasam** of water and coconut.

Some sets, such as marapachi dolls, are preserved across generations and given a place in the Golu. During Navaratri, relatives and friends are invited to the home to view the Golu; visitors, especially women and girls, are welcomed and given prasadam and **thamboolam** (betel leaf, areca nut, sweets, fruit and a small gift). Many sing songs to the Goddess before the Golu; those able accompany them with musical instruments.

## Saraswati Puja and Ayudha Puja

On the ninth day, **Saraswati Puja** takes centre stage. Books and musical instruments are placed in the prayer room and worshipped so that learning and the arts may flourish. Alongside this, South Indian Hindus perform **Ayudha Puja** — tools, machines and vehicles used in work are cleaned, decorated and placed for worship. For musicians, it is also a day to venerate their instruments.

## Vijayadashami and Vidyarambham

The tenth day, **Vijayadashami**, is regarded as an auspicious day for new beginnings. On this day **Vidyarambham** takes place — the rite in which young children take their first step into literacy; holding the hand of an elder, children write letters in rice or sand. Many begin new learning on this day — school, music, dance — in the belief that beginning on this day, when good triumphed over evil, brings success. In the Kerala tradition, this education-initiation rite is so prominent that the name Vidyarambham comes to denote the festival itself.

## Navaratri in Malaysia

In Malaysia, Navaratri is celebrated in different ways by various communities of Indian descent:

- **Tamil Hindu homes and temples:** setting up the Golu, Saraswati/Ayudha Puja and Vijayadashami worship are central. The **Sri Thendayuthapani Temple** in Sentul is noted as one of the places where a Golu display can be seen; some temples and halls in Kuala Lumpur hold large Navaratri gatherings.
- **Gujarati and North Indian communities:** they organise **Garba** and **Dandiya** dances at night — folk dances performed in groups, Dandiya with sticks.
- **Bengali community:** holds **Durga Puja**, which coincides with Navaratri; it takes place at venues organised by the community, with a Durga idol, dhak drums, bhog offerings and cultural performances.

Navaratri is not a national public holiday in Malaysia — it is celebrated largely at home, in temples and in community halls.

## Common Misconceptions

- **"Navaratri and Durga Puja are the same."** Although both relate to the Goddess Durga, they are distinct celebrations — one a widespread nine-night festival, the other largely a Bengali tradition.
- **"Navaratri is celebrated only by Tamils."** No — Gujarati, North Indian and Bengali communities also celebrate it in their own traditions.
- **"Navaratri is a national holiday."** No — it is not a public holiday in Malaysia; Hinduism's national holiday is Deepavali.
