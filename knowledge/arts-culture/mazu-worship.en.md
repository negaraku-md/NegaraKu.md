On a small hill at Robson Heights in Kuala Lumpur stands a six-storey temple of red pillars and glazed tiles that overlooks the city — this is the Thean Hou Temple, dedicated to the most famous sea goddess in the Chinese world: Mazu. In the past, Chinese people who came south by sea would pray to her for safety before and after crossing the ocean; today it is at once a temple where devotees offer incense, a gathering place for Hainanese kinsfolk, and a backdrop for tourists' photographs.

Mazu, also known as **Tian Hou** and **Tianshang Shengmu**, is the most important sea goddess and patron of seafarers in Chinese folk religion. This article explains, in a descriptive and neutral way, her origins, how she came south to Malaya with Chinese migrants, and her dual religious and cultural role in Malaysia's Chinese community today, without passing judgment on any belief.

## From Lin Moniang to Tian Hou: The Origins of the Belief

According to legend, Mazu's original name was **Lin Moniang**, a native of **Meizhou Island, Fujian**, born in the early Northern Song dynasty. In folk tales she was clever from childhood and at home in the water, often rescuing stricken fishermen and vessels amid wind and waves, and in the end died going out to sea to save others. The people of her home, moved by her virtue, built a temple to venerate her and honoured her as a protective deity of the sea.

Thereafter the imperial court repeatedly conferred titles on her — from 'Lady' and 'Consort' all the way to the title '**Tian Hou**' — and the belief gradually spread from the Fujian coast to the southeastern provinces of China and to overseas Chinese society. In 2009 UNESCO inscribed '**Mazu belief and customs**' on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, describing Mazu as the most influential sea goddess of the Chinese coast.

## A Belief That Came South by Sea

The reason Mazu is seen everywhere in Malaysia is inseparable from the maritime history of Chinese migration. Chinese people from **Fujian, Hainan and Guangdong** mostly came south by sea, and in the era of crossing the ocean to make a living and trading at sea, Mazu was their most important spiritual support.

On reaching Malaya, these migrants built temples to venerate Mazu in the **ports and trading towns** where they settled, praying for safe voyages and flourishing business. Many Tian Hou temples were funded and managed by native-place **clan associations**, with the **Hainanese community** having the deepest ties of all to Mazu. Mazu temples are therefore especially widespread across Malaysia's coastal towns, standing as witnesses to the maritime life of the early Chinese.

## The Main Temples

Temples venerating Mazu are found all over Malaysia, varying in scale and background. The table below lists a few representative examples:

| Temple | Location | Background |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Thean Hou Temple | Kuala Lumpur | Managed by the Selangor & Kuala Lumpur Hainan Association, a six-storey building, one of the largest Mazu temples in Southeast Asia |
| Tian Hou temples of Hainanese associations in various places | Malacca, Muar, Kuala Terengganu and others | Run by Hainanese native-place associations, centres of fellowship and belief for kinsfolk |
| Tian Hou temples belonging to Hokkien and Guangdong associations | Penang and various ports | Venerated by Chinese communities of different origins, often sharing a hall with other deities |

Of these, the **Thean Hou Temple** is the best known: built in the 1980s, its main hall enshrines Tian Hou (Mazu), with Guanyin and Shui Wei Sheng Niang to either side; it is both a temple thronged with worshippers and one of Kuala Lumpur's cultural landmarks.

## The Mazu Festival and Processions

The most important festival of the year in Mazu worship is the **Mazu Festival**, set on the **23rd day of the third lunar month** (traditionally held to be Mazu's birthday). On that day and around it, Tian Hou temples and associations in various places often hold rites, scripture chanting and offerings.

In some places the celebration is on a considerable scale: the temple carries out the Mazu statue in a **street procession**, the column passing through the town centre and Chinatown, accompanied along the way by lion dances, gongs and drums and opera, drawing many devotees and tourists. The Hainanese community of Kuala Terengganu, for example, has held multi-day Mazu celebrations organised by the Hainan association, with the procession of the goddess's statue ending at the local Tian Hou temple. The scale and form of the celebrations vary from temple to temple and community to community.

## From Protector of Voyages to Symbol of Association and Culture

As Chinese society shifted from crossing the sea to make a living to settling and putting down roots, Mazu's role has extended too. She remains an object to whom devotees pray for safety and protection; at the same time, **Tian Hou temple and clan association** often merge into one, making Mazu a focal point for native-place communities — especially the Hainanese — to rally their identity and strengthen their ties.

In terms of religious affiliation, Mazu belongs chiefly to the sphere of **Taoism and Chinese folk religion**, and is often enshrined together with other deities; in **some Buddhist contexts** the veneration of Mazu can also be seen. Many Chinese do not, in daily practice, strictly distinguish between Taoism, Buddhism and folk religion, in keeping with the generally syncretic nature of Chinese belief in Malaysia.

## Common Misconceptions

**Thinking Mazu, Tian Hou and Tianshang Shengmu are different deities.** In fact these are different honorifics for one and the same sea goddess — 'Mazu' is the affectionate popular name, while 'Tian Hou' and 'Tianshang Shengmu' are the titles and honorifics conferred over successive ages.

**Thinking that worshipping Mazu means Buddhism.** Mazu belongs chiefly to Taoism and Chinese folk religion; although she is venerated in some Buddhist contexts, equating Mazu worship with Buddhism is inaccurate.

**Thinking Mazu temples are merely tourist attractions.** Temples like the Thean Hou Temple do draw tourists, but they are first and foremost active places of belief and centres of clan-association activity, where rites such as the Mazu Festival are still held as usual.

**Thinking Mazu worship belongs to one native-place group alone.** Chinese of different origins — Fujian, Hainan, Guangdong and others — all venerate Mazu; the Hainanese community has especially deep ties to Mazu, but Mazu worship is not the exclusive property of any one dialect group.
