In the closing months of the year, a familiar sight appears in many towns across Malaysia: men dressed in black or blue, wearing a rudraksha mala around the neck and letting their beards grow, addressing one another as "Swami". These are the Ayyappan vow-keepers — many of them preparing to travel to Sabarimala in Kerala. This article describes that worship and the vow traditions in a neutral way; it neither explains nor evaluates any belief.

## Who Ayyappan Is

Ayyappan is a deity revered in Hindu tradition as born of Shiva and Vishnu's Mohini form; he is therefore called **Hariharaputra** (Hari = Vishnu, Hara = Shiva). As a guardian of dharma he is also known as **Dharmasastha** or simply **Sastha**. His principal shrine is **Sabarimala**, set in the Western Ghats of Kerala.

Malaysia's Hindu community is largely of South Indian Tamil heritage; although the Saiva tradition and Sakti worship are generally more prominent, Ayyappan worship is also deeply rooted. For broader background on this, see [Hinduism in Malaysia](/en/arts-culture/hinduism-in-malaysia).

## The Mandala Vow: A 41-Day Discipline

Before the Sabarimala pilgrimage, the tradition is to keep a **41-day Mandala vow**. The vow usually begins when a Guruswamy (a senior vow-keeper who has made the pilgrimage many times and guides others) or a temple priest places a **mala** on the devotee; that mala is not removed until the pilgrimage is complete.

Common disciplines observed during the vow:

- **Black or blue clothing** — regarded as a sign of withdrawal from worldly attachments.
- **Vegetarian food**, avoidance of intoxicants, **celibacy**, and a simple life.
- Not cutting the hair or nails; avoiding anger and harsh words.
- Devotees addressing one another as **"Swami"** — regarded as an expression of the attitude of equanimity that sees Ayyappan in everyone.

The Mandala season begins on the first day of the Malayalam month of **Vrischikam** (November–December) and runs into the month of Dhanu; the Mandala Puja is its high point.

## The Irumudi Bundle and Kettunira

As the vow nears its end, on the day before the pilgrimage, the **Irumudi bundle** is filled in a rite called **Kettunira** (Kettunirakkal). It is a sacred load with two parts:

- **Munmudi** (the front part) — a **ghee-filled coconut** (neyythengai), puja items, and offerings for the Swami.
- **Pinmudi** (the rear part) — the devotee's own travel provisions.

The ghee-filled coconut is regarded as a symbol of emptying out worldly attachments and filling the space with thoughts of the divine. This rite usually takes place under the guidance of a Guruswamy. It is a long-standing tradition that **only those who carry the Irumudi on their heads** are permitted to climb the **Eighteen Holy Steps** (the eighteen sacred steps) at Sabarimala.

## The Sabarimala Pilgrimage and Local Preparation

During the Mandala season many Malaysian Hindus travel directly on pilgrimage to Sabarimala in Kerala. Along the traditional pilgrimage route, the **Petta Thullal** held at **Erumeli** is notable — a ritual dance in which participants smear themselves with sandalwood and sacred ash while singing and dancing; it is interpreted as a sign of the victory of good over evil, and also as a sign of religious harmony in memory of Vavar, a Muslim warrior who aided Ayyappan.

For those unable to reach Sabarimala, local **Ayyappan/Sastha temples** in Malaysia serve as centres of preparation. The **Arulmigu Sri Ayyappaswamy Devasthanam** near **Batu Caves** in Selangor is one example; during the season from November to January, mala-wearing vow-keepers come here from various areas. At local temples there are **the donning of the mala, bhajans, abhishekam, the preparation of the Irumudi, and annadanam**; some temples have also built a replica of the Eighteen Holy Steps.

## The Saranam Chant and the Community Dimension

**"Swamiye Saranam Ayyappa"** — this saranam chant, meaning surrender of oneself to the Swami, resounds continually throughout the vow, in the bhajans, and along the climb up the mountain; it is the aural hallmark of Ayyappan worship.

The Ayyappan vow in Malaysia also has a strong **community dimension**. In the old rubber-estate communities and among urban Tamil families, groups of vow-keepers centred on Guruswamys take shape; the vow, the bhajans and the shared journey foster ties across generations and a sense of community unity. For background on the estate Tamil community, see [the estate Tamil community](/en/arts-culture/estate-tamil-community).

## Customs Vary

What is described here is the most common tradition. The number of vow days, the colour of the clothing, the dietary restrictions and the details of the rites can all vary according to **temple, family and the guidance of the Guruswamy**. This article takes no position on contemporary controversies relating to Sabarimala; it describes only the worship traditions.
