On the hills of Penang, the silhouette of Kek Lok Si looks down over George Town;
in Brickfields, Kuala Lumpur, the chanting of Pali scripture drifts from a
Theravada temple; and in the northern states of Kedah and Kelantan, the temples
(Wat) of the Thai community have stood for generations. These scenes belong to
one religion, yet spring from very different traditions.

Buddhism is Malaysia's **second-largest religion**, but it is not a single set of
practices. To understand it, one must first distinguish several parallel
threads — and the blurred line between it and Chinese folk religion.

## Population and distribution

According to the 2020 Population and Housing Census, Malaysia's religious
composition is: Islam 63.5%, **Buddhism 18.7%**, Christianity 9.1%, Hinduism
6.1%. Buddhism is thus the second-largest religion after Islam, with adherents
predominantly **Malaysian Chinese**.

An important statistical caveat: the census "Buddhist" figure overlaps heavily
with **Chinese folk religion**. Many Chinese who, in daily life, worship the
Buddha, pray to deities and venerate their ancestors all at once will register
themselves as Buddhists in the census. The figure of 18.7% therefore includes
both Buddhists in the strict sense and many syncretic folk-religion practitioners.
On the latter, see
[Chinese religion and Taoism in Malaysia](/en/arts-culture/chinese-religion-in-malaysia).

## Three main traditions

- **Mahayana Buddhism** is the most common tradition in the Chinese community,
  often blended with Taoism, Confucian thought and ancestor veneration.
  Bodhisattvas such as Guanyin (Avalokiteshvara) are widely venerated.
- **Theravada Buddhism** is practised mainly by the **Thai** (Malaysian Siamese),
  Sri Lankan and Burmese communities; the Thai communities of the northern
  states have the longest history, and their temples are called Wat.
- **Vajrayana** (Tibetan Buddhism) is smaller in scale but also has its centres
  and adherents.

## Historical background

The large-scale migration of Chinese to Malaya took place mainly from the 19th to
the early 20th century, arriving with tin mining and trade; Buddhism and Chinese
folk religion took root alongside it. The Theravada tradition brought by the
northern Thai communities is older still, passed down over generations in Kedah,
Kelantan, Perlis and elsewhere. Ancient temples such as the Cheng Hoon Teng in
Malacca bear witness to the religious life of early Chinese immigrants.

## Festivals

- **Wesak (Vesak Day)** commemorates the Buddha's birth, enlightenment and
  passing, and is a **national public holiday**. Devotees visit temples to offer
  flowers, light lamps and give alms. See [Wesak](/en/arts-culture/wesak).
- **Chinese New Year**, though a cultural festival, also carries many religious
  elements of ancestor veneration and prayers for blessings; see
  [Chinese New Year](/en/arts-culture/chinese-new-year).

## Temples and organisations

Well-known temples include **Kek Lok Si** in Penang, the **Maha Vihara** in
Brickfields, Kuala Lumpur (Theravada), the **Reclining Buddha temple (Wat
Chayamangkalaram)** and the Burmese temple (Dhammikarama) in Penang — reflecting
the coexistence of different traditions.

At the organisational level, bodies such as the **Malaysian Buddhist Association
(MBA)** and the **Young Buddhist Association of Malaysia (YBAM)** have long
promoted the propagation of the dharma, education and social-welfare work. Most
temples are independently registered and managed; there is no single national
statutory governing body.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Buddhism in Malaysia is just Chinese religion."** The Chinese are the main
  body of adherents, but the Theravada Buddhism practised by the Thai, Sri Lankan
  and Burmese communities is another, independent tradition.
- **"Buddhists don't worship deities."** Many Chinese Buddhists blend Buddhism,
  Taoism and folk religion in practice, with no clear dividing line; this is
  precisely why the census figures overlap.
- **"Religion equals ethnic group."** Religion and ethnicity are two different
  things; the census measures each separately.
