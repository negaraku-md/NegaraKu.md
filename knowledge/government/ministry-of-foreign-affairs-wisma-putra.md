When a Malaysian is stranded overseas, when Malaysia casts a vote at the United
Nations, or when a new ambassador presents credentials in a foreign capital,
the same institution stands behind all three — Wisma Putra.

"Wisma Putra" is the everyday name for the **Ministry of Foreign Affairs**
(Kementerian Luar Negeri). The nickname comes from the building the ministry
once occupied; the name stuck even after it moved. Its official website today
is `kln.gov.my`.

## At a glance

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Official name | Ministry of Foreign Affairs (Kementerian Luar Negeri) |
| Popular name | Wisma Putra |
| Type | Federal ministry |
| Headquarters | No. 1, Jalan Wisma Putra, Precinct 2, Putrajaya |
| Website | `kln.gov.my` |
| Missions abroad | 111 missions in 85 countries |
| Minister (as of 2026) | Dato' Seri Utama Mohamad Hasan |

## What does Wisma Putra actually do?

Its job is to run Malaysia's relationships with the rest of the world. On its
own statement of roles, the ministry conducts Malaysia's foreign relations
across political, economic, security, and social and cultural matters.

The functions it lists fall into a few buckets:

- **Protecting national interests** — upholding Malaysia's sovereignty,
  territorial integrity and national interests, and monitoring regional and
  global developments.
- **Conducting diplomacy** — managing bilateral, regional and multilateral
  relations with foreign countries and international organisations such as the
  UN, ASEAN and the OIC.
- **Advising and coordinating** — developing foreign policy to advise the
  Government, and coordinating and leading the Government's position on
  international issues across other ministries and agencies.
- **Economic diplomacy** — promoting trade and investment with foreign
  countries at the government-to-government level.

Notice the pattern: Wisma Putra is the *coordinator and voice* of Malaysia
abroad. It does not run the country's trade policy or its passport-printing
line — it speaks for Malaysia and keeps the diplomatic machinery running.

## Where did the ministry come from?

The groundwork predates independence. In **1956**, a pioneer batch of 11
career diplomats was sent to Britain and Australia for training to staff what
was then called the **Ministry of External Affairs (MEA)**.

At independence, foreign affairs sat at the very top of government: Tunku
Abdul Rahman, the first Prime Minister, also served as the first Minister of
External Affairs, and is regarded as the main architect of Malaya's early
foreign policy.

Two later milestones shaped the ministry most people recognise today:

- In **1966**, the Ministry of External Affairs was renamed the **Ministry of
  Foreign Affairs** and moved from the Sultan Abdul Samad Building to a new
  complex called **Wisma Putra** at Bukit Petaling, Kuala Lumpur — the origin
  of the nickname.
- On **17 September 2001**, the ministry began operating from its current
  complex at Jalan Wisma Putra, Precinct 2, Putrajaya.

## How big is Malaysia's diplomatic network?

Diplomacy needs presence on the ground, and Wisma Putra runs a substantial
overseas footprint. By its own count, Malaysia maintains **111 missions in 85
countries**, made up of:

| Type of mission | Count |
| --- | --- |
| Embassies | 66 |
| High commissions | 18 |
| Consulates general | 21 |
| Consulates | 2 |
| Permanent representatives to the UN | 2 |
| Permanent representative to ASEAN | 1 |
| Malaysian Friendship and Trade Centre | 1 |
| **Total** | **111** |

(High commissions, rather than embassies, are Malaysia's missions in fellow
Commonwealth countries — the two are equivalent in rank.) These missions are
the institutional extension of the Government of Malaysia abroad, tasked with
maintaining good relations with foreign states and bodies such as the UN,
ASEAN and the OIC, and with advancing Malaysia's national interests.

## What can a Malaysian abroad ask a mission for?

For most citizens, this is where Wisma Putra becomes personal. Missions
provide **consular services**, including issuing passports, travel documents
and visas, attesting and certifying documents, furnishing certificates of good
conduct, and answering consular enquiries.

They also step in when things go wrong. Wisma Putra and its missions extend
assistance to Malaysians in distress abroad — the work that becomes visible
when citizens need help during conflicts or emergencies overseas.

A practical note: routine passport renewal at home is handled by the
Immigration Department, but overseas it is a Malaysian mission that issues
travel documents. If you are abroad and in difficulty, the nearest embassy,
high commission or consulate is the front door.

## Where does Wisma Putra end and MITI begin?

This is the most common point of confusion. Malaysia's international economic
relations are split between diplomacy and trade:

| Body | What it owns |
| --- | --- |
| **Wisma Putra** (Foreign Affairs) | Diplomacy, foreign policy, government-to-government economic relations, and the missions abroad |
| **MITI** (Investment, Trade and Industry) | Trade and industrial policy; negotiating free trade agreements |
| **MATRADE** | Promoting Malaysian exports into overseas markets |

Wisma Putra frames the relationships and opens the doors; MITI sets the trade
rules and MATRADE does the export promotion. The lines blur in practice —
economic diplomacy is a shared agenda, and Malaysian missions often support
trade goals — but the policy and promotion levers for trade sit with the trade
ministries, not the foreign ministry.

## What is the Strategic Plan 2026-2030?

On 23 February 2026, Prime Minister Dato' Seri Anwar Ibrahim launched the
**Ministry of Foreign Affairs Strategic Plan 2026-2030**, the ministry's
five-year roadmap, at a Wisma Putra event in Putrajaya. It is built around
**8 strategic thrusts, 33 strategies and 139 programmes**, continuing from the
mid-term review of the 2021-2025 plan.

As reported by Business Today, the eight thrusts cover consolidating bilateral
diplomacy, multilateral leadership, ASEAN centrality, maritime sovereignty,
economic diplomacy, public diplomacy and service delivery, institutional
capacity, and Malaysia's obligations under the Chemical Weapons Convention. A
headline theme for the period is **economic diplomacy** — the same report
describes the plan as positioning Malaysia as a "bridge" between the Global
North and South, drawing on strengths in the halal industry, the digital
economy and high-value semiconductors. (These characterisations should be
checked against the primary Strategic Plan document before publishing.)
