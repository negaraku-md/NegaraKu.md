If you have driven through a roadblock on the eve of a public holiday, queued at a crowded festival, or passed a disaster-relief station after a flood, you have probably seen **RELA** — uniformed volunteers directing traffic and lending a hand to the authorities. The **People's Volunteer Corps** (*Ikatan Relawan Rakyat*, today the *Jabatan Sukarelawan Malaysia*, **RELA**) is a civilian volunteer force organised as a department under the **Ministry of Home Affairs** (*Kementerian Dalam Negeri*, KDN). Its members assist the police, the armed forces and other agencies — they are not themselves an independent police force.

## What RELA is

RELA is a volunteer body: ordinary citizens who enrol, train and turn out to support official operations when called upon. According to the federal service portal, membership is open to Malaysian citizens aged 18 and above who are not already serving in the Armed Forces or another statutory force, enrolment runs for renewable five-year terms, and members are treated as public servants under the Penal Code (Act 574) while on duty. A **Director General**, appointed by the Minister from the public service, commands the corps. By headcount, RELA is one of Malaysia's largest volunteer organisations, with membership reported in the millions (see *verificationNeeded* for the current figure).

## Origins: from the Home Guard to Konfrontasi

RELA's lineage runs back to the civil-defence volunteers of the mid-twentieth century. Its closest ancestor is the **Home Guard** (*Pasukan Simpanan Kampung*), set up during the Malayan Emergency declared in 1948 to help protect villages, and stood down when that Emergency ended in 1960. During **Konfrontasi** — the 1963–66 Indonesia–Malaysia confrontation — the government again leaned on community volunteer units for local security, acting as auxiliary eyes and ears for the police and army.

The corps in its modern form is generally dated to **1972**, with its early legal scaffolding provided by regulations made under the **Emergency (Essential Powers) Act 1964** — notably the *Essential (Ikatan Relawan Rakyat) Regulations*. (Older rules from the 1960s even made volunteer service compulsory for men of certain ages; that obligation was revoked in 2005.) In **2013**, RELA was upgraded from a unit into a full federal department, the **Jabatan Sukarelawan Malaysia**.

## What RELA does today

In peacetime, RELA's work is supportive and community-facing. Its typical deployments include:

| Area | Examples of what RELA does |
| --- | --- |
| Public order & events | Crowd control and marshalling at large public events (for example, traffic duty at the Formula 1 race at Sepang) |
| Traffic | Helping police and traffic wardens control and divert vehicles at roadblocks and congested points |
| Community service | *Gotong-royong*, government-premises patrols, welfare and outreach activities |
| Disaster & emergencies | Flood relief, search-and-rescue, and assisting with forest-fire suppression |
| National support tasks | Assisting the police and armed forces when directed — for instance, enforcing the Movement Control Order during the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020 |

RELA's own portal and the Ministry of Home Affairs describe the corps as assisting the security authorities "whenever required," alongside traffic control, protection of premises on request, and community service.

## Powers and legal basis

The current statute is the **Malaysia Volunteers Corps Act 2012 (Act 752)**, which received royal assent and came into force on 22 June 2012. Section 3 of the Act establishes the corps; Part III sets out its duties and powers. Under section 5, members must assist a security force or authority on request, help control and divert motor vehicles, take part in community service, help protect buildings or property at the owner's request, and carry out the Director General's orders. Crucially, while assisting a security force, members operate **under the command of an officer of that force** who is present.

On powers of enforcement, the Act is deliberately narrow. Its only such provision, section 6, lets members divert motor vehicles under the command of a police officer or traffic warden. The Act confers **no power of arrest or detention** — a significant change from the force's earlier footing.

## Debate and criticism

RELA's wider powers and immigration role in the 2000s were the subject of sustained criticism, which this profile records descriptively and attributes to its sources. Under a 2005 amendment to the emergency-era regulations, RELA was given far broader authority — reported to include bearing and using firearms, stopping and searching, demanding documents, and arresting or entering premises **without a warrant** — and was deployed in operations against undocumented migrants, including charge of immigration detention centres.

**Human Rights Watch** in 2007 called for the corps to be disbanded, alleging illegal raids, extortion and abuses by members. **Amnesty International** urged the government to halt the force's expansion, arguing that RELA "lacks a clear structure of command responsibility," and criticised the later return of immigration-detention duties to the corps after a pledge to strip that role. A 2009 UN Human Development Report likewise recorded migrant activists describing some RELA volunteers as "vigilantes." The Malaysian government, for its part, defended the corps as necessary and pledged training and tighter discipline, and the **2012 Act** removed the arrest and search powers that had drawn the sharpest objections. Debate over RELA's residual role at immigration depots has continued since.

## Why it matters

RELA sits at the meeting point of two things many countries handle separately: community volunteering and public security. For residents, it is the most visible face of auxiliary policing — the people at the roadblock or the flood-relief tent. For understanding Malaysian governance, RELA is a case study in how a force's legal powers can expand and then be reined back: the arc from emergency-era regulations and warrantless powers to a narrowly drawn 2012 statute shows the law adjusting to criticism over time. Knowing what RELA can and cannot do — assist and direct, but not arrest — helps set accurate expectations in any encounter.
