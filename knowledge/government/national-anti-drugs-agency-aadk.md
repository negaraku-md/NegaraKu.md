Malaysia splits its response to drugs into two broad halves. One half is criminal enforcement — arresting traffickers, seizing supply, prosecuting offences — and that is led by the police. The other half is **demand reduction**: stopping people from using drugs in the first place, and treating and rehabilitating those who do. That half is led by the **National Anti-Drugs Agency** (*Agensi Antidadah Kebangsaan*, or **AADK**, also styled **NADA**), an agency under the **Ministry of Home Affairs** (*Kementerian Dalam Negeri*, KDN). AADK runs the country's drug-rehabilitation centres, delivers prevention and education programmes, and manages aftercare for people in recovery.

## What AADK is

AADK is a federal agency under the Ministry of Home Affairs, headquartered in Kajang, Selangor. According to the agency's account of its own history, it was formed in 1996, and it was later placed on a statutory footing by the **National Anti-Drugs Agency Act 2004** (Act 638). Because the agency pre-dates that Act, the 2004 law is best understood as formalising and empowering an existing body rather than creating it from nothing.

The agency's orientation is treatment-led. As its public messaging and the "Cure & Care" branding of its services suggest, AADK frames drug dependence primarily as a health and rehabilitation issue — a framing reinforced by recent legislative change (described below). This is a description of how the agency positions its work; the broader debate over Malaysian drug policy is beyond the scope of this profile.

## What AADK does

AADK's work spans the full demand-reduction cycle, which can be grouped into three streams.

**Prevention and education.** The agency runs awareness and preventive-education programmes aimed at schools, families, workplaces and communities, intended to reduce the uptake of drugs and to build community resilience against drug abuse.

**Treatment and rehabilitation.** This is the most visible part of AADK's work. The agency operates residential rehabilitation centres known as **Puspen** (*Pusat Pemulihan Penagihan Narkotik*, Narcotics Addiction Rehabilitation Centres), alongside community-based services delivered under the agency's **Cure & Care** model. Programmes run both institutionally and in the community so that clients can, where suitable, receive treatment while remaining in their own environment.

**Aftercare.** Recovery does not end at discharge. AADK provides follow-up and reintegration support for people leaving treatment, and in 2024 it opened an intermediate (halfway) home in Kuala Lumpur offering screening, assessment, referral and in-community treatment to help recovering individuals reintegrate — a model it indicated it may expand.

AADK publishes its own outcome statistics. In March 2026 the agency reported an **81 per cent recovery rate** among 26,852 clients evaluated for 2025, a figure its Director-General attributed to community-based programmes and measured using the agency's Cure and Care Recovery Index. These are agency-reported figures (see *verificationNeeded*).

## The legal basis

Two statutes anchor AADK's work:

- The **National Anti-Drugs Agency Act 2004 (Act 638)** establishes the agency. It received Royal Assent on 24 December 2004 and was gazetted on 30 December 2004. The Act's purpose is to vest the agency's officers with preventive, treatment, rehabilitation, enforcement, investigation, special-preventive, forfeiture and administrative functions in relation to drug offences under laws such as the Dangerous Drugs Act 1952 and the Drug Dependants (Treatment and Rehabilitation) Act 1983.
- The **Drug Dependants (Treatment and Rehabilitation) Act 1983 (Act 283)** governs how drug and substance dependants are brought into treatment and rehabilitation.

The 1983 Act was significantly amended in 2024, with the changes coming into force on **22 August 2025**. The Home Minister described the amendments as broadening the law to cover substance abusers (not only drug dependants) and shifting the emphasis away from imprisonment toward treatment. Crucially, the amendments expanded **voluntary treatment**: individuals can now approach AADK directly, and the agency and Home Ministry have stated that those who come forward voluntarily are regarded as patients needing treatment, not recorded as offenders. Reported voluntary-treatment durations run from three months to two years depending on severity.

## AADK versus the police

A common point of confusion is where AADK ends and the police begin. The table below sets out the practical division of labour.

| Dimension | National Anti-Drugs Agency (AADK) | Royal Malaysia Police (PDRM) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary role | Demand reduction: prevention, treatment, rehabilitation, aftercare | Supply control: criminal enforcement, investigation, prosecution |
| Typical focus | People who use or are dependent on drugs; recovery | Trafficking, possession and supply offences; criminal networks |
| Parent ministry | Ministry of Home Affairs (KDN) | Ministry of Home Affairs (KDN) |
| Key facilities | Puspen rehabilitation centres; Cure & Care services | Police stations; the Narcotics Criminal Investigation Department |

The distinction is one of emphasis rather than an absolute wall. Under the National Anti-Drugs Agency Act 2004, AADK officers do hold statutory powers — when the Act was being passed, press reporting noted that agency officers would be empowered to arrest drug offenders and enforce anti-drug laws. In practice, however, criminal enforcement against trafficking and supply is led by the police, while AADK concentrates on identifying, admitting and rehabilitating people who are dependent on drugs. Both agencies sit under the same ministry.

## How people engage with it

For most members of the public, contact with AADK happens in one of a few ways: a family seeking help for a relative who uses drugs; an individual choosing voluntary treatment under the amended 1983 Act; a person completing a court-referred or officer-assessed rehabilitation programme; or a school, employer or community group inviting AADK to run prevention and awareness activities. Since the 2025 legal changes, the voluntary route has been promoted as a way to seek help "without fear of arrest," and the agency has reported growing numbers of people reporting voluntarily to Puspen centres.

## Why it matters

AADK is the institutional embodiment of the health-and-rehabilitation side of Malaysia's drug response. Understanding it clears up a frequent misconception — that all drug matters run through the police — and tells families, employers and people who use drugs where to turn for treatment rather than prosecution. The recent legislative shift toward voluntary admission has also made the agency, rather than a courtroom, the first point of contact for many seeking help.
