Most people meet the **Road Transport Department** at a single moment — renewing road tax, sitting a driving test, or transferring a used car. But JPJ is the department that stands behind every one of those transactions. The **Road Transport Department** (*Jabatan Pengangkutan Jalan*, or **JPJ**) is an agency under Malaysia's **Ministry of Transport (MOT)**. It registers motor vehicles and drivers, issues driving licences, road tax and number plates, licenses commercial vehicles, and enforces the laws that govern how vehicles and drivers use public roads.

## What JPJ is

JPJ is the civilian regulator of road transport in Malaysia. According to the department's own portal, its headquarters sits in Putrajaya and its work is organised into divisions covering vehicle and driver licensing, automotive engineering and road safety, enforcement and revenue management, and supporting functions such as digital technology and its training arm, the *Akademi Pengangkutan Jalan Malaysia* (Malaysian Road Transport Academy). The department's roots run deep: historical accounts trace it to road-transport administration set up in the late 1930s to 1940s, later consolidated under successive laws into the modern department. (Sources disagree on the exact founding date — see *verificationNeeded*.)

As a department rather than a ministry, JPJ carries out policy set by the Ministry of Transport and implements the federal laws that regulate motoring. Its public-facing identity is built almost entirely around service delivery — the licence, the registration, the road tax — which is why nearly every Malaysian motorist interacts with it repeatedly over a lifetime of driving.

## What JPJ does

JPJ's responsibilities cluster into a handful of core areas:

- **Driver licensing.** JPJ issues and renews driving licences and sets the licence structure. Learners begin with a **Learner's Driving Licence (LDL)** and display an "L" plate; new drivers then hold a **Probationary Driving Licence (PDL)** with a "P" plate for a two-year probation before qualifying for the full **Competent Driving Licence (CDL)**. Commercial drivers additionally need a **vocational licence** — a Goods Driving Licence (GDL) for lorries and goods vehicles, or a Public Service Vehicle (PSV) licence for taxis, buses and e-hailing.
- **Motor-vehicle registration.** Every motor vehicle must be registered with JPJ, which records ownership, assigns the registration (number-plate) details and handles transfers of ownership.
- **Road tax (LKM).** What Malaysians call road tax (*cukai jalan*) is formally the **Motor Vehicle Licence** — *Lesen Kenderaan Motor*, or **LKM** — which JPJ issues to permit a vehicle to be used on public roads. The MyGovernment portal describes the LKM as issued by JPJ to allow a vehicle to operate on the road, with valid motor insurance a precondition for renewal.
- **Number plates.** JPJ controls the registration-number system, including the auction of special and personalised plates through its bidding platforms.
- **Commercial-vehicle licensing.** JPJ administers licensing for goods and public-service vehicles, work historically tied to the Commercial Vehicles Licensing Board framework.
- **Enforcement.** JPJ runs its own enforcement operations against unlicensed drivers, unregistered or unroadworthy vehicles, overloaded lorries and other breaches, and operates the **KEJARA** demerit-point system that penalises traffic offenders.

### Driving licence classes

Licences are issued by vehicle class. The table below summarises the main classes as reported in public guides; exact thresholds should be confirmed against JPJ's official list.

| Class | Vehicles it covers |
| --- | --- |
| B / B2 | Motorcycles (B for larger engines; B2 for smaller) |
| C | Three-wheeled motorcycles / trikes |
| D / DA | Cars up to 3,500 kg (DA for automatic transmission) |
| E / E1 / E2 | Heavy vehicles and lorries, by weight band |
| F–I | Tractors and mobile machinery |
| A / A1 | Adapted vehicles for persons with disabilities |

## The legal basis: the Road Transport Act 1987

JPJ's central legal authority is the **Road Transport Act 1987 (Act 333)**, which received Royal Assent in August 1987 and came into force the following month. The Act governs the registration and licensing of motor vehicles and drivers, construction and use rules, traffic offences and penalties, and the enforcement powers JPJ relies on. Commercial-vehicle licensing historically draws on a companion statute, the **Commercial Vehicles Licensing Board Act 1987 (Act 334)**. Subsidiary rules made under these Acts fill in the operational detail.

Because the Act is amended regularly — including periodic reviews of penalties — motorists should treat specific fines and section numbers as liable to change and check the current authoritative text where it matters.

## How you interact with JPJ

JPJ has moved much of its service delivery online while keeping physical counters. Transactions can be done through:

- **mySIKAP** — the public online portal (at *public.jpj.gov.my*) for renewing road tax and driving licences and other transactions.
- **The MyJPJ app** — a mobile app that provides digital copies of the driving licence and road tax (**e-LKM**), lets users check licence status and summonses, and supports renewals. Existing mySIKAP credentials carry over to the app.
- **JPJ counters and appointed partners** — branch offices, self-service kiosks, and partners such as Pos Malaysia and MyEG for over-the-counter renewals.

Since 2023, JPJ has issued digital road tax and digital driving licences, and physical display of the road-tax sticker is no longer mandatory for most private vehicles.

## JPJ vs the traffic police

A common point of confusion is where JPJ ends and the police begin. JPJ is a department of the **Ministry of Transport** and handles licensing, registration, road tax and road-transport enforcement. The traffic police are a branch of the **Royal Malaysia Police ([PDRM](/government/pdrm))**, which sits under the Ministry of Home Affairs. Both enforce rules on the road — and a motorist may receive a summons from either — but they are separate agencies with different parent ministries and different core mandates. The MyJPJ app reflects this overlap by letting users view and pay both JPJ and PDRM summonses in one place.

## Why it matters

For an individual, JPJ is a gatekeeper: you cannot legally drive without a licence it issues, or use a vehicle on the road without a registration and a current road tax it administers. For the country, JPJ underpins road safety and transport policy — it holds the national vehicle and driver registers, sets the standards vehicles must meet, and is a significant revenue collector through licensing and road tax. Understanding that JPJ (not the police) is the licensing and registration authority, and that "road tax" is really the LKM, resolves most of the everyday confusion about who to deal with for what.
