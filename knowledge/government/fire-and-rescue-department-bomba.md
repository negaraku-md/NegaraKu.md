Ask anyone in Malaysia who to call when a building is burning or someone is trapped, and the answer is the same single word: **Bomba**. The **Fire and Rescue Department of Malaysia** — *Jabatan Bomba dan Penyelamat Malaysia* (**JBPM**), known to almost everyone simply as **BOMBA** — is the federal agency responsible for firefighting, rescue, hazardous-material response and fire-safety enforcement across the country. It is an agency under the **Ministry of Housing and Local Government** (*Kementerian Perumahan dan Kerajaan Tempatan*, KPKT), and in an emergency it is reached by dialling **999**.

## What is BOMBA?

The name itself is instructive. "Bomba" is a Malay word derived from the Portuguese *bombeiros*, meaning firefighters — a reminder that organised firefighting in the region predates independence. Today the department is a national, uniformed public-safety service whose official English name is the **Fire and Rescue Department of Malaysia**; its Malay name, *Jabatan Bomba dan Penyelamat Malaysia*, places "rescue" (*penyelamat*) on an equal footing with firefighting, reflecting how the modern service has grown well beyond fires alone. The department's headquarters is in Putrajaya, and it operates a network of fire and rescue stations in every state.

## What does BOMBA do?

BOMBA's work spans several related missions. According to the MyGovernment portal's statement of the department's role, its central duty is to "extinguish, fight, prevent and control fires," together with protecting lives and property during disasters and emergencies. In practice its operations fall into a handful of broad areas:

| Operation | What it covers |
| --- | --- |
| Firefighting | Extinguishing, combating and controlling fires in homes, vehicles, industry, forests and open land |
| Rescue | Technical and urban search-and-rescue, road-accident extrication, and water rescue |
| Hazardous materials (HAZMAT) | Response to chemical, biological and other hazardous-material incidents |
| Air operations | Aerial firefighting, aerial search-and-rescue and airborne support in disasters |
| Fire safety | Inspection of premises, enforcement of fire-safety requirements and issuing of Fire Certificates |
| Fire investigation | Determining the cause, origin and circumstances of fires |

Prevention is as much a part of the job as response. BOMBA conducts safety inspections, fire-safety education and public awareness work, and handles public reporting of hazards — so that fewer emergencies happen in the first place.

## The legal basis: the Fire Services Act 1988

BOMBA's powers and duties are set out chiefly in the **Fire Services Act 1988** (Act 341), the country's principal fire-safety law. The functions the MyGovernment portal attributes to the department track the Act closely: under section 5, the service is to stop, combat, prevent and contain fires; safeguard people and property during a fire; ensure that means of escape exist and are properly maintained; investigate the cause and circumstances of fires; and provide humanitarian aid, including protecting life and property during any disaster. The Act also empowers the department to issue fire-hazard abatement notices and underpins the Fire Certificate regime for higher-risk buildings. A Fire Services (Amendment) Bill was reported in 2025 as intended to strengthen oversight of volunteer brigades and fire-safety consultants; its parliamentary status should be verified before it is relied on (see *verificationNeeded*).

## How you reach BOMBA

In any fire, rescue or disaster emergency, the number to dial is **999**, Malaysia's single national emergency line. Operated as the Malaysia Emergency Response Services (**MERS 999**), the line is a shared platform: a single call can route the caller to the Fire and Rescue Department, the Royal Malaysia Police, the Ministry of Health's ambulance service, the Malaysian Civil Defence Force and the Malaysian Maritime Enforcement Agency. Calls to 999 are free, and the number also answers when dialled as 112 from a mobile phone. In November 2025 the service was upgraded to a next-generation platform ("NG MERS 999") adding AI-assisted handling and more precise caller location, as reported by *The Star*.

For non-emergency fire-safety matters, the main interaction most businesses have with BOMBA is the **Fire Certificate** (*Perakuan Bomba*). Under the Fire Services Act 1988, designated higher-risk premises must obtain and keep current a Fire Certificate confirming the building is fit to occupy from a fire-safety standpoint. The certificate is issued after BOMBA reviews the building's fire-protection systems and carries out an inspection; renewals are likewise subject to inspection, and alterations to fire-protection systems generally need the department's approval first. Building owners should confirm the current forms, fees and whether their premises are "designated" with BOMBA directly.

## Organisation and reach

BOMBA is a federal department headed by a Director-General, supported by functional divisions covering operations, fire safety, engineering and logistics, training, planning and research, fire investigation, air operations and corporate management. It maintains fire and rescue stations across all states and federal territories, backed by specialist teams for search-and-rescue, water rescue and hazardous-material response. Figures commonly cited put the workforce at roughly 15,000 personnel; the exact headcount and the number of stations should be confirmed against the department's own corporate information (see *verificationNeeded*).

## Why it matters

For residents, BOMBA is the most visible point of the national emergency-response system: the service that arrives when a home catches fire, a vehicle crashes, a child is trapped or a flood strands a family. For businesses and building owners, it is also a regulator — the authority whose inspection and Fire Certificate stand between a premises and lawful, safe occupation. Understanding both faces of the department — emergency responder and fire-safety enforcer — helps individuals know when to dial 999 and helps occupiers stay on the right side of the Fire Services Act 1988.
