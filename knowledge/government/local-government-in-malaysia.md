Local government is Malaysia's third tier of administration, below the federal and state governments. Day-to-day services — refuse collection, licensing, drains, roads within town limits, planning approvals and public health enforcement — are delivered by **local authorities** (*Pihak Berkuasa Tempatan*, or **PBT**). In Peninsular Malaysia these operate under the **Local Government Act 1976 (Act 171)**, which classifies every local authority as a City Council, a Municipal Council or a District Council. Sabah and Sarawak run parallel systems under their own state ordinances.

## Constitutional basis

Local government is a **State List** matter under the Ninth Schedule (List II) of the Federal Constitution, so legal authority sits primarily with the states. To keep policy consistent nationwide, **Article 95A** establishes the **National Council for Local Government (Majlis Negara bagi Kerajaan Tempatan)** — chaired by a federal Minister, with one representative from each state and up to ten federal representatives — to formulate national policy on local government, town planning and housing. At the federal level, the **Ministry of Housing and Local Government (KPKT)** and its **Department of Local Government (Jabatan Kerajaan Tempatan, JKT)** coordinate legislation, upgrades and standards.

## The three types of local authority

Section 2 of Act 171 recognises three tiers. They are the same kind of institution at different sizes; a district can be upgraded to a municipality, and the largest municipalities can attain city status once they meet population and income thresholds.

| Type | Malay name | Typical status |
|---|---|---|
| City Council | Dewan / Majlis Bandaraya | Largest urban centres |
| Municipal Council | Majlis Perbandaran | Mid-sized towns |
| District Council | Majlis Daerah | Smaller towns and rural districts |

KPKT's Department of Local Government lists **155 local authorities** nationwide. Reported thresholds (approved in 2008) set **city status** at roughly 500,000 population and RM100 million annual income, and **municipal status** at about 150,000 population and RM20 million income; confirm the current figures with KPKT before relying on them.

## Key legislation

Three principal Acts frame a Peninsular council's work:

- **Local Government Act 1976 (Act 171)** — establishment, powers, councillors, finance and by-laws.
- **Town and Country Planning Act 1976 (Act 172)** — development plans and planning permission.
- **Street, Drainage and Building Act 1974 (Act 133)** — buildings, streets and drainage.

Sabah operates under the **Local Government Ordinance 1961** and Sarawak under the **Local Authorities Ordinance 1996** (citations to confirm).

## How councillors are chosen

Councillors are **appointed by the state government**, not elected. Local council elections were **suspended in 1965** under the Emergency (Suspension of Local Government Elections) Regulations 1965 and have not resumed; the appointment mechanism is set out in section 10 of Act 171. Each council is led by a Mayor (*Datuk Bandar*) or President (*Yang Dipertua*) and staffed by career officers.

## Federal Territories exception

Kuala Lumpur, Putrajaya and Labuan are **not** governed by Act 171. Kuala Lumpur is run by **Dewan Bandaraya Kuala Lumpur (DBKL)** under the Federal Capital Act 1960 and related law, with Putrajaya and Labuan administered by their own corporations under KPKT.

## How councils are funded

The main own-source revenue is **assessment tax** (*cukai taksiran*, sometimes called *cukai pintu*), a rate levied on the annual or improved value of holdings within the council area. Councils also raise **licensing fees, parking, compounds, rentals and planning charges**, supplemented by state and federal grants.

## What's next

To act on this, look up your own local authority on the KPKT/JKT List of Local Authorities to find its assessment-tax rates, licensing counters and by-laws, or read the source text of the Local Government Act 1976 (Act 171) for the exact powers behind any council decision.
