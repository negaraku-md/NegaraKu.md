A pothole, a land-title query, and an income-tax problem feel like they should
all go to "the government." They do not go to the same part of it. Send a local
drainage complaint to your Member of Parliament and it may sit in an inbox;
send it to the right council desk and a crew can be dispatched. Knowing who
actually does what is the difference between a problem that moves and one that
circles.

Malaysia has three relevant roles, and the most common mistake is assuming your
elected representative is the one who fixes things. Usually they are not — they
are the one who *raises* things.

## The three roles, in plain terms

**Your local council — the PBT (Pihak Berkuasa Tempatan).** This is the
**operational** body for local services, created under the **Local Government Act
1976 (Act 171)**. Depending on where you live it is a City Council (*Majlis
Bandaraya*, like Kuala Lumpur's DBKL), a Municipal Council (*Majlis
Perbandaran*), or a District Council (*Majlis Daerah*). The council is what
collects your rubbish, maintains local roads and drains, lights the streets,
licenses local businesses and hawkers, and enforces building by-laws. One thing
it is *not*: an elected body. Council **councillors are appointed by the state
government**, not voted in — local elections were suspended in 1965 and the Act
provides for appointed councillors (the [local councils](/en/states/local-councils)
and [local government](/en/government/local-government-councils) articles cover
that history).

**Your ADUN — Ahli Dewan Undangan Negeri.** Your elected representative in the
**state** legislative assembly (Dewan Undangan Negeri). ADUNs deal with
**state-level** matters — land, state roads, state welfare and housing, and
religious affairs — and can raise constituents' issues in the assembly. The
Federal Territories (Kuala Lumpur, Putrajaya, Labuan) have **no ADUN**.

**Your MP — Ahli Parlimen.** Your elected representative in the **Dewan Rakyat**,
the federal lower house. MPs deal with **federal** matters — national policy,
taxation, immigration, citizenship — debate and pass federal laws, and raise
constituency concerns in Parliament.

A crucial point about both the MP and the ADUN: they are **representatives, not
managers of agencies**. Only some become ministers or state executive councillors
with operational power. For an ordinary problem, their role is to raise, follow
up and escalate — not to run the department that does the work.

## Why the split exists — the Ninth Schedule

The division is constitutional. The **Ninth Schedule of the Federal Constitution**
sorts legislative powers into three lists — a **Federal List**, a **State List**,
and a **Concurrent List** (both) — and under **Article 74** the federal and state
legislatures each make law within their lists, with **federal law prevailing** in
a conflict. In outline:

- **Federal** (your MP, federal agencies): external affairs, defence and internal
  security, citizenship, finance including income tax, trade and industry, and
  federal health, education and roads.
- **State** (your ADUN, state departments): land, Islamic law and Malay custom,
  agriculture and forestry, state works and water, and — in Peninsular Malaysia —
  local government.
- **Concurrent** (shared): matters such as town and country planning, drainage and
  irrigation, public health, housing and social welfare.

**Local government sits on the State List**, which is why the council answers to
the state, not Parliament — with two important exceptions below.

## Problem → who to contact first

The rule of thumb: **go to the operational body first; use your representative to
escalate.**

| Your problem | Contact first (operational) | Escalate via |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pothole on a local road, blocked or clogged drain | **Local council (PBT)** | ADUN (state roads) / MP (federal roads) |
| Uncollected rubbish, illegal dumping | **PBT** (or its appointed waste concessionaire) | ADUN |
| Street light out, stray or roaming animals | **PBT** | ADUN |
| Local business licence, signboard, hawker/stall permit | **PBT** (licensing under Act 171) | ADUN |
| Unauthorised building works, by-law breach | **PBT** (Act 133 / by-laws) | ADUN |
| Land title or land-use, state road, state water, religious (Islamic) affairs | **State department / agency** | **ADUN** raises in the assembly |
| Income tax, immigration, citizenship, a federal agency (e.g. LHDN, JPN, Immigration) | **The responsible federal agency** | **MP** raises in Parliament |

## How to actually reach each one

- **Local council (PBT):** use the council's own complaint channel — typically a
  hotline, a website *e-aduan* portal, or a mobile app; many councils route
  complaints through the federal **SISPAA** public-complaints system. The specific
  hotline or app name changes, so check your own council's website.
- **MP / ADUN:** most run a **service centre (pusat khidmat)** in the constituency
  — a help desk that logs residents' issues and forwards them to the relevant
  agency, plus phone and social-media channels. Find your own representative's
  service centre; the address changes each term.

## The exceptions worth knowing

- **Federal Territories** (KL, Putrajaya, Labuan): no ADUN, and the "local council"
  (DBKL, Perbadanan Putrajaya, Perbadanan Labuan) is federally administered, so the
  state-level route does not apply.
- **Sabah and Sarawak** run their **own local-government law**, not the Local
  Government Act 1976 — so the Peninsular structure above is a guide, not a
  one-to-one match there.
- **Refuse collection** is handled by an appointed **concessionaire** in many
  areas rather than the council directly, so a rubbish complaint may route to the
  concessionaire via the council.

## Common mistakes

- **Going to your MP for a local drain.** That is the council's job; the MP can
  only pass it on.
- **Assuming a representative controls the agency.** They raise and escalate; they
  do not run departments.
- **Expecting to vote for your councillor.** Councillors are appointed by the
  state, not elected.
- **Using the Peninsular map in Sabah or Sarawak**, or looking for an ADUN in a
  Federal Territory that has none.

## What's next

- Identify your local council and its complaint channel (hotline / e-aduan / app)
  and lodge local issues there first.
