In a state, there is a Sultan or Yang di-Pertua Negeri, there is a Menteri Besar or Chief Minister, and there is a State Legislative Assembly elected by the people. In Kuala Lumpur, Putrajaya and Labuan, there is none of that. These three places are not states — they are Federal Territories, and they are administered directly by the Federal Government.

That is why a Kuala Lumpur resident votes for a Member of Parliament but never votes for an "ADUN" (state assemblyman); and why the city's chief executive — the Mayor — is appointed, not elected. Here is how this arrangement works.

| Head of local administration | Mayor / President who is **appointed**, not elected |
| Elected representation | Dewan Rakyat (Parliament) only; no DUN seats |

## What is a Federal Territory, and how was it formed?

A Federal Territory is an area placed under the direct administration of the Federal Government and not under any state government. Malaysia has three: Kuala Lumpur, Putrajaya and Labuan.

All three were originally part of a state. Article 1(4) of the Federal Constitution makes clear that the territory of the State of Selangor **excludes** the Federal Territory of Kuala Lumpur (established through the *Constitution (Amendment) (No. 2) Act 1973* [Act A206]) and the Federal Territory of Putrajaya (*Constitution (Amendment) Act 2001* [Act A1095]); while the territory of the State of Sabah **excludes** the Federal Territory of Labuan (*Constitution (Amendment) (No. 2) Act 1984* [Act A585]). The same Article affirms that "all such Federal Territories shall be territories of the Federation."

According to the Federal Territories Department, each territory was formed for a specific role: **Kuala Lumpur** as the national capital, **Putrajaya** as the Federal Administrative Centre, and **Labuan** as the International Business and Financial Centre. Kuala Lumpur was declared a Federal Territory in 1974, Labuan in 1984, and Putrajaya in 2001.

## Why do none of the three have a state government or a DUN?

The reason is simple but important: **only a state has a State Legislative Assembly**. Because a Federal Territory is not a state, no Ruler or Sultan reigns over it, there is no Menteri Besar or Chief Minister, and there is no DUN to make state laws or pass a state budget.

The powers normally held by a state — such as local government and town planning — are held by the Federation for these territories. The Constitution places "all matters relating to the Federal Territories" within the jurisdiction of Parliament; see the [division of Federal–State powers](/en/government/federal-state-division) for the full picture of the Federal List and the State List.

This means that decisions made by a DUN in other states are here made through federal machinery and appointed local authorities.

## Who administers each territory?

Each territory has one local authority that carries out local government functions — licensing, town planning, public health, waste management and development. The head of this body is **appointed**, not elected.

**Kuala Lumpur — Kuala Lumpur City Hall (DBKL).** DBKL is headed by the **Mayor**. According to DBKL, municipal elections have been suspended since 1970, and since Kuala Lumpur attained city status on 1 February 1972, its chief executive has been titled the Mayor of Kuala Lumpur. The Mayor is appointed for a three-year term as a Federal Government appointee.

**Putrajaya — Putrajaya Corporation.** Putrajaya Corporation was established under the **Perbadanan Putrajaya Act 1995 [Act 536]** to administer and manage the Federal Territory of Putrajaya. It carries out all local government functions within the territory in addition to advancing economic, social, infrastructure and residential development.

**Labuan — Labuan Corporation.** Labuan Corporation is the local authority that administers the Federal Territory of Labuan, carrying out the functions of a local government and a local planning authority, and advancing Labuan as a tourism centre, duty-free area and trading hub.

## If there is no DUN, how are residents represented?

Residents of the Federal Territories still have a voice in Parliament — just not at the state level.

**Dewan Rakyat.** Article 46 of the Federal Constitution provides for **13 seats** in the Dewan Rakyat for the three territories: eleven for Kuala Lumpur, one for Labuan and one for Putrajaya. It is these seats that residents vote for in a general election.

**Dewan Negara.** Article 45, in turn, provides for Members of the Dewan Negara (Senators) who are **appointed by the Yang di-Pertuan Agong**: two for the Federal Territory of Kuala Lumpur, one for Labuan and one for Putrajaya.

The key distinction is as follows:

- **Dewan Rakyat** — elected by the people through the ballot.
- **Dewan Negara** — appointed, not elected.
- **State Legislative Assembly (DUN)** — does not exist for the Federal Territories.

So a voter in Cheras votes for their Member of Parliament, but there has never been a ballot for a state representative — because Kuala Lumpur has no [State Legislative Assembly](/en/government/state-assemblies-dun).

## Who coordinates at the federal level?

Above the three local authorities sits the **Federal Territories Department (JWP)**, which operates under the **Prime Minister's Department**. The JWP coordinates policy, development and administration across Kuala Lumpur, Putrajaya and Labuan, and serves as the link between the Federal Government and DBKL, Putrajaya Corporation and Labuan Corporation.

This function was once held by a full ministry — the Ministry of Federal Territories — before being placed as a department under the Prime Minister's Department. What has not changed is the basic principle: these territories are administered from the federal level, not the state level.
