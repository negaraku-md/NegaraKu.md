The three service branches — land, sea and air — operate under a single chain of command rooted in the Yang di-Pertuan Agong as Supreme Commander, but are run day to day by a minister and a civilian apparatus. This is how Malaysia organises its defence: supreme authority in the King, policy control in civilian hands, and execution by a professional military.

## What are the Malaysian Armed Forces and their branches?

The Malaysian Armed Forces (ATM) are the nation's permanent forces. Section 4 of the Armed Forces Act 1972 (Act 77) establishes three permanent service branches:

- **The Malaysian Army (TDM)** — referred to as "the Army" in the Act;
- **The Royal Malaysian Navy (TLDM)** — "the Royal Malaysian Navy"; and
- **The Royal Malaysian Air Force (TUDM)** — "the Royal Malaysian Air Force".

The same Act defines the "regular forces" as comprising all three of these branches. Alongside the permanent forces, there are also "volunteer forces" — including the Askar Wataniah (Territorial Army), the Royal Malaysian Navy Volunteer Reserve and the Royal Malaysian Air Force Volunteer Reserve. The raising of these volunteer forces is provided for under section 195 of Act 77 ("Raising of volunteer forces"), while their composition is provided for under section 196 ("Composition of volunteer forces") — an arrangement that parallels the permanent forces (section 4 establishes them, section 5 sets out their composition). The volunteer forces are under the command of their respective Service Chiefs.

| Branch | Abbreviation | Reference in Act 77 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Malaysian Army | TDM | Led by the Service Chief (Chief of the Army) |
| Royal Malaysian Navy | TLDM | Led by the Service Chief (Chief of the Navy) |
| Royal Malaysian Air Force | TUDM | Led by the Service Chief (Chief of the Air Force) |

Act 77 is a law to "amend and consolidate the law relating to the establishment, command and discipline of the armed forces of Malaysia", and received royal assent on 28 April 1972.

## Who holds supreme command?

Article 41 of the Federal Constitution provides that "the Yang di-Pertuan Agong shall be the Supreme Commander of the armed forces of the Federation". This makes the King the constitutional head of the armed forces.

On the professional side, the highest-ranking officer of the military is the Chief of Defence Force (referred to in the Constitution as the *Chief of Defence Force*), appointed by the Yang di-Pertuan Agong under Article 137(3)(c). Each service branch is in turn led by its own Service Chief.

This chain separates two things: the supreme authority that is constitutional and symbolic, vested in the King, from the day-to-day professional leadership vested in appointed military officers.

## How does civilian control over the military work?

Article 137 of the Constitution establishes the Armed Forces Council, which "shall, under the general authority of the Yang di-Pertuan Agong, be responsible for the command, discipline and administration of, and all other matters relating to, the armed forces, other than matters relating to their operational use".

Article 137(3) sets out the membership of the Council, which includes:

- **The Minister of Defence** — as Chairman;
- **a member representing the Conference of Rulers**, appointed by the Conference of Rulers;
- **the Chief of Defence Force**, appointed by the Yang di-Pertuan Agong;
- **a civilian member** performing the duties of the Secretary General of Defence, as Secretary of the Council;
- **a senior officer** of the armed forces, together with senior representatives of the navy and the air force; and
- **additional members** (military or civilian) appointed by the Yang di-Pertuan Agong.

This arrangement means that the body which commands and administers the military is chaired by a minister — a politician answerable to Parliament and the Cabinet. This is the basis of the principle of civilian control over the armed forces in the Malaysian system.

## What is the role of the Ministry of Defence (MINDEF)?

The Ministry of Defence (MINDEF) is the ministry that implements the National Defence Policy and manages all defence affairs as well as the resources allocated to the ATM to defend the country.

According to MINDEF, the ministry is led by the Minister of Defence, assisted by a Deputy Minister, and consists of two principal services: the **Public Service**, led by the Secretary General, and the **Malaysian Armed Forces**, led by the Chief of Defence Force. The ministry's scope of responsibility covers defence, national security, the land, sea and air forces, intelligence services, national service and veterans' affairs.

In other words, MINDEF provides the policy and civilian administration layer, while the ATM is the operational and combat layer.

## What is Malaysia's defence posture?

Malaysia's defence posture is set out in general terms in the Defence White Paper (DWP), which was tabled in the Dewan Rakyat on **2 December 2019** — the first time such a defence policy document was released to the public. The DWP outlines three pillars of the defence strategy:

1. **Concentric Deterrence** — a layered approach to defence;
2. **Comprehensive Defence** — encompassing the concept of Comprehensive Defence (HANRUH), which emphasises the participation of the people in the nation's defence ecosystem; and
3. **Credible Partnerships** — bilateral and multilateral defence relations, including with neighbouring countries.

The DWP also underscores Malaysia's position as a maritime nation that serves as a "bridge" connecting the Asia-Pacific region and the Indian Ocean. The document is built on the principles of transparency, inclusiveness and an evidence-based approach. ## Where do national service and public participation fit in?

The concept of Comprehensive Defence (HANRUH) positions defence as a shared responsibility, not solely a matter for the military. In this arrangement, the ATM acts as the first line, the volunteer forces as the second line, and other agencies and the public as the third line.

Public participation is expressed through volunteer forces such as the Askar Wataniah provided for in Act 77, as well as through citizen training programmes that have been carried out from time to time. The specific details of these programmes change according to prevailing policy, and no absolute figures are stated here because they need to be verified against current sources.

## What's next

Malaysia's defence framework is layered: supreme constitutional authority in the Yang di-Pertuan Agong, policy control in the Minister of Defence and the Armed Forces Council, and execution by the three ATM branches under Act 77. MINDEF also refers to long-term strategic planning documents to guide the development of defence capabilities.
