Every ringgit spent by the Federal Government — from the salary of a nurse at a district hospital to the interest paid to bondholders in London — passes through the same accounting door: the Treasury of Malaysia. The Ministry of Finance is the ministry that holds that door, and since early 2024 it has held it under a set of numerical limits enshrined in law.

This page explains what the ministry actually does: how the Budget is prepared and approved, who heads the Treasury, how large the national debt is and what its limits are, and which departments and agencies sit beneath it.

## What does the Ministry of Finance actually do?

The Ministry of Finance is responsible for both sides of the country's balance sheet: the revenue coming in and the expenditure going out. It formulates fiscal policy, prepares the Federal Budget, manages the raising and repayment of government borrowings, controls government accounting and the treasury, and oversees the financial management of the other ministries, departments and statutory bodies.

It is also the lead ministry for the national financial system. Through its agencies, the Ministry of Finance oversees the direct and indirect tax authorities, the capital market, deposit insurance, and public-sector financing. The Public Finance and Fiscal Responsibility Act 2023 (Act 850) — the law that now binds the ministry's fiscal management — encapsulates this mandate in four principles of responsible fiscal management: ensuring macroeconomic stability, achieving a sustainable fiscal balance, maintaining a prudent level of debt, and managing fiscal risks effectively.

## Who heads the Treasury?

There are two levels of leadership that are often confused. The political level is headed by the Minister of Finance — currently held by the Prime Minister himself as Minister of Finance I — assisted by a Minister of Finance II and a Deputy Minister. The civil-service level is headed by the **Secretary General of Treasury**, the senior officer who heads the Treasury of Malaysia and chairs the boards of many financial statutory bodies.

Beneath the Secretary General of Treasury are three Deputy Secretaries General — for Policy, Management, and Investment — who oversee the core divisions of the Treasury.

| Core Treasury division | Role |
| --- | --- |
| National Budget Office | Prepares and coordinates the annual Budget |
| Tax Division | Direct and indirect tax policy |
| Fiscal and Economics Division | Fiscal analysis, projections and targets |
| Government Procurement Division | Public procurement policy and governance |
| Office of the Registrar of Credit Reporting Agencies | Regulation of credit reporting agencies |
| Statutory Body Strategic Management Division | Oversight of statutory bodies |

The Treasury also operates the state Federal Treasuries in Sabah and Sarawak, as well as the Special Commissioners of Income Tax and the Customs Appeal Tribunal, which hear tax disputes.

The official MOF portal lists it administratively in the "Departments" group alongside the Treasury, Customs, the Accountant General's Department and JPPH; in law it is a statutory body.

Two agencies here are most often mentioned by the public because both collect taxes: **LHDN** for direct tax and **Customs** for indirect tax. Both sit under the same ministry but are separate authorities — see [LHDN](/government/lhdn) and [Royal Malaysian Customs Department](/government/rmcd-kastam).

## How is the Federal Budget prepared and approved?

The Budget is not a single speech; it is a months-long process that ends with a piece of legislation. The National Budget Office coordinates spending proposals from every ministry, the Fiscal and Economics Division projects revenue and growth, and the Minister of Finance finalises the position before Budget Day.

Its passage through Parliament looks like this:

1. **Tabling (Budget Day).** The Minister of Finance tables the Budget speech and the **Supply Bill** in the Dewan Rakyat. Budget 2026 was tabled on 10 October 2025.
2. **Policy stage.** The House debates the Budget as a whole, then votes to pass it at the policy stage.
3. **Committee stage.** The House scrutinises the appropriations ministry by ministry. It was at this stage, for example, that the allocations for three particular ministries were approved on 10 November 2025.
4. **Dewan Negara.** After being passed by the Dewan Rakyat, it is sent to the Dewan Negara.
5. **Royal assent and the Supply Act.** Once assented to, it becomes the Supply Act, which authorises spending for that year.

One important constitutional feature: the Supply Bill is a **money bill**. This means only a Minister may bring it, it must originate in the Dewan Rakyat, and the Dewan Negara may only delay it — not block it. The details of the Dewan Rakyat's financial powers under Articles 67 and 68 of the Federal Constitution are explained in [Dewan Rakyat](/government/dewan-rakyat) and [How a bill becomes law](/law/how-a-bill-becomes-law).

## Where does the money go in Budget 2026?

Budget 2026 is a total mobilisation of RM470 billion, up from RM452 billion in 2025. That RM470 billion figure is not all direct Federal Government spending — it includes the investments of government-linked investment companies (GLICs) and public-private partnership projects that do not pass through the ordinary budget.

| Component | Allocation |
| --- | ---: |
| Operating expenditure | RM338.2 billion |
| Development expenditure | RM81.0 billion |
| **Core Federal Budget** | **RM419.2 billion** |
| GLIC investment | RM30.0 billion |
| Public-private partnerships (PPP) | RM10.0 billion |
| Federal statutory bodies and MKD companies | RM10.8 billion |
| **Total overall mobilisation** | **RM470.0 billion** |

On the revenue side, the government is targeting collections of RM343.1 billion for 2026, up from an estimated RM334.1 billion in 2025. The gap between revenue and expenditure is the fiscal deficit, which is targeted at 3.5% of GDP for 2026 — down from a target of 3.8% in 2025.

## How large is the Federal debt, and what are its limits?

When spending exceeds revenue, the government borrows to cover the gap — and borrowings accumulated over the years become the Federal debt. The debt-to-GDP ratio is the most frequently monitored measure. By the end of March 2026, that ratio had fallen to **63.1% of GDP**, from 65.2% at the end of 2025 — the result of fiscal consolidation that has narrowed the deficit for several consecutive years.

According to the figures stated in Budget 2026, the Federal fiscal deficit has fallen in stages: 5.5% of GDP in 2022, 5.0% in 2023, 4.1% in 2024, and a targeted 3.8% in 2025 and 3.5% in 2026.

The government cannot borrow at will. Four principal acts govern its borrowing, each with its own gazetted instruments and limits:

| Borrowing act | Instrument | Statutory limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Loan (Local) Act 1959 [Act 637] | MGS, MGII | Total of MGS + MGII + MITB not exceeding 65% of GDP |
| Government Funding Act 1983 [Act 275] | MITB, Market Loans, Project Loans | (included in the 65% of GDP limit above) |
| External Loans Act 1963 [Act 403] | Offshore external loans | Not exceeding RM35 billion |
| Treasury Bills (Local) Act 1946 [Act 188] | MTB | Not exceeding RM10 billion |

Note: MGS is Malaysian Government Securities; MGII is Malaysian Government Investment Issues (Shariah-compliant); MITB is Malaysian Islamic Treasury Bills; MTB is Malaysian Treasury Bills. The numerical limit of each act should be verified against the text of the acts and the Fiscal Outlook 2026 document (Section 4).

These acts bind the government to borrow solely to finance development expenditure and to refinance existing debt — not for day-to-day operating spending. The bulk of Federal debt is denominated in ringgit and raised from the domestic capital market; offshore external loans remain well below the RM35 billion threshold.

## What does Act 850 require?

Before 2024, the statutory debt limit was temporarily raised from 60% to 65% of GDP under the Temporary Measures for Government Financing (Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID-19)) Act 2020, and the ceiling of the COVID-19 Fund was raised from RM65 billion to RM110 billion to finance the pandemic stimulus and economic recovery packages. As part of the post-pandemic reforms, the government introduced the **Public Finance and Fiscal Responsibility Act 2023 (Act 850)**, which came into force on 1 January 2024.

Act 850 changed how fiscal management is reported and constrained. It sets fiscal objectives measured by **numerical values** in its First Schedule:

| Fiscal objective (First Schedule, Act 850) | Numerical value |
| --- | ---: |
| Minimum annual development expenditure (% of GDP) | 3% |
| Fiscal balance (% of GDP) | -3% |
| Debt level (% of GDP) | 60% |
| Financial guarantees (% of GDP) | 25% |

Subsection 18(1) provides that total government debt may not exceed the percentage of GDP stated in the First Schedule — that is, the medium-term target of 60%. Note that this is Act 850's **medium-term target**, distinct from the 65% limit still gazetted under the individual borrowing acts; the current ratio of 63.1% is within the 65% statutory limit while trending towards the 60% target. All four of these fiscal objectives — including the financial-guarantee ceiling of 25% of GDP — are to be achieved over a medium-term period of three to five years.

Act 850 also established the **Fiscal Policy Committee** (Section 28) to oversee the implementation of fiscal policy, and it mandates the publication of various fiscal statements including a fiscal risk statement. Its purpose, as stated in the Act's principles, is transparency and intergenerational equity — so that today's borrowing does not compromise the welfare of future generations.

## Decision framework: which agency for your matter?

Because so many of the country's financial functions gather under one ministry, people often contact the wrong party. Use this mapping:

- **Income tax, stamp duty, real property gains tax, e-Invoicing** → [LHDN](/government/lhdn), not the Ministry of Finance directly.
- **Sales and service tax (SST), excise duties, import customs** → [Royal Malaysian Customs Department](/government/rmcd-kastam).
- **Government payments and accounting, Federal financial statements** → the Accountant General's Department.
- **Private retirement savings (EPF contributions)** → the Employees Provident Fund.
- **Bank deposit protection** → the Malaysia Deposit Insurance Corporation (PIDM).
- **Capital market investment, broker complaints** → the Securities Commission Malaysia.
- **Monetary policy, interest rates, banking regulation** → Bank Negara Malaysia.
- **Overall Budget and fiscal policy** → the Ministry of Finance (Treasury).

## Common mistakes

**"The Ministry of Finance collects taxes."** The ministry sets tax policy, but collection is carried out by its agencies — LHDN for direct tax and Customs for indirect tax. Sending a tax query to the ministry will only be redirected.

**"The RM470 billion Budget 2026 figure is all government spending."** That total is the overall mobilisation, which also includes GLIC investment and PPP projects. The core Federal Budget — operating and development expenditure — is RM419.2 billion.

**"The national debt limit is 60%."** The 60% of GDP medium-term target under Act 850 is not the same thing as the 65% of GDP statutory limit still gazetted under the borrowing acts. The current ratio may sit between those two figures.

**"Bank Negara is part of the Treasury."** Bank Negara is an agency under the ministry but is an operationally autonomous central bank. Policy rate decisions are made by the central bank, not the Minister of Finance.

**"The Budget becomes law as soon as it is tabled."** Tabling is only the beginning. The Budget must pass through the policy stage and the committee stage in the Dewan Rakyat, then the Dewan Negara and royal assent, before it becomes the Supply Act.
