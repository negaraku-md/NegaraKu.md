**Bahasa Malaysia:** Rukun Negara · **English:** National Principles · **中文:** 国家原则

Every Monday morning, in thousands of schools across Malaysia, pupils stand
upright and recite the same pledge — five lines that begin with "Belief in
God". Many have memorised it since childhood. But fewer know that those
five lines are not merely a school slogan — they were born in the wake of
one of the most difficult episodes in the nation's history, and were
deliberately crafted to reunite a divided people.

This is the Rukun Negara: Malaysia's national philosophy, proclaimed on the
very same day the nation celebrates its independence.

## What exactly is the Rukun Negara?

The Rukun Negara is **Malaysia's national ideology or philosophy**, built
upon five core principles as a guide to living in society. It is not a law
that can prosecute anyone, and it is not merely a motto. It is more
accurately understood as a **shared pledge** — a statement of values held
in common by a people of many ethnicities and religions.

According to the National Security Council (MKN), the Rukun Negara "is
Malaysia's national philosophy built upon five core principles as a guide
to living in society". It becomes the anchor, guide and practice of all
citizens toward nurturing and strengthening the spirit of unity.

The full pledge of the Rukun Negara actually has **two parts**:

1. **The national aspirations** — five great goals to be achieved (the
   preamble).
2. **The five principles** — the foundations and anchors that serve as the
   pillars for achieving those aspirations.

What is most often memorised and recited is the second part — the five
principles. But without the first part, the context is lost.

## What are the five principles of the Rukun Negara?

This is the core that every citizen must remember. In official order, the
five principles of the Rukun Negara are:

| No. | Principle | Brief meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Belief in God** | Acknowledging the existence of God as the foundation of living in society and in a nation |
| 2 | **Loyalty to King and Country** | Faithful allegiance to the Yang di-Pertuan Agong, the Rulers and the nation |
| 3 | **Supremacy of the Constitution** | Accepting the Federal Constitution as the supreme law of the land |
| 4 | **Rule of Law** | All citizens are equal before the law; justice is administered fairly |
| 5 | **Courtesy and Morality** | Upholding good conduct, courtesy and noble character in social interaction |

This order is important and deliberate. It begins with the relationship
with God, rises to loyalty to the nation and the institution of the
Rulers, then to the legal pillars (the Constitution and the law), and ends
with the personal responsibility of every individual — courtesy and
morality.

## Why was the Rukun Negara born?

The Rukun Negara was not born in peaceful times. It was born as a direct
response to the **13 May 1969 incident** — racial riots that seriously
threatened the nation's unity and harmony.

After that incident, the parliamentary system of government was suspended
and an emergency administrative body, the **National Operations Council
(MAGERAN)**, was established to restore peace. MAGERAN was chaired by the
then Deputy Prime Minister, **Tun Abdul Razak Hussein**.

Under MAGERAN, Tun Abdul Razak established three bodies to restore unity:

- **The National Goodwill Council** — to rekindle the spirit of goodwill
  between the races.
- **The Department of National Unity** — a permanent body to coordinate
  unity efforts.
- **The National Consultative Council (MPN)** — a multi-party forum for
  discussion on issues of unity and the nation's direction.

According to MKN, "as a result of the discussions held by the MPN, the
Rukun Negara was born". In other words, the Rukun Negara is not the
formulation of a single individual — it is the product of the
deliberations of a consultative council representing many layers of
society.

## When and by whom was it proclaimed?

The Rukun Negara was officially proclaimed by the **Yang di-Pertuan Agong
on 31 August 1970**. That date was no coincidence — it was chosen to
coincide with the **celebration of Malaysia's 13th anniversary of
independence**.

According to the Official Portal of the Government of Malaysia
(MyGovernment), the proclamation was made by the **fourth Yang
di-Pertuan Agong, Tuanku Ismail Nasiruddin Shah**. By placing the
proclamation on Independence Day, the Rukun Negara was symbolically bound
to the very meaning of independence itself: a free nation determined to
build unity out of diversity.

| Event | Date / Detail |
| --- | --- |
| Triggering incident | 13 May 1969 |
| Drafting body | National Consultative Council (under MAGERAN) |
| MAGERAN chairman | Tun Abdul Razak Hussein (the then Deputy Prime Minister) |
| Official proclamation | 31 August 1970 |
| Proclaimed by | The fourth Yang di-Pertuan Agong, Tuanku Ismail Nasiruddin Shah |
| Marking | The 13th anniversary of independence |

## What are the five aspirations behind the five principles?

Before listing the five principles, the Rukun Negara pledge states five
**aspirations** or goals that the nation seeks to achieve. This part is
often forgotten because it is rarely recited aloud, but it explains *why*
the five principles exist.

The nation's five aspirations are to:

- Achieve **a more perfect unity** among the whole of society.
- Preserve **a democratic way of life**.
- Create **a just society** in which the wealth of the nation can be
  enjoyed together fairly and equitably.
- Guarantee **a liberal approach** toward the rich and diverse cultural
  traditions.
- Build **a progressive society** that will make use of modern science and
  technology.

Notice how the aspirations and the principles complement each other. The
aspirations set the *destination* (a united, just, democratic, progressive
society); the principles set the *value foundation* for getting there.
Reading one without the other gives only half the picture.

## How does the Rukun Negara differ from the Constitution and the anthem Negaraku?

Many confuse these national symbols. Each has a different role:

| Symbol / Document | What it is | Legal force? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Rukun Negara** | National philosophy and pledge (5 principles) | No — a values guide, not an enforceable law |
| **Federal Constitution** | The supreme law of the land | Yes — binds all other laws |
| **Anthem Negaraku** | The national anthem | Protected under national anthem law |
| **Jalur Gemilang** | The national flag | An official symbol of the nation |

Interestingly, the third principle of the Rukun Negara — **Supremacy of
the Constitution** — directly refers to the Federal Constitution. So the
Rukun Negara actually *honours* the Constitution as the supreme law rather
than competing with it. The Rukun Negara is a statement of values; the
Constitution is enforceable law.

## How can each principle be understood more deeply?

For new citizens, students or anyone who has memorised without
understanding, here is how to grasp the intent behind each principle:

- **Belief in God.** This principle recognises that Malaysian society is a
  religious society. It emphasises *belief in God*, not the imposition of a
  single religion — consistent with the position of Islam as the religion
  of the Federation while freedom of religion is guaranteed by the
  Constitution.
- **Loyalty to King and Country.** It demands faithful allegiance to the
  institution of the Constitutional Rulers — the Yang di-Pertuan Agong at
  the Federal level and the Rulers or the Yang di-Pertua Negeri at the
  state level — and to the nation.
- **Supremacy of the Constitution.** Accepting the Federal Constitution as
  the supreme law. "Supremacy" means nobility or eminence of standing — the
  Constitution overrides all other laws.
- **Rule of Law.** Every person, regardless of standing, is subject to the
  same law. No one is above the law.
- **Courtesy and Morality.** The most personal principle — it demands good
  conduct, courtesy and noble character in daily interaction, including
  respect for the beliefs and cultures of other races.

## Decision frame: how to explain the Rukun Negara according to the audience?

The best way to explain the Rukun Negara depends on who is asking:

| If you are explaining to… | Focus on… |
| --- | --- |
| A schoolchild | Memorising the five principles in order + a brief meaning of each |
| A new citizen | The historical context (13 May 1969) and the goal of unity |
| A foreign visitor | Its role as a pledge of unity for a multi-ethnic nation |
| A civic / academic discussion | Both full parts — the five aspirations and the five principles — and their relationship with the Constitution |

The key: do not stop at memorising the five lines. Mention at least *why*
it exists (unity after 1969) so that its meaning stays alive.

## Common mistakes about the Rukun Negara

- **Stating the first principle as "Belief in religion".** The official
  wording verified by government sources (MKN and MyGov) is **"Belief in
  God"** — not religion. This difference in wording matters.
- **Reversing the order.** The official order begins with Belief in God and
  ends with Courtesy and Morality. Placing Rule of Law or Supremacy of the
  Constitution in the wrong position is a common mistake.
- **Assuming the Rukun Negara is part of the Constitution.** It is not. The
  Rukun Negara is a national pledge and philosophy; the Federal
  Constitution is a separate, supreme legal document.
- **Confusing the date with Independence Day 1957.** The Rukun Negara was
  proclaimed on 31 August **1970**, not 1957 — even though both fall on
  31 August. The 1970 date was chosen *to mark* the 13th anniversary of
  independence.
- **Equating "principles" with "aspirations".** The five principles are the
  value foundation; the five aspirations are the nation's goals. The two
  are different but complement each other in the full pledge.

## Where is the Rukun Negara recited today?

The Rukun Negara remains alive in Malaysia's civic life:

- **School assemblies** — recited together, especially at formal assembly
  days.
- **School exercise books** — the five principles are commonly printed on
  the back cover of pupils' exercise books.
- **Official government ceremonies** — the Rukun Negara pledge is often
  recited at national ceremonies and National Day celebrations.
- **Unity programmes** — it serves as a basic reference in the activities of
  the Department of National Unity and National Integration.
