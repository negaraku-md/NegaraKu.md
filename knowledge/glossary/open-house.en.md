**Bahasa Malaysia:** Rumah Terbuka · **English:** Open House · **中文:** 开放门户 (敞开家门待客)

Picture three neighbours on the same street — one Malay, one Chinese, one Indian — and across the year each of their homes opens in turn: Syawal at the first house, Chinese New Year at the second, Deepavali at the third. You don't need a special invitation to drop by. You just need to know that on that day, that door is open to everyone.

That is the **open house** — and it is not merely how Malaysia marks a celebration, but how one community's celebration turns into an invitation for everyone.

## It began as an Aidilfitri visit

The practice did not begin as a cross-ethnic concept. According to Tinta Minda BERNAMA, the open house is closely tied to the celebration of Hari Raya Aidilfitri — an expression of gratitude after a month of fasting during Ramadan, and at the same time a space for seeking forgiveness and strengthening family ties. What sets it apart from an ordinary visit: the host deliberately invites many guests and serves food at a set time, rather than waiting for guests to arrive one or two at a time.

From that foundation, the practice grew. Colleagues, neighbours and acquaintances of other ethnicities and religions were also invited to drop by — and, over time, "open house" became a label in its own right for this style of visiting, applied to other celebrations as well.

## Same pattern, different celebrations

Today, the open house is no longer exclusive to Aidilfitri. The same pattern — a wide invitation, food served, guests of various ethnicities welcomed — has also become a feature of other celebrations in Malaysia:

| Celebration | Associated ethnic group/religion |
|---|---|
| Hari Raya Aidilfitri | Muslim community |
| Chinese New Year | Chinese community |
| Deepavali | Hindu/Indian community |
| Christmas | Christian community |
| Gawai (Sarawak) | Dayak community |
| Kaamatan (Sabah) | Kadazan-Dusun-Murut community |

Guests who attend need not share the host's religion or ethnicity. That is why the open house is often described as a "cross-ethnic" practice — it is built on a particular community's celebration, but its invitation reaches beyond that community itself.

## Not merely a private-home practice

The open house in Malaysia also exists on a scale far larger than a single residential home. The federal government, state governments, public agencies and companies also hold their own open house events.

The Prime Minister's Office (PMO) reports that the Prime Minister attends a series of **Rumah Terbuka Malaysia Madani** across various states throughout the festive season — Aidilfitri, Deepavali and others — together with Cabinet ministers. The Department of Information Malaysia likewise reports similar events at the state government level as an effort to reach out to the people. RTM, for its part, reports statements by state government officials that these events are "for ethnic unity" — a space where guests of various ethnicities gather together.

This places the open house in a rather unique position: it began as an informal social practice among neighbours, but has also become a format for official government events.

## Its link to national unity policy

The Ministry of National Unity (KPN), through its official website, describes its mandate as strengthening identity, social integration and harmony among adherents of different religions — carried out through frameworks such as the National Unity Action Plan 2021-2030. KPN does not treat the open house as a specific programme on its portal, but it does oversee community-level programmes such as Rukun Tetangga that share a similar goal: strengthening ties among residents of different ethnicities at the grassroots level.

The open house, therefore, functions as a social practice aligned with the goals of that unity policy — even though it itself grew organically out of family and religious tradition, rather than being designed as a government programme.

## Common misconceptions

Some people take the open house to be a generic term for "inviting guests over for a meal" — whereas its distinguishing feature is a scope of invitation deliberately widened across ethnicities and religions, not merely a visit among family or peers.

Another misconception: assuming the open house is solely an Aidilfitri practice. While that is its root, the government's own official reports now cover the Rumah Terbuka Deepavali Madani, Chinese New Year open houses and others under the same label.

## What's next

The open house is most often mentioned alongside [Hari Raya Aidilfitri](/en/arts-culture/hari-raya-aidilfitri) and [Deepavali](/en/arts-culture/deepavali), and frequently coincides with waves of [balik kampung](/en/glossary/balik-kampung) ahead of major celebrations. For the policy backdrop underlying practices like this, see [Rukun Negara](/en/malaysia/rukun-negara) — the national principles that, among other things, emphasise unity among a multi-ethnic people.
