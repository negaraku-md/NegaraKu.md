**Bahasa Malaysia:** Merdeka · **English:** Independence / Freedom · **中文:** 独立

9.30am, 31 August 1957. Tunku Abdul Rahman stood at Stadium Merdeka
and proclaimed one word — seven times — welcomed by more than 20,000
people in attendance. That word was **"Merdeka"**.

Many assume this word was born at that moment. In truth, it is far
older than the Federation of Malaya itself.

## Its meaning, according to the dictionary

Kamus Dewan (Dewan Bahasa dan Pustaka) defines **merdeka** as:

> free (from colonisation, confinement, protectorate status, and so
> on), released (from ransom, claims), standing on one's own, not
> dependent on another.

Notice the scope — it is not confined to politics. A person can be
"merdeka daripada hutang" (free from debt), an organisation can be
"merdeka" from another party's control. The political meaning (free
from colonisation) is merely one specific application of this
broader meaning.

## A word older than 1957

According to general linguistic references, the word "merdeka" is
believed to originate from the Sanskrit **"maharddhika"**, meaning
rich, glorious and powerful — not "free" in the modern sense. It has
cognates in other related languages of the region: **mardika**
(Javanese) and **maradeka** (Buginese).

Its meaning shifted gradually — from "a rich, high-standing person"
to "a person who is unbound, not enslaved" — before finally becoming
the modern Malay word for freedom and sovereignty. No official
Malaysian government source publishes this etymological history in
detail, so the details above are drawn from general linguistic
references, not government documents.

## Three forms in the Malay language

| Bentuk | Jenis kata | Contoh penggunaan |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Merdeka** | Adjective | "Tanah Melayu merdeka pada 1957" (Malaya became independent in 1957) |
| **Kemerdekaan** | Noun | "Kemerdekaan Persekutuan Tanah Melayu" (Independence of the Federation of Malaya) |
| **Memerdekakan** | Verb | "British memerdekakan Tanah Melayu" (Britain granted Malaya its independence) |

All three share the same root, but their grammatical roles differ —
a common point of confusion for second-language speakers.

## The moment it became a national word

According to Arkib Negara Malaysia, the official proclamation took
place at Stadium Merdeka at 9.30am, 31 August 1957. Tunku Abdul
Rahman Putra, Prime Minister of the Federation of Malaya, proclaimed
"Merdeka" seven times after reading the declaration of independence,
welcomed by more than 20,000 attendees including the Malay Rulers,
foreign guests and members of the Federal Cabinet.

The ceremony also included the call to prayer (azan), the raising of
the Federation flag accompanied by the national anthem, and a
101-gun salute. The representative of Queen Elizabeth II, the Duke
of Gloucester, handed the constitutional instrument to the Prime
Minister — a symbol of the end of British rule.

This event was preceded by the London Agreement signed on
8 February 1956, which set 31 August 1957 as the date of
independence, according to records from the National Security
Council (Majlis Keselamatan Negara, MKN).

## A name that lives on

The word did not stay confined to history books — it is now the
name of places and buildings:

- **Stadium Merdeka** — the site of the 1957 proclamation, in Kuala
  Lumpur.
- **Dataran Merdeka** — the historic square near Stadium Merdeka.
- **Menara Merdeka 118** — named for its proximity to Stadium
  Merdeka, in honour of that historic site.
- **The month of August** — often called "Bulan Kemerdekaan"
  (Independence Month), the month of Hari Merdeka celebrations each
  year.

## Common mistakes

- **Assuming this word was coined for 1957.** Its roots are far
  older than the Federation of Malaya; 1957 was only the moment it
  became Malaysia's national word.
- **Equating Hari Merdeka with Hari Malaysia.** 31 August marks
  Malaya's independence from Britain; 16 September marks the
  formation of Malaysia when Sabah, Sarawak and Singapore joined the
  federation six years later — two different events.
- **Limiting its meaning to the political context alone.** Kamus
  Dewan itself defines it more broadly — free from any form of
  bondage, not solely colonisation.
