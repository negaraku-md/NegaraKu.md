**Bahasa Malaysia:** Gotong-royong · **English:** Communal mutual aid · **中文:** 互助合作（甘榜守望相助）

On a weekend in a kampung, a group of neighbours gathers early in the morning
with hoes, brooms and a big cooking pot. No one is paid, there is no official
task list — only a shared understanding that the work belongs to everyone.
That is gotong-royong: not merely the work that gets done, but the spirit that
drives it.

## The real meaning

Kamus Dewan defines *gotong-royong* succinctly as "mutual help (assisting one
another), working together". Those two ideas are its foundation: people help
one another, and they do so together for one shared purpose.

What sets gotong-royong apart from ordinary work is that it is **voluntary and
unpaid**. What is contributed is effort, time and skill — not money or paid
services. The reward is social: a big task becomes light, and the bonds
between neighbours grow closer.

| Feature | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Nature** | Voluntary, unpaid, of one's own free will |
| **Who takes part** | Neighbours, community members or the residents of an area |
| **Purpose** | Completing a collective task of mutual benefit |
| **Underlying spirit** | Social responsibility, neighbourliness and muhibah |

## From the kampung to the city

Gotong-royong is most deeply rooted in the setting of the
[kampung](/en/glossary/kampung), where houses are close together and the ties
between residents are tight. The term is used widely across the Malay world,
and is often associated with rural life before urbanisation changed the way
people socialise.

Its form varies according to need. Among the most common:

- **Area clean-ups** — sweeping roads, clearing drains, cutting grass and
  tidying the grounds of a surau, mosque or community hall.
- **Event preparations** — putting up tents, cooking and arranging tables for a
  wedding feast or an [open house](/en/glossary/open-house) celebration.
- **Building and repairing** — erecting or repairing houses, small bridges and
  shared public facilities.
- **Disaster recovery** — clearing mud and belongings after a flood, a form of
  gotong-royong still often seen in Malaysia today.

Although urban lifestyles have put distance between neighbours, the spirit of
gotong-royong has not disappeared. It resurfaces in housing-estate clean-up
programmes, residents' association activities, and volunteer movements during
emergencies — proof that it is not merely an old [adat](/en/glossary/adat), but
a value that is still alive.

## Common mistakes

- **Thinking gotong-royong means paid group work.** Its essence is willingness
  and the voluntary spirit; the moment there is payment or coercion, it is no
  longer gotong-royong in the true sense.
- **Assuming it is only physical labour.** Cooking, organising events and
  donating materials are also part of gotong-royong — a contribution need not
  take the form of heavy manual work.
- **Equating it entirely with "teamwork" at the workplace.** Both involve
  cooperation, but gotong-royong is rooted in community and willingness, not
  formal roles or an employer's instructions.
