**Bahasa Malaysia:** Rakyat · **English:** The people / populace · **中文:** 人民

Every time someone says "rakyat Malaysia", "hak rakyat" or "suara rakyat",
they are using a word that has **never been defined** in the Federal
Constitution. That is not a flaw — it shows that "rakyat" and "warganegara"
are two different words carrying two different functions in Bahasa Malaysia.

## What "rakyat" means

According to **Kamus Dewan** (Dewan Bahasa dan Pustaka), via the Pusat
Rujukan Persuratan Melayu (PRPM) portal, "rakyat" carries several meanings:

| Meaning | Description |
| --- | --- |
| Primary meaning | The entire population of a country, as a condition for establishing a government |
| Secondary meaning | Followers, subordinates |
| Secondary meaning | Common folk, the general public, ordinary people |
| Secondary meaning | Fighting forces, troops |

It is this primary meaning that is most often used in the modern context —
"rakyat" as the entire population or the general public of a country,
without reference to any particular individual's legal status.

## "Rakyat" versus "warganegara"

This is the point most often misunderstood. "Rakyat" is a socio-
political and general term; it describes the general public as a
collective. "Warganegara", on the other hand, is a **legal** term that
determines whether an individual is a lawful member of Malaysia, complete
with the rights and obligations that come with it.

Part III of the [Federal Constitution](/en/law/federal-constitution)
(Articles 14 to 22) sets out in detail how a person becomes a citizen —
by operation of law, registration or naturalisation — as well as how
citizenship can be lost. There is no equivalent provision defining
"rakyat" as a legal category.

In other words: a foreign tourist in Malaysia is also included in the
common meaning of "rakyat" (the people present in a place), but is not a
"warganegara" of Malaysia. Conversely, a Malaysian warganegara who
migrates abroad remains a warganegara in the legal sense, even though
they are no longer among the "rakyat" referred to in the local context.

## Where the term appears

"Rakyat" is one of the most frequently used words in the names of
Malaysian government bodies and programmes:

- **Dewan Rakyat** — the House of Parliament whose members are elected,
  as distinct from the Dewan Negara whose members are appointed. See
  [Dewan Rakyat](/en/glossary/dewan-rakyat).
- **Rukun Negara** — the national pledge proclaimed by the Yang
  di-Pertuan Agong on 31 August 1970, to be "held, guided by and
  practised by the whole rakyat of Malaysia", according to the National
  Security Council (MKN).
- **Various government aid and payment schemes** that have used the
  word "rakyat" in their names from time to time, as a way of referring
  to recipients in general without limiting them to a particular legal
  status.

## Common misconceptions

**"Rakyat is a constitutional term just like warganegara."** No.
"Warganegara" is defined and elaborated in Part III of the Federal
Constitution; "rakyat" is a general language term that has no
equivalent legal definition.

**"Rakyat and warganegara are interchangeable."** In everyday
conversation, yes — both are often used to mean much the same thing.
But in a legal or administrative context (for example, the eligibility
conditions for some aid or registration), "warganegara" is the word
that carries legal effect, while "rakyat" does not.

## What's next

If you need to verify a person's legal status as a lawful member of
Malaysia — for the purposes of an application, registration or research
— refer to the "warganegara" provisions under Part III of the
[Federal Constitution](/en/law/federal-constitution), not the general
usage of the word "rakyat".
