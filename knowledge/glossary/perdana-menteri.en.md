Malaysia can change its Prime Minister without a single vote being cast by the public — because the office is not granted by voters, but by the Yang di-Pertuan Agong to whoever is seen as capable of assembling a Dewan Rakyat majority. That is why Malaysia witnessed several changes of Prime Minister between 2018 and 2022 without a single new general election deciding it midway through that period.

The Prime Minister is Malaysia's **head of government** — the person who leads the day-to-day executive power, chairs the Cabinet, and serves as the principal adviser to the Yang di-Pertuan Agong, who is the head of state. Understanding this office means understanding three interlinked institutions: the Agong who appoints, the Dewan Rakyat that confers legitimacy, and the Cabinet that is led.

## Who appoints the Prime Minister, and how?

The Prime Minister is **not** elected directly by the people. The people vote for the 222 members of the Dewan Rakyat in a general election; the Prime Minister is then appointed by the Yang di-Pertuan Agong.

The basis is Article 43(1) and 43(2)(a) of the Federal Constitution. Article 43(1) provides that "The Yang di-Pertuan Agong shall appoint a Jemaah Menteri (Cabinet of Ministers) to advise him in the exercise of his functions." Article 43(2)(a) then provides that His Majesty shall **first** appoint as Prime Minister:

> "…a member of the House of Representatives who in his judgment is likely to command the confidence of the majority of the members of that House."

Three key elements in that sentence:

- **A member of the Dewan Rakyat** — not a Senator, not someone outside Parliament.
- **In His Majesty's judgment** ("in his judgment") — this is the Agong's own assessment.
- **Likely to command the confidence of the majority** — not necessarily an absolute majority of a single party, but the confidence that the individual can gather the support of the majority of the House.

The most frequently misunderstood point: the appointment of the Prime Minister is one of the **discretionary functions** of the Yang di-Pertuan Agong. Article 40(1) generally requires the Agong to act on the advice of the Cabinet. But Article 40(2) lists exceptions where His Majesty "may act in his discretion", and the first listed is "the appointment of a Prime Minister" (Article 40(2)(a)). So for this matter, the Agong is not bound by the advice of the existing government.

## What are the constitutional requirements to become Prime Minister?

The Constitution sets clear and firm requirements. In summary:

| Requirement | Provision | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Must be a member of the Dewan Rakyat | Article 43(2)(a) | Not the Dewan Negara; other Ministers may come from either House |
| Likely to command the confidence of the majority | Article 43(2)(a) | Assessed by the Agong in His judgment |
| Not a citizen by naturalization/registration | Article 43(7) | A specific bar for the office of Prime Minister only |
| Take the oath of office, allegiance and secrecy | Article 43(6) | In accordance with the Sixth Schedule, before the Agong |

Article 43(7) is often overlooked but important: it provides that "a person who is a citizen by naturalization or by registration under Article 17 shall not be appointed Prime Minister." This bar is specific to the office of Prime Minister and does not apply to the ordinary office of Minister.

Note also what is **not** required: the Constitution does not set a specific minimum age (beyond the qualification to be a member of Parliament), does not require a particular party, and — as it stands — does not limit the term of office.

## What does "confidence of the majority of the Dewan Rakyat" mean?

"Confidence of the majority" (confidence of the majority) is the lifeblood of this office. It means that more than half of the 222 members of the Dewan Rakyat support the individual as head of government — that is, at least 112 seats.

Confidence can be demonstrated in several ways:

- **Election results** — when a party or coalition clearly wins a majority of seats.
- **Statutory declarations (SD)** from members of Parliament supporting a particular candidate.
- **Votes in the Dewan Rakyat** — whether a confidence motion, a no-confidence motion, or a vote on the Budget (which by convention is regarded as a test of confidence).

When no single party obtains a majority — known as a **hung Parliament** — the Agong's assessment becomes critical. This is what happened after the 15th General Election (GE15) in 2022, the first hung Parliament in Malaysia's history: no coalition reached the 112-seat threshold. Pakatan Harapan won the most seats but needed coalition partners to form a government. After a process of assessing support, the Yang di-Pertuan Agong Al-Sultan Abdullah Ri'ayatuddin Al-Mustafa Billah Shah consented to the appointment of Anwar Ibrahim as the 10th Prime Minister on 24 November 2022, under Articles 40(2)(a) and 43(2)(a).

## What is the Prime Minister's relationship with the Yang di-Pertuan Agong?

Malaysia is a constitutional monarchy. The Agong is the head of state; the Prime Minister is the head of government. The "real" executive power is largely exercised by the Prime Minister and the Cabinet, even though formally it is vested in the Agong.

Article 39 explains this structure: the executive authority of the Federation "shall be vested in the Yang di-Pertuan Agong and exercisable… by him or by the Cabinet or any Minister authorized by the Cabinet." That power is **vested** in the Agong, but **exercised** by the Cabinet.

Article 40(1) locks in this relationship: in exercising his functions, the Agong shall act on the advice of the Cabinet (which is led by the Prime Minister). There are only a few discretionary exceptions under Article 40(2):

| Discretionary function of the Agong | Provision |
| --- | --- |
| Appointment of the Prime Minister | Article 40(2)(a) |
| Withholding consent to a request to dissolve Parliament | Article 40(2)(b) |
| Convening a meeting of the Conference of Rulers concerning the privileges of the Rulers | Article 40(2)(c) |

Beyond this list, the relationship is one of Westminster convention: the Agong "reigns but does not rule", and the Prime Minister bears political responsibility for the nation's policy and administration.

## What happens when the Prime Minister loses the majority?

This is the question that determines the stability of Malaysian politics. The answer is in Article 43(4):

> "If the Prime Minister ceases to command the confidence of the majority of the members of the House of Representatives, then, unless at his request the Yang di-Pertuan Agong dissolves Parliament, the Prime Minister shall tender the resignation of the Cabinet."

This means that when the Prime Minister no longer commands the confidence of the majority, there are only two paths:

1. **The Prime Minister requests that the Agong dissolve Parliament** — opening the way to a new general election. But the Agong may **withhold consent** (Article 40(2)(b)); dissolution is not an automatic right of the Prime Minister.
2. **If dissolution is not granted**, the Prime Minister shall tender the resignation of the entire Cabinet.

Note the subtle point here: the Constitution does **not** give the Agong the direct power to **dismiss** a Prime Minister who still commands the confidence of the majority. The mechanism is centred on the confidence of the Dewan Rakyat — not the will of the palace. Constitutional scholars such as Emeritus Prof. Datuk Dr Shad Saleem Faruqi emphasise that the resignation arises from the loss of the confidence of the House, not from dismissal by the monarch.

## What are the powers and responsibilities of the Prime Minister?

Besides being head of government, the Prime Minister holds several concrete constitutional powers:

- **Advising on the appointment of Ministers** — under Article 43(2)(b), the Agong appoints other Ministers "on the advice of the Prime Minister", from either House of Parliament.
- **Advising on the appointment of Deputy Ministers** — under Article 43A(1), the Agong may, on the advice of the Prime Minister, appoint Deputy Ministers.
- **Appointing Parliamentary Secretaries** — under Article 43B(1).
- **Advising on the revocation of a Minister's appointment** — under Article 43(5), the appointment of a Minister may be revoked by the Agong on the advice of the Prime Minister.
- **Requesting the dissolution of Parliament** — under Articles 43(4) and 40(2)(b).
- **Chairing the Cabinet**, which is collectively responsible to Parliament (Article 43(3)).

The Prime Minister also heads the Prime Minister's Department, and the Prime Minister's Office (PMO) plays the role of creating an environment that enables the Prime Minister to carry out his duties. In practice, the Prime Minister often holds other portfolios concurrently — for example, the current Prime Minister also holds the office of Minister of Finance.

The principle of **collective responsibility** (Article 43(3)) means that the entire Cabinet shares responsibility for government decisions before Parliament — not the Prime Minister alone.

## The Prime Minister compared with other offices: what are the differences?

The office of Prime Minister is often confused with other offices in the system. A brief comparison:

| Office | Role | Constitutional basis | Appointed by |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Yang di-Pertuan Agong | Head of state | Article 32 | Conference of Rulers (elected) |
| Prime Minister | Head of government | Article 43(2)(a) | Yang di-Pertuan Agong |
| Deputy Prime Minister | Assists & acts for the PM | Convention; appointed as a Minister | Agong on the advice of the PM |
| Cabinet Minister | Heads a ministry | Article 43(2)(b) | Agong on the advice of the PM |
| Menteri Besar / Chief Minister | Head of a state government | State constitution | Sultan / Yang di-Pertua Negeri |

The most fundamental difference: **the Agong is the permanent head of state above politics; the Prime Minister is the head of government dependent on the politics of the House.** The Deputy Prime Minister, meanwhile, is not an office that must exist under the Constitution — it is a ministerial office given a title, and the number of such offices can differ from government to government.

## How is a government formed after an election?

The step-by-step framework after the polls:

1. **Votes are counted** — the 222 seats of the Dewan Rakyat are decided. The majority threshold is 112.
2. **Check the results:**
   - *If a coalition wins ≥112 seats* → the leader of that coalition is usually clearly qualified, and the Agong appoints him as Prime Minister.
   - *If no coalition reaches 112 (hung Parliament)* → step 3.
3. **Confidence assessment** — the Agong assesses who is "likely to command the confidence", often through letters of support (SD), interviews with party leaders, or the advice of the Conference of Rulers.
4. **Appointment** — the Agong appoints the Prime Minister under Articles 40(2)(a) and 43(2)(a); the Prime Minister takes the oath (Article 43(6)).
5. **Formation of the Cabinet** — the Prime Minister advises the Agong to appoint Ministers (Article 43(2)(b)).
6. **Confirmation** — confidence may be tested in the Dewan Rakyat (for example through a Budget vote or a confidence motion).

A concrete example: at GE15 (2022), step 2 produced a hung Parliament. The process moved to step 3, where the Agong called for the formation of a unity government, before appointing Anwar Ibrahim at step 4 on 24 November 2022.

## Is the term of the Prime Minister limited?

Under the text of the Constitution in force, there is **no term limit** for the office of Prime Minister — a person may serve as long as he commands the confidence of the majority and wins successive elections.

However, this is being reconsidered. In 2026, a **Federal Constitution (Amendment) Bill** was proposed to limit the Prime Minister's tenure to an **aggregate of ten years** — whether served continuously or not. The proposal introduces new subclauses to Article 43 (among them subclauses (2A) and (4A)–(4D)) providing that a Prime Minister appointed under Article 43(2)(a) shall not hold office for a period amounting in aggregate to ten years. According to the official verbatim text, the government framed it as strengthening the institution — the speech stated: "In the genealogy of democracy, the maturity of a nation has never been measured by how long a person holds the reins of power." The blueprint was distributed to Members of Parliament on 23 February 2026 and the second reading was on 2 March 2026.

Because a constitutional amendment requires a two-thirds majority of the Dewan Rakyat and the Dewan Negara, the final status of this proposal should be checked against official sources (the Government Gazette / AGC) before being treated as law in force.

## Common misconceptions about the office of Prime Minister

- **"The people vote for the Prime Minister directly."** No. The people vote for their Dewan Rakyat representatives; the Agong appoints the Prime Minister based on the confidence of the majority of the House.
- **"The Prime Minister must be the leader of the party that won the most seats."** Not necessarily. The requirement is the *confidence of the majority* — the second-largest party, if able to assemble a majority coalition, can produce a Prime Minister.
- **"The Agong can dismiss the Prime Minister at any time."** No. The Constitution does not grant a power to dismiss a Prime Minister who still commands the confidence of the majority; the mechanism is through the loss of the confidence of the Dewan Rakyat (Article 43(4)).
- **"The Prime Minister can dissolve Parliament at will."** No. Dissolution requires the consent of the Agong, which may be withheld under Article 40(2)(b).
- **"A Senator can be Prime Minister."** No. Article 43(2)(a) requires the Prime Minister to be a member of the Dewan Rakyat, not the Dewan Negara.
- **"Any citizen can be Prime Minister."** No. Article 43(7) bars a citizen by naturalization or by registration from holding the office.

## What next

To understand Malaysia's system of government fully, the office of Prime Minister is best read together with the three institutions that surround it:

- **Yang di-Pertuan Agong** — the head of state who appoints the Prime Minister and holds several important discretionary powers.
- **The Cabinet (Jemaah Menteri)** — the executive body led by the Prime Minister and collectively responsible to Parliament.
- **Dewan Rakyat** — the House whose confidence determines the life and death of a government.

If you follow current developments, two things are worth watching: whether the **2026 ten-year term-limit proposal** is ultimately passed as a constitutional amendment, and how the convention of "confidence of the majority" continues to be tested in an era of a more fragmented Parliament.
