Their name means "the original people" — the first people. Yet behind those two words lies a distinct legal category, a dedicated government department, and a set of communities far more diverse than a single term would suggest.

This article explains, descriptively, who the Orang Asli are, how they are defined in law, and the administrative framework that governs their affairs. It reports what is enshrined in the law and in official sources, without evaluating policy.

## Who is meant by "Orang Asli"

**Orang Asli** is the official term for the indigenous peoples of Peninsular Malaysia — the descendants of the Peninsula's original inhabitants.

A distinction in terminology is worth noting at the outset. "Orang Asli" is not the same phrase as "**Orang Asal**". The term "Orang Asal" is broader and usually also encompasses the natives (indigenous communities) of Sabah and Sarawak, whereas "Orang Asli" refers specifically to the indigenous peoples of the Peninsula. In federal law, the term used for this Peninsular group is "Orang Asli", as enshrined in the Aboriginal Peoples Act 1954.

## How the law defines Orang Asli

The legal status of the Orang Asli is not derived from the definition of "Malay" (Article 160) or "native" of Sabah and Sarawak (Article 161A) in the Constitution. Instead, it has its own instrument: the **Aboriginal Peoples Act 1954 (Act 134)**.

**Section 3** of the Act defines an Orang Asli, in brief, as a person who:

- has a father who is a member of an aboriginal ethnic group;
- speaks an aboriginal language; and
- habitually follows an aboriginal way of life, customs and beliefs.

The definition also encompasses descent through the male line, as well as certain specific circumstances — among them a child adopted in infancy by an Orang Asli and brought up as an Orang Asli [Section 3(1)(b)], and a child of a union between an Orang Asli woman and a man of another race, provided that the child habitually follows the Orang Asli way of life and becomes a member of an Orang Asli community [Section 3(1)(c)]. Section 3(2) also provides that an Orang Asli who adopts another religion does not necessarily cease to be an Orang Asli merely on that account, if he still follows the Orang Asli way of life. Section 3(3), in turn, provides that when a question arises as to whether a person is or is not an Orang Asli, that question **shall be decided by the Minister**.

Besides Act 134, the [Federal Constitution](/law/federal-constitution) also touches on their position. Article 8, which establishes equality before the law, permits special provision to be made for the protection, well-being or advancement of the Orang Asli — an express exception to the prohibition on discrimination.

## Three groups, eighteen sub-ethnic groups

The Orang Asli are not a uniform community. According to **JAKOA**, they divide into **three main groups**, and each of those groups contains **six sub-ethnic groups** — making **18 sub-ethnic groups** in total. The division into three racial groups (Negrito, Senoi and Proto-Malay) is also expressed in the interpretation under Section 2 of the Aboriginal Peoples Act 1954.

| Main group | Sub-ethnic groups (six per group) |
| --- | --- |
| **Negrito** (also called Semang) | Kensiu, Kintak, Lanoh, Jahai, Mendriq, Bateq |
| **Senoi** | Che Wong, Mahmeri, Jahut, Semoq Beri, Semai, Temiar |
| **Proto-Malay** (Melayu-Proto) | Orang Kuala, Orang Kanaq, Orang Seletar, Jakun, Semelai, Temuan |

In terms of racial group size, the DOSM Report (2020 Census) recorded the Senoi as the largest group at 53.5 percent, followed by Proto-Malay (44.4 percent) and Negrito (2.1 percent). At the sub-ethnic group level, the **Semai** (within the Senoi group) are usually cited as among the largest sub-ethnic groups while the **Orang Kanaq** (within the Proto-Malay group) as among the smallest in number — however, the detailed figures for each sub-ethnic group are not stated separately in the official sources referred to here, and sub-ethnic-group-level figures warrant further verification. Each group has its own history, language and geographical distribution — for example, the Negrito group is more concentrated in the northern areas of the Peninsula, while the Proto-Malay peoples are found largely in the southern part.

## How many, and where

The total population figures come from the **Department of Statistics Malaysia (DOSM)**. According to the *Special Report on the Demographics of Orang Asli* released on 14 October 2025 based on the 2020 Census:

- **213,461** people were recorded in the **2020 Census**;
- the figure for **2025** is estimated at **227,900** people; and
- the average growth rate for the 2020–2025 period is estimated at about **1.2 percent** per year.

In terms of distribution, the 2020 Census shows the largest concentrations in three states:

- **Pahang** — 82,824 people;
- **Perak** — 58,942 people; and
- **Selangor** — 21,312 people.

By contrast, the smallest numbers were recorded in **Perlis** (26 people) and **Penang** (128 people), while the Federal Territories also recorded small numbers (Kuala Lumpur 300, Labuan 15 and Putrajaya 11 people in 2020).

In terms of sex, the 2020 composition was 108,354 males (50.8 percent) and 105,107 females (49.2 percent), with a sex ratio of 103 males for every 100 females.

## The department responsible: JAKOA

Orang Asli affairs are administered by a dedicated department whose name has changed several times over its history:

- A department for Orang Asli affairs was **established in 1954**, alongside the enactment of related legislation in the same year;
- it was known as the **Department of Aboriginal Affairs (JHEOA)** from around 1963; and
- it was renamed the **Department of Orang Asli Development (JAKOA)**, with effect from 2011.

JAKOA now sits under the **Ministry of Rural and Regional Development**. According to the department itself, its main aim is to implement a comprehensive development approach encompassing socioeconomic development, education, health and human capital development in a planned and sustainable manner.

## Land and protection under the Act

Besides defining who the Orang Asli are, the Aboriginal Peoples Act 1954 also provides mechanisms relating to land and settlement. Among them are provisions for gazetting **aboriginal areas** (Section 6) and **aboriginal reserves** (Section 7), together with the related administrative powers.

The extent to which these provisions protect the customary land rights of the Orang Asli is an area that has been a focus of public discussion and has been brought before the courts in a number of cases. There are various views on it — some parties emphasise the protection that exists under the Act and court decisions, while others highlight its limits. Elaborating on and evaluating those views is beyond the scope of this glossary article; here we merely note that the land issue is an actively discussed aspect of Orang Asli affairs.

## Common misconceptions

**"Orang Asli and Orang Asal mean the same thing."** Not accurate. "Orang Asli" refers specifically to the indigenous peoples of the Peninsula, while "Orang Asal" is a broader term that also encompasses the indigenous peoples of Sabah and Sarawak.

**"Orang Asli are Malays."** The Orang Asli have their own legal definition under the Aboriginal Peoples Act 1954, distinct from the definition of "Malay" in Article 160 of the Constitution. They are usually counted within the [Bumiputera](/glossary/bumiputera) category for administrative purposes, but through a different legal basis.

**"Orang Asli are a single, uniform tribe."** In fact there are three main groups and 18 sub-ethnic groups, with differing languages, customs and distributions.
