**Bahasa Malaysia:** Kenduri · **English:** Communal feast · **中文:** 宴席（马来社群聚餐）

In the village, a kenduri does not begin on the day of the event. It begins a
few days earlier — when the marquee starts going up in the yard, the big pots
come out, and neighbours arrive one by one to chop onions and stir the curry.
This is what sets a kenduri apart from an ordinary banquet: it is not only the
food that is shared, but the work of preparing it.

## What a kenduri is

According to *Kamus Dewan*, a kenduri is a feast usually preceded by prayers or
zikir (devotional recitation), held to mark an occasion — seeking blessings,
praying for those who have died, or celebrating an event such as a wedding. The
word is used widely among Malay communities in Malaysia, and also in Singapore,
Indonesia and southern Thailand.

What makes a kenduri more than just a banquet is its communal role. Preparing
the food — from cooking in large woks to putting up the marquee and setting out
the tables — is usually done through gotong-royong (mutual help) by family,
neighbours and residents of the [kampung](/en/glossary/kampung). In many cases,
taking part in such work is regarded as part of [adat](/en/glossary/adat) and of
one's social responsibility to fellow members of the community.

## Types of kenduri

A kenduri is named according to its purpose and the occasion it marks. Among the
most common:

| Type of kenduri | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| **Kenduri kahwin** | To celebrate a wedding; usually the largest and liveliest event, often paired with the persandingan (the sitting-in-state of the couple) |
| **Kenduri doa selamat** | A sign of thanksgiving or to seek blessings — for example welcoming someone's return, moving into a new home, or beginning a venture |
| **Kenduri arwah** | To pray for and remember those who have died, sometimes held on certain days after a death |
| **Kenduri aqiqah / cukur jambul** | To celebrate the birth of a baby |
| **Kenduri kesyukuran** | A sign of thanks for a success, such as completing one's studies or finishing a reading of the Quran |

This list is not exhaustive — a kenduri may be held for almost any event a
family considers important. A kenduri kahwin in particular is often held openly:
in many villages, attending does not require a strict formal invitation, and
neighbours and passers-by are welcomed to eat. This open spirit is closely
linked to the practice of [open house](/en/glossary/open-house) during festive
seasons.

## The social role

Beyond the food, a kenduri works as a social bond. The work of preparing it
brings together neighbours who may rarely meet, and the event itself becomes a
space to strengthen ties of kinship and friendship across generations and
families. For a kenduri arwah, gathering together also offers moral support to a
grieving family. For this reason, a kenduri is often seen not as a mere banquet,
but as an effort and an experience shared by the whole community.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating a kenduri as the same as any banquet or party.** A kenduri marks a
  specific occasion and is usually preceded by prayers; it is not just a
  gathering to eat.
- **Assuming all kenduri are joyful events.** A kenduri arwah is held to
  remember the dead, so its mood is solemn and not festive like a kenduri
  kahwin.
- **Thinking a kenduri must be attended with a formal invitation.** Many
  kenduri, especially a kenduri kahwin in the village, are open — the presence
  of neighbours and the public without an invitation card is quite normal.
