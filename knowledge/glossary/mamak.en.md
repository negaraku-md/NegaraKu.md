**Bahasa Malaysia:** Mamak · **English:** Mamak · **中文:** 嘛嘛（印度裔穆斯林／嘛嘛档）

Call someone "mamak" in some settings and it's an insult. Call the eatery
"mamak" and it's just an ordinary name used millions of times a day across
Malaysia. The same word, three different meanings — and all three trace back
to the same root: a respectful term for an uncle.

## One word, three meanings

| Meaning | Context of use |
| --- | --- |
| **Term of respectful address** | From Tamil *māmā* — maternal uncle, or male head of household |
| **Community name** | Refers to Indian Muslims, mostly of Tamil descent, long settled in Malaysia and Singapore |
| **Type of eatery** | A street-style shop or restaurant serving Tamil Muslim cuisine, usually blended with local elements |

The second meaning is sometimes used disparagingly outside a business
context, so many people are more comfortable using the word specifically to
refer to the eatery, or in its original respectful sense.

## From roadside stall to social institution

Mamak shops began as small stalls run by Indian Muslim immigrants during the
British colonial era — selling tea and simple snacks before growing into
full-time restaurants serving roti canai, mee goreng and teh tarik. Many
operate 24 hours, a feature that first served shift workers before it grew
into a late-night stop for anyone at all.

It is this long operating hours and open layout that is often linked to the
mamak shop's role as a meeting space frequented by customers of different
races and religions, outside the setting of the workplace or the home.

In December 2024, the element *Breakfast culture in Malaysia: dining
experience in a multi-ethnic society* was inscribed on the UNESCO
Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. The
element's description also names roti canai, nasi lemak and teh tarik —
dishes that form the core menu of most mamak shops — as part of a breakfast
culture shared across races, not belonging to a single community alone.

## Common mistakes

- **Assuming "mamak" is the same as "kopitiam".** Kopitiam is rooted in the
  Chinese coffee-shop tradition, while mamak is rooted in the Indian Muslim
  tradition — even though both now serve overlapping menus.
- **Using "mamak" to address someone directly.** Outside a business context,
  many consider it impolite; it is safest used to refer to the shop or
  restaurant.
- **Assuming all mamak shops operate 24 hours.** Many do operate 24 hours,
  but not all — operating hours vary by location and owner.
