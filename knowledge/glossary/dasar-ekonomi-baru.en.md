After the 13 May 1969 events, the Malaysian government made an assessment that reshaped the direction of the country's economic policy for the following two decades: that economic growth alone was not enough if wealth and opportunity remained unevenly concentrated along racial lines. From that assessment came the **New Economic Policy (NEP)** — in Malay, *Dasar Ekonomi Baru* (DEB).

This article describes the NEP descriptively as a policy: what its goals were, how it was drafted, and the figures it contained. It does not evaluate whether the policy succeeded or not; views on that vary, and the closing section of the article reports the range of those views without taking sides.

## What is the NEP, and when did it begin?

The NEP is a socioeconomic policy framework **launched in 1971** as part of the [Second Malaysia Plan](/economy/rancangan-malaysia) (1971-1975). According to Economic History Malaysia, the final draft of the policy document was **tabled in Parliament on 12 July 1971**.

Its background matters. In the wake of the 13 May 1969 events, the country was administered under emergency rule by the **National Operations Council (MAGERAN / Majlis Gerakan Negara)** led by **Tun Abdul Razak Hussein**. During that period, two streams of policy thinking competed — one from the Economic Planning Unit (EPU) that placed greater emphasis on growth, and another from the Department of National Unity that pushed for broader state intervention to reduce inequality between the races. The combined result of those deliberations became the NEP.

In terms of duration, the NEP was designed as a **20-year (1971-1990)** project under the **First Outline Perspective Plan** (*Rangka Rancangan Jangka Panjang Pertama*, OPP1). It was carried out through several successive five-year Malaysia Plans over that period.

## What are the two "two-pronged" goals?

The **ultimate** goal of the NEP was not a purely economic target, but **national unity**. To achieve it, the NEP set out two strategies carried out simultaneously — often referred to as the **two-pronged** approach:

- **Eradicating poverty** among all citizens, **regardless of race**, by raising incomes and increasing employment opportunities.
- **Restructuring society** to reduce and ultimately **eliminate the identification of race with economic function** — that is, the situation where a given race is narrowly associated with a particular employment sector or location.

The early policy document expressed the aspiration for Bumiputera to become **"full partners in the economic life of the nation" within one generation**. The document also contained a guarantee that was later much debated — that in this restructuring process, no group would suffer loss or feel deprived.

## Where did the 30 percent Bumiputera equity target come from?

Of all the figures associated with the NEP, the most frequently cited is the **30 percent Bumiputera corporate equity target**. This target became more prominent in the **Third Malaysia Plan (1976-1980)**, when the NEP's long-term focus was narrowed from "full participation" to a specific equity ownership figure.

The target sat within an equity ownership framework targeted for 1990:

| Equity holder | Target by 1990 |
| --- | --- |
| Bumiputera | 30% |
| Non-Bumiputera (other Malaysians) | 40% |
| Foreign interests | 30% |

In the original framework, the 30% Bumiputera share was also broken down into about **7.4% individual ownership** and **22.6% held by trust agencies** on behalf of Bumiputera — a detail that was later often forgotten in general discussion.

The low starting point explains why this restructuring became the core of the policy: in **1970**, Bumiputera-related corporate equity ownership was estimated at only **about 2.4 percent**.

## What happened by 1990?

Equity ownership data show significant movement over the NEP period, even though the 30% target was not fully achieved by the planned date. According to Economic History Malaysia's analysis:

| Year | Bumiputera-related corporate equity ownership |
| --- | --- |
| 1970 | about 2.4% |
| 1980 | about 12.5% |
| 1990 | more than 19% |

The same source explains that much of this increase involved equity **held by public institutions and trust agencies** on behalf of Bumiputera, and not solely individual ownership. It was this structure that also gave rise to many of the [government-linked companies (GLCs)](/glossary/glc) and investment agencies that still exist today.

On poverty, the NEP unfolded during a period of widespread reduction in poverty rates. As a point of reference, Economic History Malaysia notes that **about a decade after independence in 1957 — that is, towards the end of the 1960s — about 60 percent of the population still lived in poverty**, and that share declined significantly over the decades of the NEP.

The NEP's 20-year period ended in **1990**. It was succeeded by the **National Development Policy (NDP)** in 1991, which continued part of the NEP's goals within a new frame.

## Why is the NEP still a subject of debate?

As a race-based affirmative-action policy, the NEP continues to be a subject of differing views. Descriptively, the range of those views can be reported as follows:

- **The supportive perspective** emphasises that the NEP unfolded during a period of major poverty reduction, the formation of a Bumiputera middle class, and the expansion of access to education and business opportunities — and argues that the goal of reducing the identification of race with economic function required state intervention.
- **The critical perspective** raises questions about effectiveness, cost and the side effects of implementation — including the impact on non-Bumiputera citizens, inequality among Bumiputera themselves, and the extent to which benefits reached those most in need rather than particular groups.

Both perspectives refer to a largely shared set of data, but place different emphases on it. This glossary article does not adjudicate between these views; it merely reports that the debate exists and continues in Malaysian policy discourse.

## Common misconceptions

**"The NEP had an official expiry date in 1990."** The 20-year planning period did end in 1990, but no single legal provision declared the NEP "terminated" on that date; it was succeeded by later policies, with several elements continued.

**"The 30% target means 30% individual Bumiputera ownership."** In the original framework, the 30% was broken down into individual ownership and a far larger share held by trust agencies on behalf of Bumiputera.

**"The NEP was only about Bumiputera equity."** The equity target was only one component. The NEP's ultimate goal was national unity, and one of its two strategies — poverty eradication — was designed to apply to all citizens regardless of race.

**"The NEP and Bumiputera mean the same thing."** The NEP is a policy; [Bumiputera](/glossary/bumiputera) is a term for the group that was the focus of part of its strategy. The two are closely related but not synonymous.
