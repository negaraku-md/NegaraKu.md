Sarawak is Malaysia in a different key. It is by far the largest state in the
federation by land area, sits on Borneo rather than the peninsula, and holds a
distinct constitutional standing that sets it apart from every peninsular state.

## Key facts

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Capital | Kuching |
| Population | Around 2.9 million |
| Head of state | Yang di-Pertua Negeri (Governor); state government led by a Premier |
| Key industries | Oil & gas (LNG), timber, palm oil, hydropower, tourism |

## Geography

Sarawak occupies the northwest of Borneo island, bordering Indonesian Kalimantan to
the south and the Sultanate of Brunei and Malaysia's other Bornean state, Sabah, to
the northeast. It covers roughly 124,000 square kilometres — close to a third of
Malaysia's total land area — but is separated from Peninsular Malaysia by around 600
kilometres of the South China Sea. Much of the state remains covered by tropical
rainforest and is drained by major rivers including the Rajang, Malaysia's longest,
which historically served as the main transport artery into the interior.

## Economy

Oil and gas dominate Sarawak's formal economy. Bintulu hosts one of the world's
largest integrated liquefied natural gas complexes, operated by Petronas, and
petroleum-related activity accounts for a large share of state revenue and exports.
Timber has historically been a major industry, alongside palm oil cultivation, which
has expanded significantly across the state in recent decades. Sarawak also has
substantial hydroelectric capacity, led by the Bakun Dam, one of the largest in
Southeast Asia, feeding into the state's push to develop energy-intensive industries
under the Sarawak Corridor of Renewable Energy initiative. Tourism, built around the
state's rainforests, national parks and indigenous cultures, is a smaller but
growing contributor. Sarawak has also pursued greater fiscal autonomy from the
federal government, including negotiations over oil royalty rates and state
revenue rights tied to its position under the Malaysia Agreement 1963.

## Doing business here

Sarawak's state government operates with more autonomy than peninsular states,
a legacy of the terms under which it joined the Malaysian federation in 1963.
Notably, Malaysians travelling from the Peninsula or Sabah must clear a separate
immigration check to enter Sarawak, and the state maintains its own land law and
resource administration in several respects, alongside federal frameworks. For
energy and resource-sector investors, engagement typically involves both Petronas
and Sarawak's own state-owned entities, such as Petros, which has taken an
increasing role in the state's gas sector. Abundant and low-cost hydropower makes
Sarawak attractive for energy-intensive manufacturing, and the state government
actively promotes digital economy and downstream resource-processing investment
alongside its traditional oil, gas and timber base. Businesses should budget for
longer logistics lead times and higher transport costs than the more connected
peninsular states, given Sarawak's geographic separation and lower population
density outside Kuching and a handful of other towns.

## Notable places

Kuching's waterfront along the Sarawak River anchors the state capital, combining
colonial-era buildings with modern civic architecture including the Sarawak State
Legislative Assembly. Bako National Park and Gunung Mulu National Park, the latter a
UNESCO World Heritage Site known for its vast cave systems, showcase Sarawak's
rainforest ecology. The Semenggoh Wildlife Centre near Kuching is known for its
semi-wild orangutan population. Bintulu is the centre of the state's LNG industry,
while the annual Rainforest World Music Festival, held near Kuching, has become one
of Malaysia's best-known cultural events, drawing performers and visitors from
around the world.
