  - q: "If I move to another state, do I need a new MyKad, driving licence or car registration?"
    a: "No — those are federal and valid nationwide, so you do not re-register them. Your IC number is permanent, your driving licence works in every state, and your vehicle's registration and plate stay the same (the state-letter prefix is historical and need not change). What you do have to do is update your address, and this is where people get caught: the National Registration Department (JPN) and the Road Transport Department (JPJ) are separate systems that do not sync. Updating your MyKad address at JPN does not update your licence and vehicle records at JPJ — you must do each one. Both have their own rules and time windows for reporting a change of address, so confirm the current requirement, fee and deadline with JPN and JPJ directly rather than assuming one update covers everything."
  - q: "What do I actually have to set up new when I move states?"
    a: "A short list of genuinely local accounts and calendars. Open a new water-supply account with the destination state's operator (water is organised state by state). Register for local-council assessment (cukai taksiran / cukai pintu) with the new council if you own or occupy the property, and check its by-laws for things like refuse collection and parking. If you own property, your quit rent (cukai tanah) is collected by the state land office of the new state. Update your leave calendar for the new state's public holidays, which differ. Electricity is a new account at the new premises but the same company across the Peninsula (TNB); only a move to Sabah (SESB) or Sarawak (Sarawak Energy) means a different electricity utility. Rates, deadlines and the exact operator vary, so confirm each with the relevant authority."
  - q: "Is moving to Sabah or Sarawak different from moving within the Peninsula?"
    a: "Yes, materially. Under the Malaysia Agreement 1963, Sabah and Sarawak retain control of their own immigration, and this is current and enforced. A Malaysian from the Peninsula uses their MyKad as identification (no passport is needed), but still passes a state immigration control on entry and is admitted as a visitor for a limited period. To work, study or reside there long-term, you generally need a pass or permit from the state immigration authority. Sabah and Sarawak also have their own separate land codes rather than the Peninsular National Land Code, and both their electricity and water are run by state bodies. Because these rules have changed over time and are the most variable part of this topic, confirm the current entry, visit-duration and work-pass rules with the Sabah or Sarawak Immigration Department before relying on anything here."
  - q: "I'm a Muslim — does moving states change anything about marriage or religious matters?"
    a: "The structure is the thing to understand: in Malaysia, Islamic personal and family law is administered state by state. Each state has its own Islamic religious authority, its own Syariah courts and its own enactments covering matters such as marriage, divorce and related administration. So after moving you would deal with the destination state's religious authority and Syariah court for those matters, and procedures and requirements can differ between states. This is an administrative map, not advice on any particular case — confirm the specific process with the religious authority or Syariah court of your new state."
  - q: "Do my income tax, EPF, SOCSO and my children's schooling change?"
    a: "Income tax, EPF and SOCSO do not change — they are federal, there is no state income tax, and your files follow you nationwide; just keep your address current in each portal. What changes for schooling is placement: school seats are allocated by catchment area, so moving means applying for a school transfer (pertukaran sekolah) for your children through the Ministry of Education's process, even though what they study does not change. Confirm the current transfer procedure with the receiving school or the state education department."

Moving house within Malaysia feels as though it should trigger a mountain of re-registration — new documents, new numbers, a fresh start with every agency. For the most part it does not. The reason is structural: **most of the things that feel official about your life in Malaysia are federal**, and federal systems work the same in every state, so they simply follow you. What actually changes is a smaller, more local layer — and knowing which is which turns a daunting move into a short checklist.

This guide sorts your relocation into three buckets: **what does not change (just tell it your new address), what you genuinely set up new, and the one move — to Sabah or Sarawak — that is different in kind.**

## The frame: federal follows you, state and local do not

Malaysia divides law-making power between the federation and the states. The **Federal Constitution (Article 74 and the Ninth Schedule)** sets out what belongs to each. The practical upshot for someone moving house:

- **Federal matters are uniform nationwide** — national registration and identity, road transport and the licensing of drivers and vehicles, income tax, labour and social security (EPF, SOCSO), the courts, and national education policy and curriculum. These **do not change** when you cross a state line.
- **State and local matters vary** — land (and the quit rent on it), local government (and its assessment rates), the administration of Islamic law, and water supply. These are where the **real changes** are.

Hold that split in mind and the rest is detail.

## What does NOT change (just update your address)

Your identity and entitlements travel with you. The only chore is telling several federal systems your new address — and the trap is that **they are separate chores that do not talk to each other.**

- **MyKad / IC (JPN).** Your IC number is permanent and valid everywhere. You do, however, have a duty to update the address on your card after a move — confirm the current rule, time window and fee with the National Registration Department.
- **Driving licence and vehicle (JPJ).** Your licence and your car's registration are valid nationwide; you do not re-register them, and the state-letter plate prefix need not change. But you **must separately update your address with JPJ** within its own deadline. Crucially, **updating your MyKad address at JPN does not update your records at JPJ** — they are different systems. Do both.
- **Income tax (LHDN), EPF and SOCSO.** All federal; there is no state income tax. Your files follow you — just keep your address current in each portal.
- **Passport, and the civil and criminal courts.** Unchanged. (Syariah courts are separate and state-run — see below.)
- **School curriculum.** The syllabus and national exams are the same in every state. What changes is the **seat**: school places are allocated by catchment, so moving means applying for a **school transfer (pertukaran sekolah)** through the Ministry of Education, even though what your child studies does not change.

> The one-line rule: *federal systems follow you; the only task is updating your address in each — and JPN, JPJ, LHDN and EPF are each a separate update.*

## What you genuinely set up new (state and local)

This is the short list of real changes:

- **Water.** You open a **new account with a different operator**, because water supply is organised state by state. In Peninsular Malaysia and the Federal Territories the operators are regulated by **SPAN**; Sabah and Sarawak run their own water bodies outside SPAN. Set up the new account and close the old one.
- **Local-council assessment (cukai taksiran / cukai pintu).** A different council bills you, at its own rate and on its own basis, under the Local Government Act 1976. Register with the new council if you own or occupy the property, and check its by-laws (refuse, parking, pets, business premises).
- **Quit rent (cukai tanah) — owners only.** This is a **state** charge collected by the **state land office** of the state your property sits in. Different state, different land office, different rate schedule and deadline. (For some strata properties the equivalent is parcel rent, *cukai petak*.) Renters do not pay this directly.
- **Electricity — only different in Borneo.** Across the whole of **Peninsular Malaysia the supplier is TNB**, so a Peninsular move is just a new premises account with the same company. Move to **Sabah (SESB)** or **Sarawak (Sarawak Energy)** and it is a genuinely different utility.
- **State public holidays.** Your days off change. State holidays mark the state ruler's birthday and local festivals, and they differ — some observed in one state and not the next. Update your leave calendar against your new state's gazetted list each year.
- **For Muslims — the state religious authority.** In Malaysia, **Islamic personal and family law is administered state by state**: each state has its own religious authority, its own Syariah courts and its own enactments on matters such as marriage and divorce. After moving, those matters are handled by the destination state's authority, and procedures can differ. This is a map of where to go, not guidance on any case — confirm the specific process with your new state's religious authority or Syariah court.
- **Some licensing and land rules.** Certain retail, signage and premises by-laws, and land-dealing and Malay reserved land rules, are set at state or local level and differ — relevant mainly to business owners and property buyers.

> The one-line rule: *a handful of new accounts and new calendars — water, assessment, the state land office if you own, your new holidays, and, for Muslims, the state religious authority.*

## The one that's different: moving to Sabah or Sarawak

A move **to Sabah or Sarawak** — or between them — is not just an inter-state move with extra paperwork. Under the **[Malaysia Agreement 1963](/malaysia/ma63) (MA63)**, both states keep powers the Peninsular states do not, and the one you will feel immediately is **immigration**.

- **They control their own immigration, and it is enforced.** A Malaysian from the Peninsula uses their **MyKad as ID** (no passport needed) but still **clears a state immigration control on entry** and is admitted as a visitor for a limited period.
- **Living or working there needs more than an address change.** To **work, study or reside long-term**, you generally need a **pass or permit** from the state immigration authority — arrange this *before* assuming you can simply move in.
- **Separate land and utilities.** Both states use their **own land codes** (not the Peninsular National Land Code), and both their electricity and water are run by state bodies.

Because these rules have shifted over the years and are the most variable part of this whole topic, **confirm the current entry, visit-duration and work-pass rules directly with the Sabah or Sarawak Immigration Department.** Treat a Borneo move as "a move with an immigration step."

## Your move checklist

**Update an address (federal — keep the account), each one separately:**
1. JPN / MyKad · 2. JPJ (licence + vehicle) · 3. LHDN, EPF, SOCSO · 4. Banks, insurer, employer HR.

**Open or transfer a local account (new provider):**
5. Water (new state operator) · 6. Electricity (new premises; a different company only to Sabah/Sarawak) · 7. Local council — assessment and by-laws · 8. State land office — quit rent, if you own.

**One-offs and calendar:**
9. School transfer for children · 10. Note the new state's public holidays · 11. For Muslims, the destination state's religious authority for family-law matters · 12. Business owners: re-check local licensing and premises by-laws · 13. **Sabah/Sarawak only:** sort state immigration (entry record, and a pass if working or staying).

One honest caveat throughout: this is general orientation, **not legal or tax advice**. Rates, fees, deadlines and the exact state operators change, and the owner-only items (quit rent, assessment, land rules) do not apply to renters. When an item matters to your situation, confirm it with the authority named beside it.
